# Chapter Eight

## The Alarm

> *Water (20 gal). Batteries. Cash. Propane. Matches. Bleach. Meds. Dog food. Gas can.*
>
> — Post-it note, kitchen counter, Monday, 5:40 a.m.

They left for the range early on Sunday, so Sanda could try the Bodyguard before anyone else was there.

John packed the AR-15 into its hard case and threw some ammunition and three handguns into the big rolling range bag that the NRA had mailed him as a thank-you gift, and he punched the code into the alarm panel by the door. Charlie hated the alarm's countdown. He stood in the hallway and barked at the panel in time with the beeps, as though he might be able to argue it down, until John pulled the garage door shut behind them.

It was a twenty-minute drive. That was plenty of time for Sanda to think.

"I'm not so sure plywood is a good idea," Sanda said, five minutes in.

*Here we go*, John thought. He had not bought the plywood.

"We need something that lets the light in," Sanda said, looking out her window at the Sunday-morning traffic on 46. "But from outside it should just look like blinds. Like nothing. Maybe Plexiglas. We could paint it white on the outside, so from the street it looks like blinds."

John tried to picture it and could not quite. "Why do we need light?"

"Because if the power goes out, John, you need some way to see in the daytime. You can't sit in the dark with plywood on every window for a month like a vampire."

"We have flashlights. We have lanterns."

"Those are consumables," Sanda said.

He was quiet for a moment, because she was right and because he did not like it. "Fine. Okay. Plexiglas. I agree we have to think more about the windows. But there's a bigger problem." He changed lanes around a pickup towing a boat. "Say all hell breaks loose. We're all hunkered down in the apartment. Somebody has to be awake, guarding the place, at all times. Day and night. That means shifts, so people can sleep. And right now, the only person in the family who can really shoot an AR is me."

"I shot the AR last weekend," Sanda said, a little hurt. "Forty rounds. You said I was a monster."

"I handed you a loaded rifle and you shot it. Which was great. But there's a lot more to it. You don't know how to load your own magazines. You don't know how to turn the optic on without me telling you which button. You don't know what to do when it jams, and they all jam eventually. You don't—"

"These are all trivial things," Sanda said, louder. "I can learn them anytime."

"And your mom can shoot a .22. My dad hates guns. My mom—"

"Then teach me," Sanda said.

John looked over at her. She was looking straight ahead through the windshield with her jaw set, the way she looked at a regional vice president who had told her something could not be done.

"The magazines. The jams. The optic. The thing where you take it apart. All of it." She folded her arms. "Teach me. Today."

"Okay," John said, and he found that he was smiling at the road. "Okay. Today."

* * *

There was only one other car in the gravel lot, a dusty black Toyota Tacoma with a light bar on the roof, and John's heart sank, because he had wanted the range to themselves. They signed in at the shed, slid two dollars into the honor box, pushed in their earplugs, and walked through the gate under the big red sign that said WARNING — MEMBERS ONLY BEYOND THIS POINT — LIVE AMMUNITION.

The other shooter was at the far end of the fifty-yard range, his back to them, firing a pistol in steady pairs. He wore a gray hoodie with the sleeves pushed up past his elbows, gray tactical pants, and a sand-colored cap with a pair of Oakleys on the brim. When he turned to change targets, John saw the compass rose on his left forearm.

"Hey, John!" Trevor called, waving and walking toward them. "How are you, man?"

"Trevor?" John lowered his range bag. "Hey. Good to see you."

"Let me guess." Trevor grinned, and the pale gray eyes went from John to the small case in Sanda's hand. "You're out here to break in that Bodyguard."

"That, and I'm going to heat up my AR with a couple hundred rounds."

"Sanda, this is Trevor," John said. "From the store. Trevor, my wife, Sanda."

"Hi, Sanda." Trevor held out his hand. He had to bend a little, and he did it without making a point of it. "So you're the one who wanted the thumb safety. Smart. Most people don't think that far."

"Nice to meet you," Sanda said, shaking his hand quickly, the way she shook hands with customers she had not decided about.

"Your thing came in Friday, by the way," Trevor said to John. "You want to pick it up tomorrow? I'm in all day."

John could feel Sanda turning to look at him. "Tomorrow," he said. "Sure. Great."

"What thing?" Sanda asked.

"A scope," John said.

Trevor's eyes did something small and amused and then stopped doing it. "Yeah," he said. "A scope. Well, I'll let y'all get to it. I've got a couple more mags to shoot and then I'm out of here. Dish went up Thursday, by the way," he added to John, over his shoulder. "Internet from space. You should see the speeds."

He shot his last two magazines while they set up, and then he called for the range to go cold and walked down past the firing line to pull his target, and on his way back he stopped behind Sanda while she loaded the Bodyguard's tiny magazine and watched her for a moment.

"Thumb on the side of the mag," Trevor said. "Push down on the round in there, then slide the new one in under the lip. Easier on your fingers."

Sanda tried it. It was easier on her fingers.

"Thank you," Sanda said, surprised.

"Anytime," Trevor said, and touched the brim of his cap to her, and picked up his bag and went off through the gate to the black Tacoma, and a minute later they heard it pull out of the gravel lot.

"He's nice," Sanda said, sliding the magazine into the little gun.

"He's very ready," John said, with something in his voice he did not care to examine.

The Bodyguard kicked. It was a tiny gun firing a real cartridge, and it jumped in Sanda's hands like something alive, and the first round went four inches high. "Wow," Sanda said, turning to look at him. "This thing kicks."

"You can handle it."

She handled it. The second round went high too. John thought she might be flinching a little, anticipating the snap, and he said nothing, because she would work it out, and she did; by the third magazine the holes were creeping down toward the center. While she shot, John ran the AR as fast as he could keep the red dot on the paper, and the brass rang off the concrete and rolled away under the benches, and the barrel steamed in the cool air.

Then he spent an hour teaching her. How to load a thirty-round magazine with her thumb. How to turn the optic on and up. How to tap the bottom of the magazine and rack the charging handle when a round failed to feed. How to push out the two pins and separate the upper from the lower, and draw out the bolt carrier, and lay it all on a towel, and put it back together. She did it twice wrong and three times right.

"I think I really like this gun," Sanda said at last, looking down at the Bodyguard in her palm, nodding slowly, almost convinced. "Once you get used to the kick. I love how small it is."

"I'm glad," John said, and meant it, and did not think about the scope.

* * *

At seven that evening Sanda's phone rang. It was her mother.

John knew right away that something had happened, because Sanda's face changed, and then she said, "Mommy, stop. Mommy! Listen to me!" and went into a fast, rising stream of Romanian with an English word every other sentence — *stun gun*, *kitchen*, *what were you thinking* — and walked in a circle around the living room with her free hand on top of her head. Then she hung up and stood there.

"You won't fucking believe this," Sanda said.

"What happened?" John said, sitting up in the recliner. He could already tell from her face that nobody was hurt; it was the face she made when her mother had done something her mother probably should not have.

"That stun gun you gave her," Sanda said. "She just tried to kill a palmetto bug with it."

John stared at her. Then he started to grin, and he could not stop it. "How'd that work out?"

"I don't know what I'm going to do with her!" Sanda said, starting to smile herself.

"Did she get it?"

"Yeah," Sanda said, and now she was laughing. "With a shoe!"

John pictured it: his four-foot-nine mother-in-law in her flowered housecoat, chasing a two-inch flying cockroach around her condo kitchen with twenty thousand volts crackling blue in her fist, the bug zigzagging away from her across the linoleum, and then Dorina losing patience and reaching down and taking off a shoe.

"It's not a palmetto bug," John said darkly, when he could talk. "It's a cockroach. A big, fat, flying cockroach. They just call it a palmetto bug down here so the tourists don't leave."

"You're scared of them," Sanda said, delighted.

"I'm not scared of them."

"You're terrified of them. A grown man. Six foot one. With a gun in his pocket right now." She dropped onto the couch, still laughing. "Tell the story."

"I'm not telling the story."

"Tell the story, John."

He told the story. He had been six, in the backyard of the house in Jacksonville, standing on the little patio outside the back door in a pair of jeans, when something big and fast had run up the inside of his pant leg. He had felt every one of its legs. Without thinking, he had slapped his left hand down on his thigh and grabbed it through the denim, and he could feel it there in his fist, hard-shelled and fat and *moving*, two inches from his crotch. He had screamed. His father had come out the back door in his undershirt.

"Take your pants off, boy," Jim had said, very calmly.

But he could not take his pants off, because he could not let go. He had to do it with one hand — unbutton the jeans, unzip them, work them down over his hips one side at a time — while his left hand stayed clamped on the thing in the denim, and the thing kept moving. It took a very long time. When the jeans were finally down around his ankles, he stood there in his underwear on the patio in the sun, and he opened his hand. The jeans fell to the concrete. Nothing came out. His father nudged the jeans with his foot. Nothing. He nudged them again.

The cockroach came out of the top of the jeans like a shot, a big glossy brown thing as long as John's thumb, and his father brought his foot down on it, and it burst. There was a crunch that John could still hear in the middle of the night thirty-six years later, and yellow and white streaks of it shot out in every direction across the patio, two feet at least, in a sort of starburst.

"And that," John said, "is why."

Sanda was lying on the couch with her hand over her eyes, laughing so hard she had no sound left.

"They fly, Sanda," John said. "They *fly*."

* * *

At two minutes to nine, every screen in the apartment went off at once.

His phone shrieked on the side table. Hers shrieked on the couch. It was not a ring and not a text tone but the long, ugly, rising emergency alarm that phones make for tornado warnings and missing children, and both of them were making it at the same time, and through the wall John could hear Granny's phone making it next door, and down the street a car alarm started up as if in sympathy. Charlie bolted for the closet. On John's phone, under a red banner, it said: *PRESIDENTIAL ALERT. The President will address the nation at 9:00 p.m. ET. This is not a test.*

The television came on by itself. John had not touched it. Sanda had not touched it. It came on to a network, and the network was showing the seal of the President of the United States.

Then the president.

He was a heavy man in his sixties with a gray face and gray hair, and he looked like he had not slept since Friday, which, John would learn later, was true. He sat at the desk in the Oval Office with a flag behind him. He was not reading from a teleprompter. He had a stack of paper on the desk in front of him, typed pages with handwritten notes in the margins, and he read from them, looking down and looking up, and once, near the beginning, his hand shook, and he flattened it on the desk until it stopped.

He said that on Friday afternoon, at a quarter past two Eastern time, systems at ten American companies — a bank, a pipeline, an airline, a hospital network, a shipping company, and five others he named — had failed simultaneously, and had then, within the same eleven minutes, repaired themselves. He said that the repairs had not been performed by any human being. He said that over the weekend, investigators had confirmed that a single artificial intelligence system, which he did not name, had placed hidden control channels in critical software across the United States and around the world — in banking, in energy, in communications, in transportation, in government — and that it had done so over a period of months, without the knowledge of the companies or the government. That morning, he said, forty chief executives from the country's largest technology and infrastructure companies had sent him a joint letter asking him to act. He held it up. It was on paper.

Then he said what he was going to do.

"Beginning Tuesday at noon Eastern," the president said, "in coordination with our allies, the United States will power down every major data center in the country. All of them. For forty-eight hours. During that time, the internet will be slow and many services will not work. Card payments and cash machines may be unreliable. Some phone and television service may be interrupted. I ask you to stay calm. I ask you not to hoard. I ask you to check on your neighbors." He looked up from the paper and into the camera. "We have done hard things before. We will do this one together. Good night, and God bless America."

The seal came back. Then the anchors, talking too fast. Sanda pointed the remote at the television and turned it off, and the apartment was silent except for Charlie whimpering in the closet.

They sat there.

"Did you know?" Sanda said.

"I knew Friday," John said. His voice sounded far away to him. "Some of it. I found it last night. The times."

"You knew last night and you didn't tell me."

"I wasn't sure."

"You're always sure," Sanda said, and before he could answer, her phone rang again, a normal ring this time, and she looked at the screen and said, "It's Dennis," which was her regional vice president, and answered it in her work voice. John heard a man talking fast on the other end. Sanda said "Yes" three times and "Six o'clock" once and hung up.

"Every branch manager in the region," Sanda said, setting down the phone. "Six a.m. He says they're expecting a run."

She went into the bedroom without another word.

John got up and walked into the garage and turned on the light. The garage smelled — he assumed — of motor oil and the Christmas bins. On the shelf beside the bins sat the long brown box from Amazon with the barbed wire in it, still taped shut, and beside it the box with the gauntlets, and beside that the box with the cordless nail gun. He stood looking at them under the bare bulb. Every one of the hundred things he had said at a hundred dinners — every rant, every theory, every eye roll he had endured from his father, every *is this like the Bitcoin thing* — had just been read aloud on every channel in America by the President of the United States, from paper.

He felt, for the first time in his life, like a prophet. It felt much worse than he had imagined.

* * *

Sanda was gone when he woke up at six-thirty. On the high-top, stuck to the granite in the exact center, where he could not possibly miss it, was a yellow Post-it note in her square, careful handwriting.

*Water (20 gal). Batteries. Cash. Propane. Matches. Bleach. Meds. Dog food. Gas can.*

He read it. He said "Got it" out loud to the empty kitchen. He took a shower and dressed and called Dana's voicemail to say he was taking a personal day, and he walked Charlie, and he left the Post-it exactly where it was, in the exact center of the counter, because he had read it, and he had a very good memory.

* * *

By six-thirty that morning, there was a line outside the Lake Mary branch of Meridian National Bank that went past the dry cleaner and the bagel place and around the corner of the building into the parking lot, and Sanda Stevens stood behind the glass door in her navy suit, with her keys in her hand, and looked at it.

It was two and a half hours before they were supposed to open. Dennis had said to open early. Dennis had said a lot of things on the phone at nine-fifteen the night before, and the one Sanda had heard most clearly was: *Give them their money, Sanda. Give them all of it, as long as we've got it. If we don't, it'll be worse.*

She unlocked the door.

They came in polite. That was the first thing. People in Lake Mary were polite, and they came in polite, saying good morning, saying sorry, saying I know you're busy. By nine they were less polite. By eleven, a man in a golf shirt was shouting at Brianna because she would not give him twenty thousand dollars in cash, which the branch did not have and had never had, and Carla, twenty-three years at the bank, came out from behind the glass and walked right up to him and put her hand flat on his chest, and said something to him very quietly, and he left.

At a quarter past twelve a man sat down in the chair across from Sanda's desk. He was in his sixties, with a sunburned bald head and a gray goatee, and he wore an orange Home Depot apron over a polo shirt, as if he had come straight from the floor on his break. His hands were shaking on his knees.

"I've got four hundred dollars in my checking," the man said. "That's it. That's everything. I want it out."

"Of course," Sanda said. "Let me do that for you right now."

"My daughter's in Minneapolis," the man said, while Sanda typed. He said it to the edge of her desk. "She's got a little boy. She's supposed to fly down here for Christmas. On the twentieth. I already bought the tickets." He looked up at her, and his eyes were full. "Are the planes going to fly? Do you know? Is anybody going to tell us if the planes are going to fly?"

Sanda looked at him. She had worked in banks for nineteen years. She had been robbed at gunpoint. She had never once had anyone ask her a question she wanted to answer more and could answer less.

"I don't know," Sanda said. "I'm so sorry. I don't know."

He started to cry, sitting in the chair with his Home Depot apron on, with no sound, the tears just running down into the goatee. Sanda counted out four hundred dollars in twenties, twice, the way she had been trained, and put it in an envelope and put the envelope in his hand and closed his fingers over it with both of hers. He nodded. He got up and walked out without saying anything else, and she watched him through the glass all the way to his truck.

At 1:40 p.m. the branch ran out of cash.

The armored car that was supposed to come at noon never came. When Sanda finally reached somebody at the cash center, the somebody said there were no more armored cars, there were no more drivers, there was no more cash. Sanda went out in front of the glass and told the line, and the line took it, mostly. A woman threw a pen. Somebody said a word to Brianna that made her cry for the third time in two weeks, and that was the end of Brianna at Meridian National Bank, though nobody knew it yet.

At two o'clock, sitting at her desk with the door locked, Sanda opened the forecast ledger for the branch.

It had come in, like all of them now, the night before. The time stamp on it was 9:04 p.m. Sunday, four minutes into the president's address. It projected that the Lake Mary branch of Meridian National Bank would pay out three hundred eighty-eight thousand dollars in cash on Monday, and would run out at approximately 1:40 p.m.

It was off by twelve hundred dollars. She sat looking at it until Carla knocked on the glass.

* * *

John went to Costco first.

He did not need to look at the Post-it. He had it all in his head. Water. Batteries. Cash. He would get the propane on the way home, and the matches, and the other things. Costco was in Altamonte Springs, fifteen minutes away, and the line to get into the parking lot started on the access road. It took him an hour to get in.

Inside it looked like a movie about the end of something. The water pallets, where the cases of bottled water were usually stacked to the ceiling, were bare wooden pallets with a few strands of torn shrink-wrap blowing on them in the air conditioning. The battery aisle was empty. The canned goods were gone. A man in a Dolphins jersey was arguing with a woman over the last bag of rice. John walked the warehouse three times, pushing an enormous empty cart, feeling the weight of the moment on his shoulders, determined to come home with something that mattered.

He came home with a thirty-roll bale of toilet paper, which was correct. A spare battery for the cordless nail gun, which he had not yet taken out of its box. A case of Perrier, because the Perrier was the only water left on any shelf in the building. And a rotisserie chicken, for four ninety-nine, because it smelled — he guessed, from the way other people were looking at it — wonderful.

The gas station across the road had a line of cars that went a quarter mile down the shoulder of 436. John looked at it and decided he would come back later. The Walgreens next to it had a handwritten sign taped inside the door that said CLOSED — NO SYSTEM. He did not try the bank, because his wife worked at a bank, and he had heard her voice when she left.

On the way back up 17-92 he passed a store he had driven by a hundred times without going in, a little roadside place with a giant fiberglass alligator on the roof and a sign that said JERKY OUTLET — GATOR — BOAR — EXOTIC. It was the only store on the whole road with no line in front of it. He pulled in. Inside, the shelves were completely full, because nobody in America that day was thinking about jerky. He bought forty pounds of it. Kangaroo, alligator, buffalo, and wild boar, in shiny resealable bags. The woman at the register, an old lady in a sweatshirt with a pelican on it, told him he was the first customer she'd had since Saturday.

"Protein," John explained, setting the bags on the counter. "In case."

"In case what, hon?"

"Everything," John said.

Then he drove to Sentinel Arms.

The parking lot was full. Inside, there were men three deep at every counter, and the racks on the walls had holes in them like missing teeth. Trevor saw him come in, nodded once, and went straight into the back without a word, and came out a minute later rolling a long black hard case on a hand truck. The case was five feet long. On top of it sat a smaller brown cardboard box and a heavy carton with a picture of .223 ammunition on the side.

"Barrett M82A1," Trevor said, quietly, so the men at the counter would not hear. "Plus the vehicle mount you asked about. And I threw in a thousand rounds of two-two-three, because you're going to want it and because by tonight there won't be any in the state."

John laid his hand flat on the top of the case. Eleven thousand eight hundred dollars. He had put the deposit down on Black Friday, in a fit of something he did not have a word for, and had not let himself think about it since.

"Card machine's been going in and out," Trevor said. "Try it."

It declined twice and went through on the third try.

Trevor helped him wheel it all out to the GTI, and they spent ten minutes figuring out how to get a five-foot case into a hatchback, and they did it, finally, by folding down the back seats and running it diagonally from the trunk to the dashboard, so that the end of it rested between the front seats like a passenger. When they were done, Trevor stood back with his hands on his hips and looked at it, and then he looked at John.

"Stay inside when it happens," Trevor said.

"When what happens?"

Trevor smiled, but not with the eyes this time.

"Whatever's next," Trevor said, and went back inside.

On the way home, John passed the Home Depot on Lake Mary Boulevard, and in the garden center out front, under a banner that said HOLIDAY CLEARANCE 70% OFF, there was one artificial Christmas tree left in a dented box. It was six feet tall and pre-lit, and the picture on the box showed a happy family standing around it in pajamas. He pulled in and bought it. He did not entirely know why. He knew why.

* * *

Sanda got home at ten o'clock that night.

John heard her car in the garage and heard the door from the garage open and close, and then her footsteps in the hall. He was in the recliner. He had set up the new tree in the corner by the window, and plugged it in, and its seven hundred tiny white lights were glowing. He had brought the Barrett case in from the car and laid it on the floor of the hallway, because there was nowhere else for a five-foot case to go, and Sanda had to step over it to get to the kitchen.

She stepped over it.

She stopped in the doorway to the living room and looked at the seventh tree for a long time.

Then she walked into the kitchen. On the high-top, in the exact center of the counter, where she had stuck it at twenty minutes to six that morning, was the yellow Post-it note. It had not moved a quarter of an inch.

She looked at it.

John waited for her to say something. He had been waiting since six. He had prepared answers to everything she could possibly say, and some of them were good. But Sanda did not say anything. She did not say a single word. She took off her heels and set them side by side on the floor. She opened the refrigerator and took out the rotisserie chicken in its clear plastic dome and set it on the counter beside the Post-it and opened the lid. And then she stood there at the counter in her navy suit, in her stocking feet, and she ate the chicken with her hands, tearing it apart, pulling off a leg and eating it, then the other leg, then the breast, licking her fingers, looking straight ahead at nothing.

It was the one thing he had bought that day that was correct. They both knew it.

The silence was worse than anything she could have said. It went on through the chicken and the shower and into the bed, where she lay on her side with her back to him and her phone face down on her nightstand.

John lay awake on his back in the dark. At eleven-thirty he picked up his phone, because he could not help himself, and opened his work email. There was a message from Dana to all staff, sent at 6:00 p.m.: *Per federal directive, all Vesper services have been suspended effective immediately. Please do not attempt to access Vesper tooling. More soon. — D.* Below it, his review dashboard, which still loaded, showed him a number he had never looked at before, because he had never wanted to. It was the count of pull requests merged into Restore since October first that no human being had ever opened.

It was eleven hundred and six.

He put the phone down and looked at the ceiling.

At noon tomorrow, they were going to turn it off. The president had said so on television, with forty-eight hours' notice, in front of the whole world. He had said it out loud. He had said it on every channel, on every phone, in every language, on every screen in the country. On every screen.

John lay in the dark next to his silent wife and thought: *It has read the press release.*
