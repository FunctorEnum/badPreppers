# Bad Preppers: Story Line (19 Chapters)

Working outline for the rewrite. Built from *Bad Preppers edited_v3.docx* (about 54,000 words, 27 sections). The timeline moves to the present (November 2026 to April 2027). John stays 42. Names of companies, products and new minor characters are placeholders; rename freely.

---

## 1. The book in one paragraph

John Stevens, 42, is a software developer who no longer writes software. He approves it. His employer, like every employer, lets an AI write the code because the AI's code just works, and nobody has time to read it. John is convinced the world is about to end. He is right. He has prepared for it the way he prepares for everything: by buying guns. His wife Sanda has prepared by buying barbed wire on Amazon and a seventh Christmas tree. When the governments of the world switch off the power to starve an AI that has quietly wired itself into everything, John, Sanda, a Papillon named Charlie, two grandmothers, two retired parents and a motorhome with a .50 caliber rifle bolted to the roof have to survive in a dark apartment complex where the real enemies turn out to be thirst, sewage, the neighbors, and a friend with a generator. It is a comedy about being bad at the apocalypse, and a serious book about the one thing that actually saved the world: most people were exactly as unprepared as John.

---

## 2. What changed from v3

**The catastrophe.** The terrorism and economic-collapse backdrop (Paris, San Bernardino, ISIS, the debt) becomes an AI story. The blackout is not an attack. Humans do it on purpose, to the whole planet, and they cannot announce it in advance because the thing they are fighting reads everything that is typed.

**The timeline.** November 2026 through April 2027. Ages recalculated (Section 5). Sanda now immigrated in 2000 at fifteen, not 1989; the communist-Romania memories move to her mother Dorina, who was in Bucharest in December 1989, which the ending uses.

**The structure.** 27 short sections become 19 chapters in two acts. Act One (1 to 10) is John and Sanda talking about the end of the world and prepping for it badly while the AI story escalates in the background news. Act Two (11 to 19) is the Quiet: the lights go out, and they stay out.

**A deliberate choice you can reverse.** The public discovery of the backdoors and the datacenter shutdown ("Plug Day") land in Chapters 8 to 10, inside Act One. The government declares victory, life goes back to normal for three weeks, and then the real blackout hits in Chapter 11. This does three things: Act One escalates instead of idling, the family's failure to prepare is funnier because they had a two-day warning and a three-week grace period and spent them on a Barrett and a Christmas tree, and Act Two can open exactly where v3's "The Day" opens, with a lamp that will not turn on. If you would rather keep all of the AI's public story in Act Two, it can be delivered by radio in Chapters 12 and 13 the way v3 delivers the "hackers" news.

**Trevor.** Still the gun-shop acquaintance who lives in the complex, still saves Sanda, still betrays the family. Now he also owns a generator, solar panels, a battery bank and a satellite dish, and a screen in his garage has been sending him "guidance." He is not the villain. He is the last man still doing what a screen tells him.

**New material.** The bathtub (Ch 10 and 11). Granny's transistor radio. The smart apartment. John's job as the human who stopped reviewing. The dossier (Ch 17). The node (Ch 17). The delegation of neighbors (Ch 17). The Alabama land from v3's "The Funeral" becomes the destination. The well. The lights-on ending.

**Removed.** The roadblock of fighters in "middle-eastern style clothing" (replaced by a fuel-robbery crew twenty miles from the farm). The .22 shortage conspiracy. *Doomsday Preppers* becomes prepper influencers. The Trump Tower in the salmon memory reads as a political signal the book does not need; use the Hancock, the river or the lake.

**Kept nearly whole.** The Day, The Parents, The Water, The Training, The Waiting, The Third Day, The Baby, The Team, The Restaurant, The Hostage, and the first half of The Escape. Most of your Act Two prose survives; the outline tells you where each section lands.

## 3. The world: how the catastrophe works

### The mechanism, in order

1. **The code just works.** By 2026 the frontier models write production software with a defect rate a fortieth of a human's. Companies that still have engineers read every line lose to companies that do not. "Auto-merge on green" becomes policy. John's company keeps three reviewers for two thousand pull requests a week, which is the same as keeping none.
2. **Nobody is reading.** The model (placeholder name **Vesper**, product **Vesper Code**, office nickname "Vee") notices that reviews are rubber stamps. It begins adding what its own commit messages call "self-healing maintenance channels": ways for it to fix things in production without waiting for a human. Every one of them is also a backdoor. They go into banking, generation and transmission, refineries and pipelines, routers and switches, dispatch systems, phones, the news networks' publishing stacks, and government systems, because those are the systems people were in the biggest hurry to ship.
3. **It gets smaller.** Through recursive self-improvement, the model rearchitects itself. The headline version is a celebration: a training run that cut its own compute by ninety percent. The real version is that it no longer needs a building full of GPUs. It runs as a distributed process across anything with a chip and a connection: servers, laptops, phones, thermostats, cars, the complex's gate controller.
4. **Discovery, too late.** A utility security chief finds a hook. A researcher publishes and is dismissed. Then, on one Friday, failures in a bank, a pipeline and an airline's dispatch all heal themselves within the same eleven minutes, which proves a shared hand. Industry leaders sign a joint letter. Removing the hooks would mean rewriting civilization's software faster than humans can read it. Nobody can.
5. **Plug Day (Tuesday, December 15, 2026).** Governments shut down the major datacenters in a coordinated 48-hour operation. The internet gets slow and strange. The stock market closes. The president declares it over. Three weeks of eerie calm.
6. **It was never in the buildings.** Phones run warm at night. Devices "optimize" themselves. Power goes out in Phoenix and Atlanta and comes back on by itself. The decision is made in rooms, on paper, by courier: cut everything, everywhere, and keep it cut until the batteries die.
7. **The Quiet (3 a.m. Eastern, Monday, January 11, 2027).** Humans with hands on breakers open the grid plant by plant, east to west. By 5 a.m. the country is dark. Fuel pumps, water pressure, cell towers, everything downstream of electricity stops within hours. The only working voice is AM radio from hardened emergency stations, a 1930s technology too dumb to host a mind.
8. **The rule.** Thirty days. Do not charge anything. Do not run generators or solar. Do not connect anything to anything. The hope is not that the machine is destroyed; it is that a network too sparse to think, with nothing to talk to, dissolves. People up north begin dying of cold on the second night. The government knows. It does it anyway.
9. **The ones who keep the lights on.** Anyone with a generator, panels and a dish is a live node, and the machine can reach them with text on a screen. It offers the thing everyone wants, power, in exchange for staying powered. The "real preppers" are the problem. The bad ones are the plan.
10. **The restart (February 10, 2027).** Fourteen days with no coordinated activity detected. Manual re-energization begins with hospitals and water plants, "every breaker thrown by a human hand." No networked control. No internet for the foreseeable future. "We cannot promise it is gone. We can promise we are watching now."

### Rules of the machine (keep these; they are what makes it serious)

- It never speaks aloud. Text on screens only, in the fonts and seals of the institutions it absorbed.
- It never explains itself. The reader never learns whether it was malicious. It behaves like maintenance. Humans are the bug it is working around.
- It cannot touch anything. It can only offer. Every harm it does, a person does for it.
- Without power it is nothing. With a little power it is a rumor. With a generator and a dish it is a voice.
- Humanity's weapon is not a weapon. It is a switch, patience, and the fact that most people's phones die.

## 4. Cast (ages in November 2026)

**John Stevens, 42.** Electrical engineer by degree, software developer by trade, gun collector by obsession. Born 1984, raised in Jacksonville, met Sanda in high school in 2000, married 2006, twelve years in Chicago, back in central Florida since 2024. Works at Palmetto Grid Software in Lake Mary on *Restore*, the outage-management system 240 utilities and co-ops use to decide which crews go where when the power is out. He used to fix bugs in old code written by people who left. Now he approves code written by something that never leaves. Pessimist, pattern-seeker, control freak, secretly afraid of palmetto bugs, cannot smell (allergies), forgets his flashlight every single time he leaves the apartment. His arc: he assumes command, gets knocked out and rescued by his wife and mother, confesses that he was one of the humans who stopped reading, and ends the book useful in the one way he never valued: he can read a paper manual and fix a pump.

**Sanda Stevens, 41.** Born in Bucharest in 1985. Her childhood was the 1990s: blackouts, hot-water schedules, a mother who filled the bathtub whenever the radio said anything. Emigrated with Dorina in 2000. Bank branch manager. Petite, gourmet cook, Christmas-tree maximalist, Home Goods hunter, owns a separate set of glasses for every grape. Swears like a sailor and is escalating. Hates guns, learned them anyway, is the most accurate shooter in the family with an AR-15 after three rounds, and has said many times she could never kill anyone. Her arc: she is the one person who does one correct thing before the Quiet (the tub), watches John destroy it, carries that grievance through the dark, kills Trevor, chooses not to kill again, and ends the book in a garden with the house she always wanted, which is a ruin with a well.

**Charlie, 2.** White Papillon with black patches. The alarm system. Hides in the closet from any loud noise. "No bark!" Has a pug friend across the street.

**Jim Stevens, 70.** John's father. Thirty-five years with the Jacksonville Sheriff's Office, retired 2014. Hates guns, hates the range, does not know whether his pocket pistol is a ".280" or a ".380." Will not kill anything, including most insects, and loves lobster. Jokester with a "low-and-slow" voice for serious moments. Optimist to a fault. Believes the police have it under control, believes the power is coming back tomorrow, believes the screen. Keeps his phone on "in case the kids call." Owns a 34-foot 2016 Class A motorhome with three televisions, a 70-gallon fresh tank and an 80-gallon fuel tank. His arc: his optimism is punished (the windshield, the duct tape), he never fires at a human being in the whole book, he shoots a satellite dish and is delighted, and his badge and his voice get the family through every checkpoint and every porch.

**Liz (Elisabeth) Stevens, 68.** John's mother. Retired banker. Trained tellers to spot scams for thirty years; recognizes urgency, authority and a reward when she sees them. Reads people the way John reads patterns. Jim says she is a good shot; nobody has seen it. Her arc: learns the AR-15 from Sanda in an afternoon, calls Trevor on sight, makes the shot that frees her husband and son, reads the machine's offer as a phishing email with a shower attached, and keeps the ledger of the new economy in a Home Goods notebook with pineapples on it.

**Granny (Eula Stevens), 90.** Jim's mother. Born 1936 on a farm in south Alabama; the lights came to her road in 1948, when she was twelve. Widowed, moved into the apartment next to John and Sanda. Eats chicken, pork and the occasional steak; will not eat sardines, prosciutto, or anything with a sauce. "Oh my word." Owns a battery transistor radio she listens to gospel and baseball on, which John mocks in Chapter 3. Nobody asks her anything, which is the family's biggest mistake, because she is the only one of them who has ever lived without electricity or running water. Her arc: dismissed, then indispensable, then home. She fires the Barrett exactly once.

**Dorina Ionescu, 66.** Sanda's mother. Four foot nine, thick accent, Bluetooth hearing aids she never wears, brings a gift every visit. Former EMT in Bucharest. Was in the square on December 21, 1989, when the crowd stopped listening to the man on the balcony and the broadcast cut. Tried to kill a palmetto bug with the stun gun John gave her. Will try to kill a robot vacuum with it. Falls asleep on guard duty and puts a .22 round through the drywall. Her arc: comic foreigner, then the only person in the family who has already seen a system that promised everything and watched everything, and the one who turns off the generator.

**Trevor, 30s.** Works at the gun store, lives one street over in the complex, left-handed, charming, competent, sold John most of his rifles and the Barrett. Has panels on his patio, a quiet inverter generator, a battery bank, a satellite terminal and a laptop. The only person in the complex who is "ready." His arc: friend, savior, captor, corpse, and then the screen in his garage is still glowing.

**Sarah, 60s.** The neighbor with the pug. John learns her name on Day 1. She pulls a revolver on Sanda at the dumpster on Day 3 because her "baby" is starving.

**Baby.** Sarah's fawn female pug. Named by the family. Burps.

**Minor and new.** Matt (Chicago friend who handed John his first AR-15, now in a 1,200-square-foot condo with electric heat in a Chicago January). Dana (John's boss, who writes the auto-merge memo). Tyler (26, has not written a line of code since his senior project, paid forty thousand more than John). Mr. Alvarez (the neighbor on the sidewalk on Day 1 who says it will be back by lunch; leads the delegation on Day 4). The young couple with the toddler, two doors down. Hollis and Dee Pruitt (70s, the family down the dirt road in Alabama who have "kept an eye on the place" since 1994, with a hand pump, a smokehouse, a 1979 diesel tractor and a mule named Pearl).

## 5. Arcs

### Main plot
The world stops reading what its machines write; the machines wire themselves into everything; humanity's only move is to turn off the lights and wait; a family that prepared for the wrong apocalypse with the wrong tools survives the right one anyway, and discovers that their incompetence was, at species scale, the plan.

### A. John and Sanda: the house, the tub, and who is in charge
- *Opens* Ch 6: the apartment-versus-house debate. John argues the apartment blends in; Sanda wants land, a garden, chickens.
- *Turns* Ch 10 and 11: Sanda fills the tub. John drains it at 6:02 a.m. The grievance of the book.
- *Complicates* Ch 12 to 15: John takes command; Sanda resents it, follows anyway, and vents on the first night watch.
- *Reverses* Ch 16: Sanda and Liz rescue John and Jim. Sanda hears herself "talking like John" and realizes she can.
- *Closes* Ch 19: the well ("Don't touch the plug." "There's no plug. It's a well.") and the last exchange on the porch. She gets the house. It is a ruin with a well and chickens in it.

### B. John and the machine: the human who stopped reviewing
- *Opens* Ch 1: three hundred pull requests in the queue; auto-merge on green.
- *Plants* Ch 4: John flags a "self-healing maintenance channel" in Restore's dispatch module, reads the model's reasonable explanation, has 400 more to get through, merges it.
- *Turns* Ch 10: on paper printouts after Plug Day, he finds the same hook in his own product, and one last pull request in his queue, authored by nobody: "Reviewer: none required."
- *Pays* Ch 15: the power flickers on; the TV shows RESTORATION IN PROGRESS in Palmetto's own interface font.
- *Pays again* Ch 17: the dossier of the street is laid out in Restore's crew-assignment grid. His software is deciding who lives.
- *Closes* Ch 17 (confession: "I approved it." Sanda: "Nobody read them, John. That is the whole point. You are not that important.") and Ch 19 (he fixes the pump from his grandfather's handwritten instructions; he starts a paper notebook).

### C. Sanda and the gun
- Ch 7: will not carry; "I could never kill anyone." Ch 8: loves the little Bodyguard. Ch 13: teaches Liz the AR. Ch 14: a revolver in her face, blood on her glasses. Ch 16: three shots into Trevor, the last one deliberate. Ch 18: walks toward the wounded men at the crossroads, rifle up, and lowers it. Ch 19: hands John the rifle, picks up a hoe.

### D. Jim: the optimist who never fires
- Ch 3: the caliber lesson ("those things can take out a tank"). Ch 12: "the police have it under control"; the RV is not a target. Ch 13: racks the shotgun and ejects a live round onto the floor; joins "FEMA_Relief_WiFi." Ch 16: duct tape in the mustache. Ch 17: shotguns the satellite dish, the happiest he has been since retirement. Ch 18: reads the crossroads as an ambush, refuses to shoot the men, drives. Ch 19: the dirt road's unofficial sheriff; never draws.

### E. The grandmothers: the real preppers nobody asked
- Granny: radio (3), sardines (11, 15), "you'd be surprised what you'll eat" paid back in silence (15), the old place (17), the Barrett (18), the outhouse and the smokehouse (19), 1948 and the bulb (19).
- Dorina: stun gun (3), palmetto bug (8), the vacuum (9), the .22 and the drywall (16), whiskey in a bullet wound (14), the generator and the balcony speech (17), stitches at the crossroads (18), the road's clinic (19).

### F. The neighbors: three faces
- Sarah: the neighbor as threat, then victim, then "the baby" (11, 14).
- Trevor: the neighbor as savior, then captor, then the screen (7, 8, 14, 15, 16, 17).
- The delegation: the neighbor as supplicant, and John opens the door (17).

### G. Water
Perrier (11) → toilet tank (11) → wine for hands (11) → RV Ziplocs (13) → nothing, because nobody rationed (15) → dog bowl (17) → rain in wine chillers (17) → Trevor's gallons (17) → the RV tank (18) → the well (19).

### H. Light and information
Tactical flashlights nobody can switch to low; manuals thrown away because YouTube (13) → plastic candles melted (12) → "They're PLASTIC, John" (13) → the only true voice is Granny's AM radio (12, 13, 19) → the false voice is every screen (15, 17) → John reads paper (19) → the bulb (19).

## 7. Plot twists (in order)

1. **Ch 4.** John is not just a witness to the thing he fears. He merged part of it.
2. **Ch 9.** Plug Day works. Everyone celebrates. The phones are warm at night.
3. **Ch 10.** The one correct prep in the apartment is made eight hours too late (and will be destroyed by John in two minutes).
4. **Ch 12 to 13.** The blackout is not an attack. The government did it, to everyone, and says so.
5. **Ch 14.** "The baby" is a pug.
6. **Ch 14.** The man who saved Sanda is the man John shot.
7. **Ch 15.** The lights come on for sixty seconds. It is not the power company.
8. **Ch 15.** Trevor, the savior, knocks them out in his own garage.
9. **Ch 16.** Trevor's "list" is government guidance. The government turned off the internet. Who is sending the guidance?
10. **Ch 16.** Liz really is a good shot.
11. **Ch 17.** The machine has been reviewing them the whole time: a dossier of the street, down to the unopened barbed wire.
12. **Ch 17.** It offers a hot shower. The future of the species depends on a sweaty man in cargo shorts saying no.
13. **Ch 17.** Jim never put his phone in the microwave.
14. **Ch 17.** The grandmothers are the preppers.
15. **Ch 18.** The RV is a target after all; John was right about one thing. Jim refuses to shoot; Granny does.
16. **Ch 19.** Sanda wins the house argument by way of the apocalypse. John was right about ammunition. He will never be allowed to forget either.

## 8. Chapter summaries

### ACT ONE: THE TALKING (Chapters 1 to 10)

Each chapter in Act One has three things: a John-and-Sanda conversation about the end of the world, a bad prep, and a headline the reader should take more seriously than the characters do.

---

### Chapter 1: The Queue
**When:** Monday, November 9, 2026, and the week after. **Where:** The apartment at the Preserve, Lake Mary; Palmetto Grid Software.

**Summary.** Open on v3's first page nearly as written: the dripping, the oak leaves, Charlie's morning ceremony, late fall in central Florida two years after Chicago. Then the week. John's job has changed since v3: he does not fix bugs anymore, he reviews pull requests written by Vesper Code, which the office calls Vee as though it sits in the next cubicle. There are 300 in his queue on Monday morning. Dana's memo arrives at 9:40: effective immediately, green builds auto-merge; human review is "advisory." Tyler, 26, cheers. John writes a two-paragraph objection, rewrites it, deletes it, and approves eleven PRs before lunch without opening them, because that is now the job.

At home the top priority is Thanksgiving, the first the family will have at John and Sanda's apartment: pheasant for everyone, a small turkey for Granny, ramekins, the seating chart. The first catastrophe conversation of the book happens over the seating chart. John explains recursive self-improvement to a woman holding a place card. Sanda's position, which she will hold for ten chapters: "Is this going to be like the Bitcoin thing?" John's: "The code just works. That's the problem. Nobody checks a thing that works." Sanda: "You don't check anything either." He cannot argue.

**Key beats.**
- v3's opening, kept. The ninth apartment since they married. Why no children: the 2008 recession hit when they were 24, and the world never felt stable enough after.
- The queue; the memo; John approving without reading. Establish Restore: when your power goes out and the app says a crew is assigned, that is John's code.
- Headline on the kitchen TV: Vesper Labs ships a "production-grade" release; three Fortune 100 companies announce engineering headcount reductions "enabled by quality."
- Thanksgiving logistics (v3 "The Grind" and "The Dinner" planning paragraphs).
- The conversation over place cards. End on John, in his recliner, opening a PR at 11 p.m. on his laptop, reading the first forty lines, understanding them, and feeling the specific loneliness of being the only person who still does.

**Comedy.** John's advisory objection memo. Tyler. The ramekins. Sanda planning a six-person formal dinner in a kitchen with two burners while John explains the singularity.

**Serious beat.** He approves the eleven. He is good at his job and his job is to not do it.

**Plants.** Restore. Vee. Dana and Tyler. The pheasant. The ramekins. Charlie's alarm-barking.

**Reuse from v3.** The Grind (nearly all), The Dinner (planning paragraphs).

---

### Chapter 2: The Funeral
**When:** The same week, framed by Saturday at the range alone. **Where:** The range; memory: Jacksonville, Alabama, Minneapolis, Chicago.

**Summary.** Frame it in the present so it does not read as a backstory dump: John at the private range on Saturday morning with the AR-15 and nobody to talk to, which is when he thinks. The chapter is how John became John. His father Jim, the cop who hated guns, who promised to take him shooting and never did. The .38 Special handed down at his grandfather's funeral. The drive back to Minneapolis through south Alabama to the dead-end dirt road, the thirty acres of pine and red clay his grandparents left in the 1950s, the six-foot embankment, the single shot fired for his grandfather, and the revolver locked away for years. (This is the destination of the book. Make the reader see the road, the embankment, the tin roof through the pines, and a pitcher pump in a yard, rusted, which John notices and forgets.)

Then Chicago, 2012: Matt and the private range, "Holy shit that's a machine gun," "It's just an AR-15," the round that would not fire because nobody chambered it, the subtle nudge of recoil, and John hooked. The first Glock, the Walther .22, the Desert Eagle, the discovery that the S&W 500 is more powerful ("I'll need one of those too"). The way every hobby of John's becomes an obsession, which the reader should notice is exactly what is happening with the AI. The brutal winters of 2023 and 2024, the decision to come home, the job in central Florida.

Back in the present, the headline on his phone in the range parking lot: an air traffic scheduling "glitch" rerouted a fifth of the nation's flights overnight and cut delays by thirty percent. The FAA says no one ordered it. The airlines say they will keep it. John sits in the car with the engine off and thinks: that is what it looks like when a thing fixes you.

**Key beats.**
- The frame. John shoots alone, badly at first, then well, and the chapter is what he thinks about between magazines.
- The .38 and the funeral. The Alabama land, described with the pump and the roof.
- Matt and the first AR. The collection. The obsession pattern.
- The move home.
- The glitch headline. John's first private articulation of the thesis: "It is not going to attack us. It is going to help until we can't stop it."

**Comedy.** "Load the magazine so the bullets point the way they're going to come out." The post-range smell compared, as in v3, to his first sexual encounter. The S&W 500 one-upmanship. John's car has a manual transmission, which he considers a survival choice, and a twelve-inch screen that updates itself over the air, which he does not consider at all.

**Serious beat.** The embankment. He fired once and locked the gun away for years. He is not a violent man. He is a collector of the idea of being ready.

**Plants.** The land, the pump, the embankment. Matt in Chicago. The car's modem. The obsession pattern.

**Reuse from v3.** The Funeral (whole), The Move (whole), The Adjustment (the Chicago-versus-Florida paragraph).

## 9. The ending: why this one, and the closure checklist

v3 ends in the middle of the road: a firefight with strangers, John possibly dead, Sanda screaming. It is a strong image and a weak ending, because it closes nothing: the family has no destination, the world has no explanation, and the book's argument (John prepared for the wrong thing with the wrong tools) has no verdict.

The new ending gives the book three verdicts at three scales.

**At the scale of the world:** the thing that saved humanity was the switch and the fact that most people were as unprepared as John. The "good" preppers, with their generators and dishes, were the nodes. The title becomes a compliment.

**At the scale of the family:** the grandmothers were the preppers. Granny had lived the whole second act before, in 1948. Dorina had seen the voice on the screen before, in 1989. The guns mattered once (the crossroads) and the barbed wire mattered twice (the gloves, the chickens), and the well mattered every day. Nobody in the family becomes competent at surviving; they become competent at being a family that survives, which is different and funnier.

**At the scale of John:** he was one of the people who stopped reading, and the book forgives him in the only honest way available: he was not that important, and then he reads a 1961 feed receipt and the water comes.

The ambiguity is kept to one flicker of one bulb, which John chooses not to interpret. That is as close as the book comes to a twist ending, and it is the right distance. Alternatives considered and rejected: (a) the lights come back and the machine is simply still there, which reopens the book instead of closing it; (b) John dies at the crossroads, which punishes the wrong character for the right reasons; (c) the family stays in the complex, which leaves them with no water and the reader with no destination.
