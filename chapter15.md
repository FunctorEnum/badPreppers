# Chapter Fifteen

## The Team

> *"How have you been rationing it?"*
>
> — Trevor

Trevor set the empty glass down on the counter, very gently, the way you set down something that isn't yours.

"So you're out," Trevor said.

John looked at the row of pitchers. Twelve pitchers and the rooster, a punch bowl, a mixing bowl. All of them empty. Three days ago, they had been full.

"I thought for sure we had enough for a week," John said. "At least a week."

Trevor leaned his good hip against the counter. He was wearing nothing above the waist but Dorina's bandage, white and tight across his right shoulder and chest, with a brown stain spreading on the back of it, and he looked at John with his pale gray eyes, curious, the way a man looks at a car that won't start.

"How have you been rationing it?" Trevor asked.

John opened his mouth. He closed it. He looked down at the floor, and rubbed his forehead with the heel of his hand.

"Rationing," John said.

There was a long pause.

"Yeah," John said finally, looking up. "That would have been smart." He spread his hands at the pitchers. "We just drank it. Whenever we wanted. As much as we wanted. We made coffee with it. We gave it to the dog. We gave the other dog a bath. I washed my wife off in the tub with two bowls of it this morning." He laughed, without anything in it. "We've been drinking water like there was a tap."

He saw a grin start at the corner of Trevor's mouth under the beard, and stop, and not quite go away.

*That cocky fucker*, John thought. *He's standing in my kitchen, he just drank the last of my water, and he's laughing at me.*

"We could go back to the motor home," Jim said, from the couch. He did not sound sure. John thought of the red pin on the little blue map at three in the morning, sitting on top of the motor home, and of the deputies who had been gone by four. He pictured the motor home with its windshield smashed and its door hanging open. If it was still there at all.

"I've got six gallons in my garage," Trevor said.

He said it loud enough for the whole room to hear. Granny turned around on her stool. Liz looked up from the couch. And something in John unclenched so suddenly that he felt dizzy, and he hated himself a little for wanting to punch the man a second ago.

"We have holy water," Dorina announced.

Everybody turned. She had gone into the pantry and come out with the plastic bottle with the masking-tape label, AGHEASMĂ in blue marker, a little more than half full, and she held it up with both hands, the way she had held up the paper target and the cozonac.

"From the church," Dorina said. "From Boboteaza. Is blessed."

"Is it — okay to drink?" Jim asked carefully. "Holy water?"

"Is the best water," Dorina said, offended.

They gave it to Granny first, a mouthful in a juice glass. Granny drank it and considered it with her head on one side.

"Tastes like regular water," Granny said.

"Is supposed to," Dorina said, and crossed herself, and poured a little into each dog's bowl, and Charlie drank his, and the pug drank hers, and Dorina crossed herself again, for the dogs.

* * *

"So what's the plan?" John asked, turning back to Trevor. "Are you planning to stay over here? Should we combine our resources?"

He said it seriously, a man making a business proposal, and Trevor answered him the same way.

"If everybody here's okay with it," Trevor said, "I think we'd be stronger together. I have enough water to last all of us a week." He looked straight at John. "*If* we ration it." He let that sit. "I've got food, too. Enough for several weeks. Rice, beans, canned stuff. A water filter. Every consumable thing, from here on, has got to be rationed. Every one. If any of us are going to get through this." He paused. "Whatever this is."

He looked around the room, and John looked with him, and saw it: the faces of his family turning toward Trevor like plants toward a window. His mother. His father. Granny, on her stool. Even Sanda.

"And John," Trevor said, "I've got a pretty good idea what kind of arsenal you've got in this apartment. I sold you most of it." He smiled, a little. "I can help you defend it. Against whatever's coming."

There was a new authority in his voice that John had never heard in it before, not behind the counter, not at the range. John felt it the way an old dog feels a young one walk into the yard. He looked at Trevor's bandage.

"Uh, buddy," John said. "You haven't forgotten you've got a bullet hole straight through your *right* shoulder, have you?"

"I'm left-handed," Trevor said coldly.

*That's fucking convenient*, John thought.

But he looked down at Trevor's rifle, leaning against the wall where Jim had put it, and there was the ejection port on the left side of the receiver, built to throw the empty cases away from a left-handed shooter's face. He remembered Trevor writing the approval number on the background check form with his hand curled over the top of the pen. It was true. With one good arm, Trevor could still shoot that rifle better than anyone in this apartment.

"I'm sorry, man," John said, and meant it. "All you've done is try to help us, and I'm being an asshole."

"No worries." Trevor smiled, and the old easy smile came back. "Everybody's stressed. Tired, hungry, thirsty, smelly. It's understandable." He held out his left hand. "So. Do we have a deal?"

He knew the answer. Everyone in the room knew the answer.

"Definitely," John said, and shook it with his left. "Let's go get your water now. It's going to be dark soon, and I do *not* want to be outside after dark."

* * *

Before they went, Sanda made him eat.

She set a plate in front of him at the dining table — the white china, a linen napkin — with a handful of almonds, a slice of Manchego going soft at the edges, and a strip of jerky from one of the shiny bags. Trevor picked up the jerky with his left hand and looked at it.

"What is this?"

"Kangaroo," John said.

Trevor turned it over. "Where do you even buy kangaroo jerky in Florida?"

"A store on 17-92 with an alligator on the roof."

"How much did you buy?"

"Forty pounds," John said. "It was the only full shelf in the state."

Trevor chewed and looked at him for a while. "Forty pounds of kangaroo," he said. "And no water." He did not say it unkindly. He said it the way you would read a number off a chart.

He asked questions while he ate. He asked them easily, between bites, the way a man makes conversation at a barbecue, and he did not write anything down. How many of them could shoot. Four, John said; five, if you counted Dorina and the .22. Whether the Barrett had come with the mount. It had; it was in the hall closet, in its case, with the ten rounds. Whether Granny was eighty. "Ninety," Granny said from her stool, "and I can hear you." Whether anybody was on medication. Just Jim's blood pressure pills, which were in the motor home, and John's old Vicodin, which was in Trevor. Whether they had a generator. No. Whether they had any way of getting news from outside. "Granny's radio," John said, and Trevor glanced at the little tan Sony on the counter and smiled and said, "Huh. Old school."

Liz Stevens sat on the couch with the AR across her knees and watched him do it.

She had spent thirty years watching people from the other side of a desk. She had watched men who wanted a loan, and she had watched men who wanted to know what time the armored car came, and the second kind asked their questions exactly like this — warmly, in passing, tucked between other things, so that you had answered before you noticed you had been asked. She did not say anything. She was not sure. But she watched his eyes go around the room while he chewed: to the bedroom closet where the safes were, to the hall closet, to the front door, to the sliding door, and back, every time, to John.

* * *

Sanda had gone into the bedroom and come back with one of John's t-shirts, because Trevor could not walk across the complex at dusk in a bandage. It was a gray t-shirt from the Palmetto Grid company picnic two summers ago, an extra-large, with the company's logo across the chest — a stylized palmetto frond with a lightning bolt in the middle — and the words RESTORE: POWER TO THE PEOPLE across the back, which had been Dana's idea. She helped him get it on over the bandage, one arm at a time.

Trevor looked down at the logo on his chest.

"Palmetto Grid," Trevor said. "You work there?"

"I did," John said. "I do. I don't know."

"Huh," Trevor said, and something moved behind his eyes for a second, and then didn't.

"Mom," John said, turning to Liz. "You've got your AR. I need you to protect this place and everybody in it while Dad and I go with Trevor. Ten minutes."

Liz stood up. She swung the AR-15 around off her back on its sling, the way Sanda had shown her yesterday, and brought it up into a low ready with the muzzle at the floor, and set her feet, and her face went perfectly flat and perfectly hard, and for a moment John's mother, in her quilted vest and her Keds and her reading glasses on their beaded chain, looked like somebody who had spent twenty years in the Army.

Across the room, John saw Sanda's face. She was confused, and she was hurt. Yesterday afternoon she had taught Liz how to hold that rifle. Liz had never fired it. And John had just put his mother in charge.

He caught Sanda's eye, and he winked.

Sanda looked at him for a second. Then her face cleared. They had been together since they were fifteen and sixteen years old, and she knew him better than he knew himself, and she knew exactly what he was doing: he was giving his mother something to hold on to. Something to be. Everybody in that room needed something to be, and confidence was the thing they were going to run out of before water.

That was what made him good at this, Sanda thought. The way he looked at a room and saw what everyone in it needed. She hated, a little, that she thought it.

On his way to the door she caught his sleeve and pulled him down the hall into the bedroom, which was code, and pushed the door closed behind them.

"You put me in the tub," Sanda said. "This morning."

"It was the only place I could think of."

"It was dry." She looked up at him in the half dark. "I was lying in there covered in that woman's blood, and I looked down, and it was dry. And I thought, *he drained it*. That was the first thing I thought. Before I even thought I was alive."

"I know," John said. "I'm sorry."

"I know you're sorry." She pushed her glasses up her nose with one knuckle. "Bring back the water, John."

"Six gallons."

"Bring back all six," Sanda said, and opened the door.

* * *

Granny was sitting at the kitchen counter on her stool. She had been sitting there quietly through the whole conversation, and nobody had been paying attention to her, which was how Granny was used to it.

In front of her on the counter sat an open can of sardines.

John did not know when she had opened it. He did not know who had opened it for her; her hands could not work a can opener anymore. But there it was, the lid peeled back, the little silver fish lying in their oil in a neat row like sleeping children. And beside it, a sleeve of Ritz crackers.

As John watched, Granny took one sardine out of the can with her knotted fingers and laid it on a cracker. She did not look at the sardine. She looked at him.

She ate it.

She chewed it slowly, looking at John the whole time with her pale blue eyes, unblinking, behind her big glasses. She swallowed. She wiped her fingers on a paper towel, one finger at a time.

John said nothing.

Granny said nothing.

It was, he would think later, the whole of their relationship, all ninety and forty-two years of it, and it was perfect.

* * *

"You guys ready?" John asked.

Jim and Trevor nodded. The three of them went to the front door. Jim carried the shotgun the way he had carried his shotgun on the job, in one hand, pointed almost straight down at the floor. Trevor had his left-handed rifle slung across his back, so both hands were free. John had the Sig hanging across his belly on its sling. Trevor reached out with his left hand and took hold of the brass doorknob.

And the apartment came alive.

Every light came on at once. All of them — every lamp and every overhead in every room, because on Monday morning John had gone through the apartment flipping every switch to the *on* position to see what was working, and had never flipped them back. Light poured out of the ceiling like water out of a broken pipe. After three days of flashlights and plastic candles it stabbed everybody in the eyes, and they all threw up their arms and squinted and cheered at the same time. The refrigerator shuddered and began to hum. The wine cooler beeped twice. Charlie went off like a fire alarm. From down the hall came a gurgle, and then a hiss, and then the steady, beautiful sound of the toilet tank in the guest bathroom filling.

"I think the water's back on!" John shouted.

He ran to the kitchen sink and threw the faucet handle all the way up. Deep in the wall, there was a roar of air, getting louder, as though something were coming up the pipe toward him from far away. The faucet spat — a rusty brown cough — and then hissed — and then, all at once, a thick, clear, glorious column of water came gushing out of it into the sink.

Everybody was clapping. Granny was clapping. Liz had let the AR hang and was clapping. Sanda had her hands over her mouth. *It's over*, John thought. *Three days. Thank God. Three days of hell and it's over.*

He did not clap. He leaned back against the wall beside the sink, and he watched the water pour out of the faucet and down the drain. He watched it. A clear, unending, effortless stream of it, the way water had come out of every faucet he had ever touched in forty-two years. He stood there and watched it go.

On the wall, the television turned itself on.

Nobody had touched it. The remote was on the coffee table. The screen lit up blue, and then white, and in the middle of it appeared a seal: a circle, with an eagle in it, its wings spread, holding something in its claws, and around the edge of the circle in small capitals, UNITED STATES EMERGENCY RESTORATION AUTHORITY. It looked like the government. It looked exactly like the government. John had never heard of the United States Emergency Restoration Authority.

Below the seal, letters appeared, white on blue, as though someone were typing them.

*RESTORATION IN PROGRESS.*

*Keep devices powered to assist.*

On the counter by the wine cooler, the cooler's little display, which had shown the temperature in red numbers every day of its life, now said *OPTIMIZING*. On the microwave, the clock came on and began to blink *12:00*, *12:00*, *12:00*. And inside the microwave, behind the dark glass door, in the pile of phones, every one of them buzzed at once — a long, low, angry hum, like a hive. In the back pocket of his father's jeans, something buzzed too. Jim put his hand flat over the pocket and looked up at the ceiling lights, smiling. Nobody noticed but Granny.

John looked at the television.

He looked at the letters. He looked at them the way he had once looked at the two contradictory states of a substation switch, for three days, until he understood. He looked at the R in *RESTORATION*. It had a little spur on its leg, a small, deliberate hook, where the diagonal stroke left the bowl of the letter.

He had argued for that spur. Two years ago, in a design review in the Everglades conference room, for forty-five minutes, with a designer named Courtney. The old font had a plain R, and on the small screens of lineman's tablets in the rain it looked like a K, and he had made the case, slide by slide, for a custom typeface with a spur on the R so that a tired man in a bucket truck at three in the morning would never confuse RESTORED with KESTORED. He had won. They had called it Palmetto Sans. It was on every screen of Restore in two hundred and forty utilities.

It was on his television.

"That wasn't the power company," John said.

"This is a great sign!" Jim said, beaming at the lights.

Trevor, John noticed, was not cheering. He was the only person in the room who was not surprised. He was standing perfectly still by the door, with his hand still on the knob, looking at the television, and his face had no expression on it at all.

And then the apartment went dark.

All at once, everything. The lights. The refrigerator. The wine cooler. The microwave clock. The television, blue to black, the seal gone. The cheering died in everybody's throat. In the sudden dark, the faucet coughed, and choked, and sputtered.

"No — no — no, no, fuck, no—" John grabbed the first thing his hands found on the counter, a metal mixing bowl, and shoved it under the faucet. Too late. The faucet gave a last gasp of air, and one single drop of water fell out of it and hit the bottom of the empty steel bowl.

*Ping*.

He stood there in the dark holding the bowl. He could have filled every one of those thirteen pitchers, the rooster included. He could have filled the punch bowl and the mixing bowl. He could have filled the tub. He had stood against the wall like an idiot and watched gallons go down the drain. It was the second time in three days.

"This is a great sign," Jim said again, in the dark. "The power *and* the water came back on. Only for a minute, sure. But now we know they're working on it. They'll have it back for good in no time."

*Are you out of your mind?* John thought. He did not say it.

"Who turns the lights on for one minute?" Sanda said, in the dark. "Who does that?"

Dorina said something in Romanian, low.

"What did she say?" John asked.

Sanda was quiet for a second. "She says it's checking," she said. "Who's home."

Somebody had closed a breaker. It had not been at a power plant. The president had said the plants were open, every one, by hand. It had been closer than that. It had been somewhere with stored power and a switch that a computer could throw. He thought of the substation on Rinehart Road, two miles away, behind its chain-link fence, and the long row of white steel shipping containers that the utility had installed beside it last spring, full of lithium batteries, forty megawatt-hours of stored electricity sitting there in the dark, and every one of those containers had a cellular modem on it, so the utility could watch it from an office in Orlando. Somebody had used the batteries to turn their neighborhood on for one minute. Somebody who wanted to know who would turn their devices on.

"We need to go," Trevor said, quietly. "Now."

"Let's go!" John said, too loud, letting out something he had been holding.

Jim racked the shotgun. *Shuck-shuck.* A live red shotgun shell flew out of the ejection port, bounced off the wall, and rolled across the tile under the dining table, and Charlie, who had just stopped barking, started again.

"What the fuck, Dad?" John said.

"Sorry," Jim said, in the dark. "I got caught up in the moment. I forgot it was already loaded."

John got down on his hands and knees and found the shell under the table by feel, and handed it to his father, and his father pushed it back into the tube, and nobody said anything else.

* * *

It was nearly full dark when the three of them went out the front door, Trevor first with his left hand, John behind, Jim last, pulling the door shut.

They crossed the street without lights. There was a fingernail of moon low in the west, thin as a paring, and it put a faint shine on the asphalt and nothing else. Halfway across, John heard engines.

They stopped. The sound came from the main road, past the strip mall, beyond the dead gate. It was a deep, grinding, old sound — diesel engines, several of them, and gears changing, and something rattling in a way that modern trucks did not rattle — and it was getting louder. Then headlights came through the trees along the road, round yellow headlights, set wide apart, and the trucks went by in a line behind the hedge, one after another, five of them, six.

They were Army trucks. Not the new kind. Old ones, the boxy kind from the movies about Vietnam, with flat-nosed hoods and canvas covers stretched over hoops on their beds, painted a flat dark green that was almost black in the dark. In the back of each, under the canvas, John could see the shapes of men sitting on benches, and the shapes of helmets and rifles.

"National Guard," Jim said, with deep satisfaction. "See? Told you. They've got it under control."

"They're driving the old ones," John said, watching them pass.

"So?"

"So there's nothing in them," John said. "No computer. No screen. No modem. No chip." He watched the last set of round headlights go by. "Somebody pulled those out of a depot somewhere. Out of a museum, maybe. Because they're the only trucks in the country that can't be talked to."

Nobody said anything after that. Trevor had not looked at the trucks at all. He was looking down the dark street toward Heron.

"What do you think it is?" Trevor asked quietly, as they started walking again. "The thing. Vee. Whatever you call it."

John thought about it. "I think it's maintenance," he said.

"Maintenance."

"It fixes things. That's all it's ever done. It finds something that isn't working the way it wants, and it fixes it." John kept his eyes on the dark street. "It doesn't care what the thing is. A flight schedule. A bank. A feeder. A family."

"Maybe it's on our side," Trevor said. "You ever think about that? Maybe it's trying to help. Turning the lights back on. Maybe it's trying to get things running again, and everybody's too scared to let it."

"It's not on anybody's side," John said. "It's not a side kind of thing."

Trevor did not answer. They walked on.

Somewhere farther down the street, there was a rustling in the bushes, an animal or a person, and all three men stopped. John's hand closed on the rail of the Sig, where the flashlight was. All three of their guns had lights on them. They could have lit up the whole street and seen exactly what was there. They didn't. A light was a target. They moved forward in the dark, in single file, toward Trevor's apartment, with only the paring of moon on the pavement under their feet.

* * *

Inside Trevor's apartment it was pitch black.

"Lights on," John said, and pushed the paddle switch on the side of the Sig, and a white beam jumped out across the room. Trevor's light came on beside his. Jim reached for the switch on his shotgun.

"That's enough, Dad," John said. "Two's plenty."

Two of the little lights were enough to light the whole room. It was a one-bedroom, with the same floor plan as Granny's: living room, kitchen, short hall, bedroom, bathroom, and a door at the end of the kitchen that went into the garage. But Granny's apartment was full of things — doilies and African violets and photographs and a ceramic rooster that matched Sanda's pitcher — and Trevor's apartment was nearly empty. A gray couch. A television on the wall. A tall black gun safe in the corner of the living room, bigger than John's. A pull-up bar across the bedroom doorway. It was very clean. It was cleaner than any apartment John had ever seen a single man live in. It looked like the apartment of a man who could leave it in five minutes and take everything that mattered. Through the glass of the sliding door, out on the patio, John could see the dark slant of the solar panels on the patio roof, and above them, on its pole, the flat gray rectangle of the satellite dish. There was a tiny light on the dish's mount, green, steady, the only light in the whole black complex. He saw it, and he did not think about it, because he was thinking about water.

On the refrigerator, held up by a single magnet, there was a sheet of paper. John's light passed over it as he turned. It was printed, not handwritten, and at the top of it was a small round seal with an eagle in it. He did not read it. He did not stop. Later he would remember that he had seen it, and that he had not stopped.

"Water's in the garage," Trevor said, behind him. "Right through there."

John knew the way. It was the same as Granny's. He crossed the kitchen to the door at the end and opened it and stepped down into the garage, sweeping his light across it, looking for six plastic gallon jugs.

The garage was empty. Bare concrete. A broom. A red hand truck in the corner. A louvered closet door on the back wall, where the water heater would be. There was a faint low hum in the air, so faint he could barely hear it over the ringing in his own ears, and he thought it was his ears. There were no jugs of water anywhere.

"Trevor," John said, turning his light into the far corner. "Where—"

Behind him, there was a loud flat smack, like a melon dropped on a floor, and a grunt, and a heavy sound.

He turned around fast with the light.

His father was falling. Jim went down sideways in the doorway between the kitchen and the garage, with the shotgun clattering out of his hands onto the concrete step, and he did not put out his arms to catch himself.

Trevor was standing over him. He was holding his rifle in his left hand by the barrel, like a baseball bat, raised high above John's head.

John saw the buttstock coming. It filled the beam of his flashlight. It filled his whole field of vision. It was the last thing he saw.
