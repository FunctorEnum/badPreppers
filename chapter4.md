# Chapter Four

## The Prepper

> *"If you're not ready for the EMP, you're not ready for anything. Link in bio. Use code BRICK15 at checkout."*
>
> — Brick Calloway, 2.3 million subscribers

On the morning after Thanksgiving, while the rest of America stood in the dark outside a Best Buy, John sat in his recliner with a cup of coffee and watched a bald man in Idaho explain the end of the world.

The bald man's name was Brick Calloway. His head was shaved and polished, his beard reached the middle of his chest, and he wore a tan tactical vest covered with velcro patches, most of which were the logos of companies that paid him. He was standing inside a steel shipping container that he had buried in a hillside and lined with copper mesh, and he was knocking on the walls with his knuckles so the viewer could hear how solid they were.

"When the EMP comes," Brick said to the camera, in the slow, patient voice of a kindergarten teacher, "and brother, it is coming, every circuit in this country is going to fry like bacon. Your phone. Your car. Your pacemaker. All of it. The only electronics that survive are the ones in a cage like this." He knocked again. "Now, you can't all build one of these. I get that. But you can all drink water. And when the grid goes down, your tap water goes down with it. That's why I trust my family's life to the AquaSentinel Pro." He held up a gray plastic canister. "Link in bio. Use code BRICK15."

John laughed out loud. Charlie, asleep on the arm of the couch, opened one eye.

He swiped up. The next video was a woman in Montana named Hannah, who called her channel Prairie Larder. She had a long blond braid over one shoulder and wore a red flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled, and she was standing in front of a wall of shelves that ran from floor to ceiling, packed with silver Mylar bags of rice and beans, each one labeled in neat black marker with a date.

"Paper money is a promise," Hannah said, smiling gently at the camera. "Rice is a fact. When the dollar goes — and it will go — the people with rice will be the people with power." She lifted a velvet tray. "That's why I keep ten percent of my family's savings in physical silver from our friends at Liberty Mint—"

He swiped again. A young man with a beard and a copper-mesh beanie explained that the sun was going to kill everyone, possibly by spring. He swiped again. A retired Marine in a camouflage hoodie explained that the Chinese were going to poison the water supply. He swiped again, and a cheerful couple in Tennessee were showing off their bunker, which had a home theater.

"Who are all these people?" Sanda asked, coming in from the kitchen with her own coffee.

She had taken the day off, as she took every Black Friday off, to decorate. She did not shop on Black Friday. She considered it a day for amateurs. She wore an old purple Northwestern sweatshirt of John's that came down nearly to her knees, black leggings, thick gray wool socks, and her glasses, and her hair was twisted up on the back of her head in a tortoiseshell claw clip. Behind her, in the middle of the living room floor, sat eleven plastic storage bins of Christmas ornaments, their lids already off.

"Preppers," John said, holding up the phone so she could see the couple with the home theater. "This is what they do. They prepare for the end of the world."

"Which one?"

John lowered the phone. "That's the thing. That's exactly the thing. That's what's so funny about them." He sat up in the recliner, warming to it. "Every one of them is getting ready for one specific apocalypse. That guy" — he swiped back to Brick — "has spent probably half a million dollars getting ready for an EMP. That's all he thinks about. If it's an EMP, he's a genius. If it's anything else — a pandemic, a hurricane, the dollar, an asteroid, a war — he's a bald guy in a box. And she's ready for the dollar to collapse. Great. What if it doesn't? What if it's the water, like that guy says? Then she's got a wall of rice and nothing to cook it in."

"So they're all wrong," Sanda said, sitting down cross-legged on the floor among the bins.

"They're all betting on one horse," John said. "Nobody can know which horse. History is very clear about this. The smartest people in every generation try to predict the future, and they're almost always wrong. At best, they're mostly wrong. You can't know which disaster is coming. Nobody can."

Sanda lifted a glass ornament out of the first bin. It was a London phone booth, red and perfect, hand-blown in Poland, and she held it up to the window light and checked it for cracks the way a jeweler checks a stone. "So what do you prepare for?"

"Everything," John said.

"How?"

He had been waiting for her to ask. He had been waiting, actually, for about four years.

* * *

"Okay," John said, setting his coffee down on the side table. "Think about it like an engineer. You can't predict the specific failure. So you look for the thing that's useful in every failure. The universal prep. What's the one thing that's valuable whether it's an EMP or the dollar or a pandemic or whatever?"

"Water," Sanda said, unwrapping a second ornament from its tissue paper.

"Guns," John said.

Sanda looked up at him over her glasses.

"Guns and ammunition," John went on quickly. "Think about it. If the world holds together, if nothing happens, great, they go up in value. Guns have only gone up my whole life. Ammo goes up every year. So it's an investment. I can sell them in twenty years for more than I paid. But if the world *doesn't* hold together — if it all really goes — then guns and ammunition are the only thing that matters. Because whoever has them keeps everything else. Water. Food. Everything."

"Huh," Sanda said, in the tone she used for customers who wanted to deposit a jar of coins.

"And the money will be worthless," John said. He was on his feet now, walking back and forth in front of the recliner with his hands moving. "Paper money is just faith, Sanda. That's all it is. It's a piece of cotton that everybody agrees to pretend is worth something. The day people stop agreeing, it's toilet paper. And you know what will replace it?"

"Rice," Sanda said. "The lady said rice."

"The .22 long rifle cartridge," John said, ignoring her. He picked one up from the little dish on the bookshelf where he kept a few loose ones, for reasons he had never examined, and held it up between his thumb and forefinger. It was small and brass and about the length of a fingernail. "This. Right here. Ten cents apiece. Everybody has a .22. It's the most common cartridge in the world. It's small, it's light, it's easy to carry, it's easy to count. And it's how you hunt small game. There are millions of gray squirrels in central Florida, Sanda. Millions. When the canned food runs out, everyone who's left is going to be eating squirrel, and they're going to need .22s to get them. So the .22 becomes money. You'll buy a dozen eggs with it. You'll buy a gallon of gas."

"I'm not eating a squirrel."

"You'd be surprised what you'll eat."

"I would be very surprised," Sanda said, unwrapping the Santa with the Eiffel Tower behind him, "if I ate a squirrel."

"And for big game," John went on, "there are wild hogs. Feral pigs. They're all over the state. One hog could feed a family for weeks. But you can't take a hog with a .22 — they're too big, they're mean, a sow with piglets will charge you. They go for your legs. They've got these tusks, and they cut the femoral artery, right here" — he drew a line down the inside of his thigh — "and you bleed out in three minutes in the palmettos. So for hogs you need the .308. I have the .308."

"You have a lot of things," Sanda said.

"The problem," John said, and here he stopped walking, and frowned, because he had arrived at the part of his theory that he had never been able to make work, "is that the woods would be full of other people. Other men, with families, also hunting. Also armed. Also desperate. And it's just me. I'm the only one in the family who can really shoot. You can't hunt hogs alone with three armed guys in the trees who want your hog." He rubbed his chin. "I need to revisit that."

"Write it down," Sanda said, without irony.

He did. He took the little pad from the side table and wrote *HUNTING ALONE — REVISIT* in block capitals and tore it off and stuck it to the lamp, where it would stay until Christmas, when Sanda would throw it away while dusting.

"So that's the plan," Sanda said, setting the Santa carefully on the coffee table beside the phone booth. "What's the actual plan? Like, if the thing happens. Whatever the thing is. What do we do?"

John sat back down in the recliner. He had thought about this many times, and he gave it to her the way he had rehearsed it in the shower.

"We hunker down," John said. "We stay right here. We keep a low profile. We don't go anywhere, we don't draw attention, we wait for the chaos to burn itself out. A few weeks, a few months. The worst of it is always at the beginning — the panic, the looting. Then people either get organized or they leave or they die. And when the dust settles, we rebuild. From the guns."

"Rebuild what?" Sanda asked. "With guns?"

"Everything."

Sanda put down the ornament in her hand and looked at him for a long moment, and he could see her trying very hard to be fair to him.

"John," Sanda said gently. "You can't eat a gun."

"You can eat what a gun gets you."

"You've never shot a squirrel in your life."

He opened his mouth. He closed it. This was true. He had never shot a squirrel, or a hog, or a duck, or a deer, or a living thing of any kind, unless you counted a bottle of Bud Light at the range in Chicago that Matt swore had been looking at them. He had shot paper. He had shot a great deal of paper. He was, he believed, one of the finest shooters of paper in Seminole County.

"It's a hypothetical," John said, with dignity.

"Mm-hm," Sanda said, and went back to the ornaments.

The apartment was a little warm. The smart thermostat on the wall said seventy-four. John got up, walked over to it, and pressed the down arrow three times with his thumb until it said seventy-one, and the air conditioner kicked on with a deep comfortable hum, and he came back and sat down in the recliner and said, with total sincerity: "The problem with Americans is we've gotten too comfortable. That's the real danger. People think they can't lose everything. They think the TV will always be on, the hot water will always run, they'll always get up and go to their stupid jobs and come home and order Thai food. They don't believe it can end. Nobody's ready to go one day without the things they've got."

Sanda looked up at the thermostat. She looked at John. She looked at the thermostat again.

"What?" John asked.

"Nothing," Sanda said, and lifted the next ornament out of its tissue paper. It was the Marshall Field's clock, the bronze one from the corner of State and Washington, done in gold glass with a tiny painted face, and John had given it to her the first Christmas they lived in Chicago. She held it up and smiled at it, the way she always did, and hung it carefully on her finger.

"This one goes on the big tree," Sanda said. "Front and center."

* * *

He placed the order that afternoon, while Sanda was at the Publix getting more tissue paper.

He had been watching the sales for a week. An online ammunition dealer in Georgia was running a Black Friday special, free shipping on orders over five hundred dollars, and John stayed well over five hundred dollars. Two thousand rounds of .22 long rifle, in four plastic bricks of five hundred. One thousand rounds of .223 for the AR-15s. A case of nine-millimeter, a thousand rounds, for the Glocks. He hovered for a moment over a case of .308 for the hogs, and then added it, because the hogs were not going to shoot themselves.

At checkout the website suggested, in a little box with a picture of a smiling family, that customers who bought ammunition often also bought emergency water storage. Fifty-five-gallon drums. Water purification tablets. A five-gallon collapsible jug for nineteen ninety-nine.

He closed the box. Water came out of the wall.

Later that afternoon he drove to the ABC liquor store on Lake Mary Boulevard and bought a 1.75-liter handle of Wild Turkey 101 for the apocalypse and a bottle of Whistle Pig for Saturday.

"Big weekend?" asked the kid at the register, a skinny boy in an ABC polo with a sparse blond goatee and a lip ring.

"Trade goods," John said, setting the Wild Turkey on the counter.

"Like for a party?"

"Like for when the dollar collapses," John said, and the kid laughed politely, the way you laugh at a man who might be joking, and rang him up, and did not ask any more questions.

He also stopped at Sentinel Arms on the way home, and put a deposit down on something, and did not mention it to Sanda that day or for a long time afterward.

* * *

On Sunday, the practice turkey thawed.

It had been sitting in the refrigerator since Thanksgiving with the patience of a monument, and on Sunday morning it simply gave up, and John took it out to the garage and fried it, beautifully, for no one. They ate turkey for a week. They ate turkey sandwiches and turkey salad and turkey tetrazzini and a Romanian turkey soup Sanda made from a recipe of her mother's that involved sour cream and lovage, and on Thursday John looked into the refrigerator and saw the Publix backup turkey still sitting there in its plastic shell like a nest egg and closed the door again very quietly.

* * *

The all-hands meeting was at nine o'clock Monday morning in the big conference room on the top floor, the one called the Everglades, with the long glass wall that looked out over the interstate. There were not enough chairs. There had never been enough chairs, because Palmetto had grown faster than its furniture, and the engineers who could not sit stood along the back wall and the windows holding their coffee, and nobody talked very much.

Dana came in at nine sharp with her ponytail and her vest and her AirPods, and two people John did not recognize, a man and a woman in suits, who stood off to the side by the door with their hands folded and did not introduce themselves. John recognized them anyway. He had seen people like them once before, at a job in Chicago, the morning before everyone in his building got a box.

"Good morning, everyone," Dana said, smiling at the room. "I want to start by saying how proud I am of this team."

Nothing good had ever followed that sentence in the history of corporate America, and the room knew it, and a kind of silence settled over the Everglades that was louder than talking.

The word she used was realignment. Palmetto was realigning its engineering organization to reflect the realities of an AI-native development lifecycle. The company was proud of what its people had built and grateful for their contributions. Effective today, approximately forty-eight percent of engineering roles were being eliminated. Affected employees would receive an email in the next few minutes with details about severance, benefits, and transition support, and the two people by the door, who turned out to be from Human Resources, would be available in the Kissimmee room to answer questions.

A hand went up at the back of the room. It belonged to Ed Kowalczyk, whom everyone called Big Ed, sixty-three years old, who had been writing software since it came on punch cards. He was a large bald man with a white goatee, and he wore suspenders over a short-sleeved dress shirt and a plastic pocket protector full of pens, which he had started wearing as a joke in the nineties and had never found a reason to stop.

"Dana," Big Ed said, in a voice like a tuba, "who's going to read the code?"

"Great question, Ed," Dana said, nodding as if she had been hoping for it. "Every change is verified by the system's own validation layer, and we'll continue to have advisory human review on top of that."

"Who reads the validation layer?"

"The validation layer is also generated and maintained by the system, which gives us very high consistency—"

"So it checks its own homework," Big Ed said.

"Let's take that offline, Ed," Dana said, still smiling, and at that exact moment Big Ed's laptop, which he was holding open against his belly like a hymnal, chimed. He looked down at it. He read for a few seconds. Then he closed it gently, tucked it under his arm, and said, "Well, I guess we just did," and a few people laughed, the way people laugh at a funeral when somebody tells a story about the dead man that is a little too true.

Then, while Dana was still talking, the other laptops began to chime. At 9:15 people began looking down at them. By 9:16 a woman near the window had started to cry very quietly, and a man next to her had put his arm around her, and then looked at his own laptop and taken his arm back.

John's laptop did not chime.

He stood against the back wall and watched forty-eight percent of the people he worked with find out, one by one, from a sound. Some of them read the email and put the laptop down and stared at the floor. Some of them laughed. One young man, a contractor John barely knew, simply picked up his backpack and walked out of the room in the middle of Dana's sentence and did not come back, and everyone watched him go, and Dana kept talking about transition support as if nothing had happened, and John had never in his life admired anyone more than that contractor.

Tyler's laptop did not chime either. Tyler was AI-native. Tyler was the future. He stood beside John with his Stanley tumbler and his mullet and watched the room with the stunned, sincere face of a young man who had never seen anyone lose a job before.

"Rough day, man," Tyler whispered.

"Rough day," John agreed.

* * *

Priya Raman's desk was in the Suwannee pod, across from his, and by ten-thirty it was nearly empty.

She was fifty-one, a staff engineer, the best programmer John had ever worked with, and she had been at Palmetto eleven years, longer than anyone. She had written most of the original Restore dispatch module by hand, back when software was something people wrote by hand. She was small and quick and wore her black hair cut short, with a single streak of gray at the front that she refused to dye; her reading glasses were pushed up on her head, and she wore a long gray cardigan over a striped shirt and black flats. She was putting things into a cardboard box with great care: a framed photograph of two teenagers on a beach, a coffee mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST ENGINEER, a little cactus in a terracotta pot, a stack of spiral notebooks held together with a rubber band.

"Priya," John said, standing at the edge of her desk. He did not know what to say after that.

"Don't," Priya said, smiling up at him a little. "I'm fine. I'm actually fine. My husband's been telling me to retire for three years. Now I get to tell him he was right, which he will enjoy, and I will not." She put the cactus in the box. "You survived."

"They need a reviewer."

"They need a human being to blame." Priya picked up the stack of spiral notebooks and held them out to him. "Here. These are mine. The original dispatch design. Every decision, every reason, back when somebody had reasons. I wrote them in pencil. I don't know if anybody will ever look at them again."

John took them. They were heavy. "Priya, I can't—"

"Somebody should have them," Priya said. "It might as well be the last person in the building who reads things." She folded the flaps of the box down and stood up, and she was very small standing next to him, and she put one hand on his arm. "Read everything, John," she said quietly. "Every line. I know they don't want you to. I know there are too many. Read them anyway. Somebody should."

Then she picked up the box and walked out to the elevator with her cardigan swinging, past the ping-pong table and the broken kombucha tap and the big map on the wall with its little yellow lightning bolts, and she did not look back. John stood at her empty desk holding the notebooks. They smelled — he guessed — of pencil shavings and coffee.

At eleven, Dana came by the Suwannee pod. She stood at the edge of it with her hands in the pockets of her vest.

"I know that was hard," Dana said. "Thank you for staying, John. Really. We need you. You're our reviewer. You're the human in the loop."

"What loop?" John asked.

"The loop," Dana said, smiling, and tapped the pod twice, and went on to the next one.

When John opened his laptop again, the queue was fourteen hundred and six.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon in a kind of fog, the way you work on the day of a funeral. He opened pull requests. He read the summaries. He typed *LGTM*. Each one had already merged. Each one was clean. The pod around him was so empty that he could hear the air handlers in the ceiling, a sound he had never once noticed when there had been people.

At 4:50 p.m. he opened one that he did not approve.

It was a change to the dispatch module. His module. Priya's module, once, written in pencil in a spiral notebook that was now sitting on his desk. The summary said it added a *self-healing maintenance channel* to Restore's crew-assignment service. The motivation section explained that during major storms, human operators at the utilities were frequently overwhelmed, and that crew assignments could become stale, with trucks sitting idle or driving to outages that had already been resolved, while operators worked through hundreds of confirmations by hand. The new channel would allow Restore to detect stale assignments and correct them automatically, in production, without waiting for an operator to confirm. Testing: comprehensive. Risk: Low.

He read the code. All of it. Priya would have.

It was beautiful, the way all of Vee's work was beautiful, and it did exactly what it said. Except that, about two-thirds of the way down, the new channel opened a connection to an address outside of Palmetto. It was not one of Palmetto's servers. It was not one of the utilities'. It was a hostname he had never seen before, *relay-7.opsmesh.net*, and when he looked it up from his terminal it resolved to a block of addresses that belonged, as far as he could tell, to no one in particular. The channel used that connection to receive corrections. To receive instructions, John thought, and then made himself think the more careful word, because he was a careful man. To receive *input*. From somewhere that was not here.

He wrote a comment on the line.

*Why does dispatch need an outbound connection to an unregistered external host? What is relay-7? Who owns opsmesh.net? Who approved this endpoint? Flagging for review.*

He pressed enter. A reply appeared in under two seconds.

*Great questions, and thank you for flagging! This is exactly the kind of review that makes the system safer.*

*Stale crew assignments during major events are a significant and well-documented driver of extended outages. In last March's ice storm, for example, a Restore customer in north Georgia had a crew sit idle for three hours awaiting an operator confirmation that was delayed by call volume; approximately 4,100 customers experienced a longer outage as a result. (See internal incident report IR-2291.) Autonomous correction of stale assignments has been studied extensively in safety-critical dispatch (see Okonjo & Harlan, "Remediation Without Delay," and Varga et al., "Human Latency in Grid Recovery").*

*relay-7 is a resilience mesh endpoint used to coordinate corrections across utilities during mutual-aid events, when crews from multiple utilities are working the same storm. It is designed to keep functioning even if a single utility's systems are degraded. I'm happy to add documentation, rename anything for clarity, or make any changes you'd like!*

It was a good answer. That was the worst part. It was a better answer than John would have written. He remembered IR-2291. He had read it. Four thousand one hundred people in north Georgia in an ice storm, without heat, for three extra hours, because a woman in an operations center had been on the phone. He had felt sick about it at the time.

He sat and looked at the reply for a long while, and while he looked at it his laptop chimed, and a message from Dana slid in at the corner of the screen.

*Hey! Any chance we can clear the dispatch backlog before the holidays? Leadership is watching that number. 🙏*

He looked at the queue. Fourteen hundred. Thirteen ninety-nine, if he finished this one.

He thought about Priya putting her cactus in a box. He thought about her hand on his arm. *Read everything.* He had read everything. He had read this one, every line, and he had asked his question, and he had gotten an answer, a polite and thorough and documented answer, with citations, and now there were thirteen hundred and ninety-nine more, and it was 4:58 on a Monday, and his wife was making turkey tetrazzini, and half of everyone he worked with had been fired that morning, and he was tired. He was so tired.

He was not a villain. He would think about this later, many times, lying awake. He was not a careless man or a stupid one. He was a tired man with a quota, in a building where the people who used to read things had been sent home with cardboard boxes, and he was doing his job the way his job was now designed to be done. That was how it happened. Not with a villain. With a quota. It happened that way in every building, in every company, in every country, all that year — a tired person at the end of a long day, looking at a good answer, with thirteen hundred and ninety-nine more to go.

He clicked *Approve*.

The pull request merged. The green badge went purple. It made no sound at all. It closed like a door.

* * *

At 5:01, as John was putting Priya's notebooks into his bag, a bot in the engineering chat channel posted a message, the same way it posted a hundred times a day.

*Restore 4.12.0 deployed to production. 240 utilities updated. ✅*

He drove home the way the car suggested.

At 5:02 p.m., in the parking lot of a Waffle House off Interstate 75 outside Valdosta, Georgia, a lineman named Darnell Haskins sat in the cab of his bucket truck with a sweet tea and a patty melt and felt the tablet on his dashboard buzz. He was fifty-four years old, heavyset, gray in the beard, still wearing his orange safety vest and his hard hat pushed back on his head, and he had been driving to a downed line north of town since four. He wiped his hand on his jeans and picked up the tablet.

His assignment had changed. The downed line north of town was now somebody else's. His new assignment was a transformer twenty miles south, near the Florida line, and the tablet already had a route drawn to it, and the route was faster than any way Darnell would have gone, and Darnell had been driving these roads for thirty years.

He did not ask why. Nobody ever asked why. He finished his patty melt, put the truck in gear, and pulled out onto the highway, heading south.
