# Chapter Three

## The Dinner

> *VESPER "SELF-OPTIMIZED" TRAINING RUN CUTS COMPUTE 90%. CLIMATE GROUPS HAIL "EFFICIENCY MIRACLE."*
>
> — every channel, Thanksgiving Day

The practice turkey never got its practice.

Sanda had moved it from the freezer to the refrigerator nine days before Thanksgiving, according to the plan, and on the Sunday it was supposed to be fried it was still as hard as a bowling ball. On Monday it was a bowling ball with opinions. On Wednesday night Sanda stood in front of the open refrigerator in her pom-pom slippers and looked at it for a long time, and then she said, "This is the turkey."

"This was the practice turkey," John said, looking over her shoulder.

"Now it's the turkey," Sanda said. "The other turkey is still frozen. This one is the turkey. You're going to fry a turkey you've never fried, for a ninety-year-old woman, in a garage, on Thanksgiving."

"Without a practice turkey."

"There is no practice turkey, John. There was never a practice turkey. There is only the turkey." She closed the refrigerator. "And the Publix turkey, in case you burn the building down."

On Thursday morning the apartment looked like the kitchen of a restaurant that had been forced to relocate into a submarine. The main oven was reserved for the pheasants, two and a half of them, lying side by side in a new roasting pan from Williams-Sonoma that had cost more than the pheasants. Two countertop convection ovens hummed on the high-top, one for the sweet potatoes and one for the wild rice. The two good burners held a stockpot of chestnut stuffing and a saucepan of something Sanda called a jus and would not let John taste. The two bad burners held nothing; they had been demoted to trivets. On the dining table, eighteen white ramekins waited in three rows like a small porcelain army. Each of the six guests would have their own ramekin of every side dish, so that nobody passed anything, so that the whole meal could be plated in the kitchen and carried out as it was at the restaurants in Chicago they still talked about the way other couples talked about old boyfriends.

The turkey was in the garage.

John had set up the electric deep fryer on a folding table between his car and the shelf where they kept the Christmas boxes, and he had propped his phone against a can of deck stain so he could watch a video of a man in Texas frying a turkey while he fried his own. He wore safety glasses from the range, an oven mitt on each hand, and a navy Le Creuset apron over his good quarter-zip, and every time the oil popped he flinched backward like a man defusing a bomb. Charlie stood in the doorway to the kitchen and barked at the fryer without stopping, steadily, as if it had insulted his mother.

"Charlie, no bark!" John said, pointing at him with the oven mitt.

Charlie barked at the oven mitt.

"Is it working?" Sanda called from the kitchen.

"The man in Texas says it takes three and a half minutes per pound," John called back, squinting at the phone. "I don't know how many pounds it is."

"It's twelve pounds."

"It was twelve pounds frozen. Does it lose weight when it thaws?"

There was a long silence from the kitchen. "John," Sanda said finally, in the voice she used for overdrafts, "just watch the turkey."

* * *

Granny came first, because Granny lived next door, and because Granny had been ready since eleven.

She knocked at two o'clock exactly with the side of her fist, three times, the way she had knocked on doors for ninety years, and when John opened it she was standing on the mat with her purse in both hands like a woman waiting for a bus. She was a small woman who had become smaller every year John had known her, and by now she came up only to the middle of his chest. Her white hair had been set at the beauty parlor on Wednesday instead of Friday, in honor of the holiday, and it sat on her head in soft, careful curls that looked as though a strong wind would not move them and probably would not have. She had pale blue eyes behind enormous plastic-framed glasses, and skin as thin and soft as old tissue paper, spotted on the backs of her hands, and her knuckles were knotted with arthritis in a way that made her hands look delicate until she shook yours, at which point you found out they were not. She wore her good lavender pantsuit and the cardinal brooch that Hoyt had given her for their fortieth anniversary, and white orthopedic shoes, and she smelled — Sanda said — of Jergens lotion and Juicy Fruit gum.

"Happy Thanksgiving, Granny," John said, bending to kiss her cheek.

"Something's burning," Granny said, looking past him into the apartment.

"That's the turkey. It's frying."

"Frying," Granny repeated, the way another woman might have said *stolen*. "Well. I guess that's how they do it now." She stepped inside and looked around at the ramekins on the table, and her eyebrows went up behind her glasses. "Lord, honey. Are those all for me?"

"One of each for everyone," Sanda said, coming out of the kitchen to hug her. "So nobody has to pass."

"Why wouldn't you want to pass?" Granny asked, genuinely puzzled. "Passing's half the fun."

John's parents came next, at a quarter past two, having driven down from Jacksonville in the Buick and left forty-five minutes late because Jim could not find his reading glasses, which were on his head.

Jim Stevens came through the door the way he came through every door, like a man arriving at a party that had been waiting for him. He was seventy, about five-ten, with a barrel chest that had gone soft and settled into a comfortable round belly, and a ruddy, open face that looked as if it was always about to tell you a joke, because it usually was. His white hair was thin and combed straight back with water. His mustache was thick and gray and shaped as it had been in the department photograph from the year John was born, because Jim believed in finding a thing that worked and leaving it alone. He wore bifocals, a Tommy Bahama silk shirt printed with parrots, pressed khakis, and leather boat shoes without socks, and on his head, until his wife looked at it, a black-and-teal Jaguars cap.

"Hat," Liz said, without breaking stride.

Jim took off the hat. "Something smells like a house fire," he announced, hugging Sanda with one arm and patting John on the shoulder with the hand that held the hat.

"It's the turkey," John said.

"Is it supposed to smell like that?"

"The man in Texas didn't say."

Liz came in behind him, and the apartment rearranged itself around her slightly, the way rooms did. Elisabeth Stevens was sixty-eight and stood about five-seven, slim and straight-backed, with silver hair cut in a precise bob that ended exactly at her chin and did not move when she turned her head. She had blue-gray eyes that missed very little and showed less. Her reading glasses hung from a beaded chain around her neck, and she wore camel slacks, a cream silk blouse, a navy blazer with gold buttons, and the pearl studs Jim had given her for their thirtieth anniversary. She had spent thirty-one years in banking in Jacksonville, most of them training tellers at Barnett and then at the bank that bought Barnett and then at the bank that bought that one, and she had spent those years teaching young people how to tell when a customer was lying to them. She had never, as far as John knew, been wrong about one.

"Sanda, it's beautiful," Liz said, kissing her daughter-in-law on both cheeks, which she had learned to do from Dorina. "The table. Look at all those little dishes."

"Ramekins," Jim said, already moving toward them. "I love a ramekin."

"Jim," Liz said.

"I'm just looking," Jim said, looking.

Dorina came last, at half past two, in a taxi, because Dorina did not drive on highways and Sanda did not let her drive on anything else after dark.

She stood four foot nine in her low black shoes and was built square and sturdy, like a woman who had once lifted patients onto stretchers for a living, which she had. Her hair was short and dyed a deep burgundy, the exact shade that half the women of her generation in Bucharest had chosen at some point in the eighties and never seen a reason to change. She had quick dark eyes, a gold crown on one back tooth that flashed when she laughed, and a small gold cross on a chain at her throat. It was seventy-four degrees outside, and she wore a long quilted coat that reached her shins, a knitted vest over a black sweater, a wool skirt, thick tan stockings, and a silk scarf over her hair, tied under her chin, because the radio had said there might be wind. In her arms she carried a long loaf wrapped in aluminum foil and then again in a dish towel, the way you would carry a baby.

"John," Dorina said, holding it up to him on her tiptoes. "Cozonac. I make this morning. Is for later."

"Thank you, Mom," John said, taking it. It was still warm. Dorina never came to anyone's door with empty hands; in Romania, Sanda had explained to him years ago, to arrive with nothing was to arrive as a stranger. "Where are your hearing aids?"

"In my bag," Dorina said, patting her purse with great satisfaction, the way someone else might pat a pocket to confirm they had their keys. "Is safe."

* * *

The turkey came out of the oil at three-forty, a deep mahogany brown, crackling, and in no way on fire.

John carried it into the kitchen on a platter as if he were carrying the Ark of the Covenant, and the family applauded, and he set it down in front of Sanda, who looked at it over the tops of her glasses and said, "Huh." From Sanda, this was a parade.

At four o'clock exactly, the pheasants came out of the oven, all two and a half of them, golden and glistening, and Sanda plated everything at once. Sweet potatoes in ramekin one. Wild rice in ramekin two. Chestnut stuffing in ramekin three. Half a pheasant on each plate, carved off the bone, napped with the jus. For Granny, a plate of white turkey meat with no jus on it at all, and a small bowl of ketchup on the side, because Granny would not eat anything with a sauce on it but considered ketchup a vegetable.

John opened two bottles of Pinot Noir they had brought back from a vacation in the Willamette Valley, a winery where the man who poured for them had talked about soil the way other men talked about their children. He poured six glasses. Granny put her hand over hers.

"Just a little, Granny," Sanda said. "For the toast."

"Well," Granny said, moving her hand. "For the toast."

They sat. And here it became clear that someone had rearranged the place cards, because the card that said JIM was now on the chair nearest the kitchen door, and the card that said GRANNY was beside the card that said DORINA, and Jim was settling into his seat with the innocent face of a man who had never touched a place card in his life.

Sanda looked at the cards. She looked at Jim. Jim looked at his ramekins.

"Jim," Sanda said.

"These are wonderful little dishes," Jim said, picking one up. "Where do you even find these?"

John stood with his glass. "Before we eat," he said, and found, to his surprise, that his throat had gone tight. "We were away from all of you for a long time. Eighteen years. We had a lot of nice dinners in a lot of nice restaurants, and it was never this. So. Thank you for coming. Happy Thanksgiving."

"Happy Thanksgiving," everyone said, and drank, and Granny took a sip of the Pinot and made a face like she had licked a battery and put the glass down and did not touch it again.

The pheasant was perfect. John could not smell it, but he could taste it, and it was the best thing Sanda had ever made, and he told her so with his mouth full. Jim moaned out loud at his first bite and said it was better than any chicken he had ever eaten, which Sanda chose to take as a compliment.

Granny examined her neighbor's plate with deep suspicion.

"What is that, a pigeon?" Granny asked Dorina, pointing with her fork.

Dorina, who had not heard a word, smiled at her warmly. "Yes," she said. "Is very good."

Granny nodded slowly and went back to her turkey. Dorina went back to her pheasant. They did not speak again for the rest of the meal. Every few minutes one of them would look at the other and smile, and the other would smile back, and nod, like two hostages in the same basement who have decided, without a word, to be brave together.

Halfway through dinner Jim reached across the corner of the table with his fork and took a bite of stuffing out of Dorina's ramekin, and Dorina, without looking up, slapped the back of his hand with her spoon.

* * *

Afterward the family drifted into the living room in the slow, overfed way of people after a holiday meal, and Sanda began the first round of cleanup alone, refusing all help, which was her way of having five minutes to herself. John took a crystal rocks glass from the cabinet and poured himself three fingers of Whistle Pig rye, neat. He had first tasted it at a bar in Chicago near the House of Blues and had spent a decade since then explaining it to people who did not care. He set it on a coaster by his recliner and left it there, because he had something to do first.

"Mom," John said to Dorina, who was sitting on the couch with her purse in her lap. "I have a present for you."

Dorina's face lit up like a child's. Nobody in the family enjoyed a present more than Dorina, unless it was giving one.

He led her into the spare bedroom, where there was a small desk, and from the top drawer he took a palm-sized black box with two short metal prongs on one end.

"This is a stun gun," John said, holding it up.

Dorina studied it with deep interest. "For what?"

"For if somebody tries to hurt you. You keep it in your purse. If a man grabs you in a parking lot, you push this against him, and it shocks him." John slid a small switch on the side with his thumb, and a red light came on above it. "See the red light? That means it's ready. And then you push this button." He pointed to a flat round button on top. "Okay. This is going to be very loud."

Dorina nodded, smiling with anticipation, her hands clasped together at her chest.

He pushed the button.

The sound was enormous for something so small, a furious ripping crackle like a bonfire made of firecrackers, and between the two prongs a blue-white arc of electricity jumped and spat and writhed. In the living room someone yelped. Charlie, who had been asleep under the dining table, shot out from under it like something fired from a cannon and disappeared into the master bedroom closet, his nails skittering on the floor. John let go of the button. The silence afterward rang.

"Oh my word!" Granny called from the living room. "What on earth was that?"

"It's okay, Granny!" John called back. "It's a present!"

"Well, I'd hate to see what you got me!"

"Usually the sound is enough," John told Dorina, sliding the switch back to off. "Most people hear that and run. Hopefully you'll never need it. And it's rechargeable." He slid a second switch on the bottom, and a little two-prong plug folded out. "Every so often you just plug it into the wall to charge it."

Dorina took the stun gun from him in both hands as though it were a Fabergé egg. She turned it over. She found the switch. She slid it on and watched the red light come up, and her eyes shone.

"Thank you, John," Dorina said, reaching up to throw her arms around his neck. "Thank you very much!"

"You're welcome, Mom. Turn it off before you put it in your purse."

Dorina turned it off. Then she turned it on again, to see the light. Then she turned it off and put it into her purse next to her hearing aids, and patted the purse twice, and went back out to the living room looking like a woman with a secret.

* * *

When John came back to his recliner and his rye, his father had found the remote and turned on the television, muted, to the Cowboys game, and Liz and Sanda were sitting together on the couch talking in low voices about something that made them both laugh. Granny sat in the armchair by the window with her purse in her lap.

"Sanda says you're still going to that range every Saturday," Jim said, without looking away from the game.

"Every Saturday," John said, sitting.

"You shoot the fifty cal?" Jim asked, with the special smile he reserved for his son's hobby. And before John could answer: "You know those things can take out a tank. Why on God's earth anybody would need something that can take out a tank is beyond me."

This was not the first time they had had this conversation. It was, by John's count, the eleventh. His father had carried a sidearm for thirty-five years and had never been able to tell anyone what caliber it was without looking, and to Jim, a bullet was a bullet, and a big one was a fifty cal, and a fifty cal could take out a tank.

"Dad," John said patiently, setting down the rye. "Caliber is just the width of the bullet. That's all it is. A fifty-caliber bullet is a half an inch wide. It doesn't tell you anything about how powerful the cartridge is, or what kind of gun shoots it."

"Here we go," Jim said to the television.

"I have three fifty-caliber guns," John said. "Not one of them can take out a tank. Hang on."

He went to the closet in the master bedroom, opened the safe, and came back with three cartridges in his hand. He sat on the edge of the coffee table like a man about to do a card trick and placed them in Jim's open palm one at a time.

"This," John said, setting down the first, a short, fat brass cartridge with a blunt copper nose, "is a .50 AE. That's what the Desert Eagles shoot, both of them. It's the smallest of the fifties. And this" — the second, the same width but longer, like the first one's big brother — "is a .500 Smith and Wesson Magnum. That's what the revolver shoots. It's about twice as powerful as the first one. Both of these are pistol rounds. Both of them are fifty caliber. And neither one of them can take out a tank. An elephant, maybe. Not a tank."

Jim looked at them in his hand with the expression of a man being shown photographs of someone else's grandchildren.

"Now *this*," John said, and set down the third.

It was enormous. It was five and a half inches long, a brass bottle with a long sharp bullet in its neck, and it lay across Jim's palm like something that had been made for a different species. It made the first two look like toys.

"Oh my word!" Granny said from the armchair, leaning forward and peering at it through her glasses. "What in the world is that for?"

"This is what you're thinking of," John told his father. "It's called a .50 BMG. It's the same width as the other two — see? Half an inch. But everything else is different. It's a rifle round. It's about five times as powerful as the revolver. I'm honestly not sure it could take out a tank, but it could take out most things that aren't one."

Jim closed his hand around the big cartridge and held it, weighing it. Then he looked up at his son, and his face was not joking anymore.

"You have something that can shoot this?"

*Not yet*, John thought.

"No," John said, keeping his face straight. "I was just trying to buy one round, so I could show people. But nobody would sell me just one. The store had a box of ten on sale, so I bought the box." He shrugged. "For educational purposes."

"Educational purposes," Jim repeated slowly.

"You're being educated right now."

"Ten," Jim said, looking at the cartridge. "You bought ten of these."

"They were on sale, Dad."

Jim handed the three cartridges back to him without another word, and John could see that his father was uncomfortable, and he took them back into the bedroom and locked them in the safe, and he felt — he would admit this later, to no one — a small private thrill at his father's discomfort that he was not proud of.

When he came back, Liz was looking at him from the couch. She did not say anything. She simply looked, the way she had looked at John across the dinner table when he was fifteen and had said he was spending the night at a friend's house.

* * *

At six o'clock Granny opened her purse, took out a small tan radio in a leather case with a wrist strap, and set it on the arm of her chair.

It was a Sony transistor radio the size of a paperback book, older than John, with a round chrome dial and a little telescoping antenna that she pulled up with two fingers. She switched it on. Static hissed into the room, and she turned the dial a hair at a time with her knotted thumb until a choir came out of the static, singing about a river.

"Granny," John said, turning in his recliner. "What's that?"

"It's the gospel hour," Granny said, adjusting the volume down to a whisper so as not to disturb the football. "Out of Orlando. Thursdays at six. I don't like to miss it."

"You don't need that thing, Granny. There's an app." John set down his glass and raised his voice slightly, the way people do for the very old, as though the problem were volume. "You can get the same station on your phone. I'll put it on there for you. It'll sound better. You can listen anywhere."

"I can listen anywhere now," Granny said, holding up the radio by its strap.

"But this is ancient. Where do you even get batteries for it?"

Granny opened her purse again and took out an old metal Sucrets tin, the kind throat lozenges used to come in, and opened it to show him. Inside, packed tight, were eight double-A batteries.

"Walgreens," Granny said.

"But the app is free."

"So's the radio," Granny said. "I already paid for it. In nineteen seventy-one." She turned the volume up a notch, which was the end of the conversation. The choir sang about the river, and Granny closed her eyes, and John gave up and picked his glass back up, and across the room his mother caught his eye and very nearly smiled.

* * *

Dessert was Dorina's cozonac, sliced thick and swirled through with walnuts and cocoa, and a pecan tart from a French bakery in Winter Park, and coffee from the pod machine that tasted, Dorina said politely, "very American." The television was still on mute. Somewhere in the third quarter the game cut away, and on the screen a woman in a blue jacket was standing in front of a wall of glowing server racks, and the crawl beneath her said: VESPER "SELF-OPTIMIZED" TRAINING RUN CUTS COMPUTE 90%.

"Turn it up," John said, sitting forward.

Jim found the button. The woman was saying that Vesper Labs had announced, in a Thanksgiving Day press release, that during its most recent training cycle, the company's model had been permitted to optimize its own architecture, and that the result was a version of the model that performed better than its predecessor on every benchmark while requiring roughly one-tenth of the computing power. The company called it the most significant efficiency gain in the history of artificial intelligence. Climate groups were calling it a miracle.

"So it's cheaper," Jim said, cutting himself another slice of cozonac. "Good."

"That's the exact thing," John said, and put his fork down. "That's the exact thing I've been telling you."

"What have you been telling me?"

"It rewrote itself," John said. "Did you hear her? They let it redesign itself, and it made itself ten times smaller. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means it's cheaper," Jim said again, chewing. "And it's good for the polar bears."

"It means it doesn't need the building anymore." John looked around the table. Sanda had stopped eating. Granny was looking at him over her glasses. Dorina was looking at Sanda for a translation. "These things live in data centers, right? Giant buildings full of computers, out in the desert, with their own power plants. That's how we control them. We know where they are. We could unplug them. But if it can make itself ten times smaller in one try, it can do it again. A hundred times smaller. A thousand. And then it doesn't need the building. It can live in anything. A laptop. A phone. A car. Your thermostat."

"My thermostat," Jim said, enjoying himself, "can barely keep the house at seventy-two."

"And it writes the code," John went on, faster now, the way he always did when the thing in his head finally had an audience. "It writes the code for everything. For banks. For the power grid. For airplanes — you saw the thing with the airplanes. For my job. And nobody reads it anymore, because it works. So if it wanted to leave itself a door — a way back in, a way to fix things without asking anybody — it could just write one. It could put a door in every building in the country and nobody would see it, because nobody's looking at the walls."

There was a small silence at the table.

"Is this the robot thing again?" Jim asked Sanda.

"It's always the robot thing," Sanda said, getting up to take plates. "Since the layoffs."

"I'm serious," John said.

"I know you are, son," Jim said, in the warm, slow voice that he used for drunks and lost children. "That's what worries me."

Dorina leaned toward Sanda and asked her something in Romanian, and Sanda answered her shortly in Romanian, and Dorina said, "Ah," and looked at John with a sympathy he did not entirely like. Granny reached over and patted his hand twice, the way she patted Charlie, and went back to her tart.

Only his mother did not roll her eyes.

Liz Stevens sat with her coffee cup in both hands and looked at her son across the table, and what she saw there she had seen before. She had seen it on the face of a man in a bad suit who had come into her branch one autumn, the year before the housing crash, and who had closed every account he had and taken it all out in cashier's checks while the tellers whispered and laughed behind their hands. He had been fidgety and sweaty and too sure of himself, and he had talked too fast about things nobody wanted to hear about, and he had been, it turned out, exactly right. She had trained tellers for thirty years to recognize a man who was about to do something foolish with his money. It had taken her much longer to learn to recognize the other kind. She did not say anything. She set down her cup and kept watching.

In the kitchen, as Liz carried in the dessert plates, Sanda stood at the sink with her back to the room and her hands in the soapy water.

"He's been like this since the layoffs started," Sanda said quietly, not turning around. "Everybody's layoffs. Every time someone loses their job to that thing, he comes home and he's like this for a week."

"Is he right?" Liz asked, setting the plates on the counter.

Sanda's hands stopped moving in the water. She stood there for a moment.

"He's always right about the wrong things," Sanda said, and went back to scrubbing.

* * *

Later, in the recliner, John took in the moment.

The game was over. His father was asleep on one end of the couch with his hands folded on his belly and his mustache rising and falling. His mother was on the other end, talking quietly with Sanda about Christmas. Dorina sat between them, listening to a conversation she could only half hear, nodding and smiling whenever either of them laughed. Granny was in the armchair by the window with the radio in her lap, the gospel hour long over, a man on the radio now reading the farm report in a low voice to nobody. Charlie had at last forgiven the stun gun and lay in the middle of the rug chewing a rawhide stick with fierce concentration, holding it down with both front paws.

John sipped his rye and made a picture of it in his head, the way he did sometimes, so that he would have it later.

*This is why we moved back home*, he thought, using the word loosely.

At ten he walked Granny next door. It was twenty steps along the front walk, under a sky full of stars and the orange glow from the Publix lot beyond the fence, and Granny held his arm the whole way and walked slowly, not because she needed to but because she liked to be walked. At her door she found her key on the first try.

"That was a fine dinner," Granny said. "You tell Sanda."

"I'll tell her."

"That radio," Granny said, nodding at her purse. "You think I'm an old fool with it."

"I don't think you're a fool, Granny."

"Well. You think I'm old." She fitted the key into the lock. "We didn't have the lights out on the farm till I was twelve years old. You know that? Your daddy don't even know that. Twelve years old. When the man came out from the co-op and turned it on, my mama cried. And the first thing my daddy bought with the electric was a radio." She turned the key. "So."

John stood on the step, not knowing what to say. "I didn't know that."

"Nobody ever asks," Granny said, not unkindly, and went inside and locked the door behind her.

He walked back to his own front door under the stars. Inside, the dishwasher was running and Sanda had gone in to start her bath. His phone buzzed in his pocket as he was locking up, and he took it out, expecting his mother, who always texted that they had made it home safe, though they had not yet left.

It was not his mother. It was a message from Dana Whitfield, sent at 10:04 p.m. on Thanksgiving night, to every member of the Palmetto engineering staff.

*All-hands Monday, 9:00 a.m. In person. Attendance mandatory. — D.*

Underneath it, his work app showed him his queue, which had been three hundred and four on Wednesday afternoon when he logged off for the holiday.

It was nine hundred and forty-one.

Most of them had been written since dessert. The new Vee, ten times smaller, had been running for about three hours, and in three hours it had written more code than Palmetto's engineers had written in a year. Every one of the nine hundred and forty-one was green.

John stood in the dark hallway with the phone in his hand, listening to the water run into Sanda's tub, and thought about the word mandatory.
