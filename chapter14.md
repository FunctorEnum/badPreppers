## The Third Day

John woke with gray light in the window and his heart pounding, sure that he had overslept, sure that his father and his wife had both fallen asleep on watch and that somebody had walked in through the front door. He grabbed the Sig off the floor and threw the bedroom door open. Behind him, his mother sat straight up in bed and looked around the room as though she had no idea where she was.

Jim was asleep in the recliner with his mouth open, the shotgun across his knees.

Sanda was wide awake on a dining chair beside him, fully dressed, with her olive jacket on, sitting very straight. She had taken the shotgun off Jim's lap at some point and was holding it across her own.

"What's that?" John asked, looking at it.

"What does it look like?"

"You've never shot one of those," John said, too loudly, and his father woke up with a snort.

"A gun is a gun," Sanda said. "I think I can figure it out. You pull the trigger and it goes bang."

"That's true, but that shotgun kicks like nothing you've ever felt. It'll knock you on your ass."

"So it kicks! What the fuck ever. If I have to shoot somebody, I don't think I'm going to care."

"It's too early to fight about this," John said, rubbing his face. "None of us slept. We can't even have coffee."

"Why not?" Jim asked, sitting up, alarmed, as though someone had told him there would be no Christmas.

"We made coffee yesterday in the French press, Dad. Now it's dirty. I'm not going to waste drinking water washing it." John tried to say this in his father's slow, reasonable voice and did not quite manage it.

"Oh, that's not a problem," Sanda said, standing up and handing Jim back his shotgun. "I have another French press. It's clean. I just poured the water out of it yesterday."

"Of course we do," John said.

"You want coffee or not?"

He wanted coffee. He lit the Korean grill and set the kettle on it, with water from a pitcher that had come from the motor home. When the kettle began to whistle, Charlie answered it from behind the guest room door, and the door opened, and Charlie came bursting out wagging his whole back end, and behind him came Dorina and Granny, attempting to have a conversation with each other in two languages that had nothing in common.

"Good morning, everybody," Dorina said, slowly, still half asleep.

Sanda poured a half cup for each of them. Everyone but John and Sanda squeezed in around the counter, reaching past each other for the last packets of sweetener and the last little cups of creamer, and Jim found a bear-shaped bottle of honey in the back of a cabinet and held it up like a trophy. The coffee lifted everybody. Breakfast was bananas and apples and the last of the oranges. Granny ate four grapes.

"I'll be back in a second," Sanda said.

John looked up from his coffee. She had a white Publix bag in her left hand, tied tight at the top, and her jacket was buttoned, and it hung lower on the right side than the left. Shit-bag detail. He tilted his cup up and drank the last of his coffee, which was already lukewarm, and did not think about it again.

* * *

The dumpster was at the end of the street, in a three-sided wooden enclosure at the edge of the parking area, and it was overflowing.

It had not been emptied since Friday. Trash bags were piled up around it on the ground, and the lid would not close, and Sanda could smell it from thirty yards away, though the cold was holding the worst of it back. She knew it would not hold much longer. She walked toward it carrying the bag at arm's length, thinking about nothing, thinking about coffee, thinking about whether her hair would ever stop smelling like the inside of the apartment.

"Hands up in the air!" said a voice behind her. "Now!"

Sanda dropped the bag. It landed almost on her feet. She put her hands up over her head.

"Now turn around," said the voice. It was a woman's voice, deep and cracked. "Slow."

Sanda turned around, slowly, with her hands up.

It was the woman with the pug. Sanda had seen her a hundred times from the window, walking that little dog, smoking her long thin cigarettes. She was in the same pink velour tracksuit she always wore, but it was filthy now, black at both knees, with a gray cardigan over it that was buttoned wrong. Her blond hair was flat and greasy, and the gray roots had grown out another half-inch, and under the Florida tan her face had gone the color of dishwater. She was so thin. Sanda had never noticed how thin she was. Her eyes were sunk back in her head like stones in mud.

She was holding a revolver with both hands, pointed at Sanda's face from six feet away. It was a short, shiny, nickel-plated revolver, very bright in the morning light. Her hands were shaking so hard the barrel made little circles. The hammer was not pulled back. But her finger was inside the trigger guard, flat against the trigger.

A tear ran down her left cheek.

"Ma'am," Sanda said calmly. She used the voice she used at the bank, with the people at the end of the line. "Everything's okay."

"Shut up!" the woman said. "I can't let my baby starve. I'm out of food. I'm all out. There's nothing."

"It's okay. I can help you. We have food. If you just give me the gun, we can—"

"I'm not giving you anything!" the woman shouted, and the barrel jumped. "My baby is starving and you want me to give you my gun? Are you crazy?"

"You're right," Sanda said. "You're totally right. I'm sorry. I just want to help you, that's all. My apartment is right over—"

The sound was the loudest thing she had ever heard.

It hit her like a slap across her whole body, and at the same instant something warm and wet sprayed across the left side of her face and her neck and her shoulder and her arm. Her left eye went red. She could not see out of it. She could see, out of her right eye, only a blur. She was certain, completely certain, that the woman had shot her in the head, and that this was what it felt like, and that in a second she would fall down and be dead.

"Sanda!" a man shouted, from somewhere. "Are you okay?"

She turned toward the voice with her one eye. A tall man was coming toward her quickly across the grass. He was carrying a rifle. He was blurry. He was getting bigger.

"Fucking shit," Sanda said, and dug in her right jacket pocket for the Bodyguard, and her hand would not work.

* * *

John heard the shot inside the apartment. He was out the door before he decided to go, with the Sig up and the red dot on, sweeping it left and right across the street.

He saw Sanda standing by the dumpster. She was covered in blood. The whole left side of her was red. She was swaying.

He saw a tall man with a rifle walking fast toward her across the grass.

John put the red dot in the middle of the man's back, between the shoulder blades, on the spine, and pulled the trigger.

Without earplugs, the Sig sounded like lightning hitting a tree ten feet away. The man went down. He fell forward onto the grass, fifteen feet short of the dumpster, and lay there.

John ran. He could hardly hear his own feet. He could not hear his own voice when he shouted Sanda's name; he could feel it in his throat, but the world had become a single high, enormous ringing tone, as if someone were holding down one key on an organ inside his head. Sanda had sat down on the ground. She was shaking all over. He knelt in front of her and took her glasses off her face and wiped them on his shirt, both sides, until they were mostly clean, and put them back on her, carefully, over her ears.

"Sanda," John said. "Look at me. Are you hurt? Are you shot?"

She looked at him with both eyes. Her mouth moved. He had to read it.

*I don't know.*

His father was coming across the street with the shotgun. John slung the Sig around onto his back and got one arm under his wife's knees and one behind her back and lifted her off the ground. She weighed nothing. She had always weighed nothing.

"Who are they?" Jim said, pointing the shotgun at the two people lying on the ground.

"I don't know," John said, "and I don't care until I know she's not shot."

* * *

He carried her through the front door past a chorus of *Oh my God* and *What happened* and straight down the hall into the master bathroom, and he laid her down in the bathtub.

It was the only place he could think of to put her. It was the only place built to hold water and not be ruined by blood. It was dry. It was completely dry, white and clean and empty, and he set his wife down in it like something precious, and he did not think about why it was empty, not then, not for many hours. He would think about it later.

He pulled her jacket off and her top off and her bra off and looked. Her left arm and shoulder and the left side of her face and neck were dark red and sticky, and there were small pieces of something in her hair. He looked for a hole. He ran his hands over her shoulder, her collarbone, her ribs, her breasts, her arm, front and back.

"Bring me water!" John shouted, forgetting, for one second, that he could not just turn on the shower. "Now!"

Dorina and his mother were already in the doorway, and they ran, and came back with the punch bowl and the big mixing bowl, full of drinking water from the motor home. He took one and poured it slowly over her left arm. She flinched at the cold. The blood ran off her in long pink ribbons into the dry white tub and down toward the drain. He cupped water in his hand and washed her face. Liz tore off paper towels and he wet them and wiped her cheek and her ear and her neck, and the towels came away red, and then pink, and then clean.

No holes. Anywhere.

"Can you sit up?" John asked her.

She nodded. He put his hand behind her back and helped her sit forward and washed her back. Nothing. It was not her blood. None of it was her blood.

He lifted her out of the tub, wet and shivering, and wrapped her in a towel, and carried her to the bed, and laid her down and pulled the sheet and the blanket over her up to her chin.

"Is she okay?" Dorina asked. Her face was gray.

"She's okay. She's not hurt. She's in shock." John straightened up. "Please stay with her. She might want water. She'll probably just sleep."

* * *

He went back outside with the Sig up and the safety off.

The man he had shot was sitting up on the grass now, talking to Jim. Jim had made him take off the flannel shirt he was wearing and had tied it tight around his right shoulder. Under it the man wore an olive-green t-shirt, dark with blood on the right side. Jim had his rifle — a black AR with a long scope, set up for a left-handed shooter, the brass deflector on the wrong side — leaning against his own leg.

Then John saw the woman by the dumpster.

She was lying face down on the asphalt next to the bag Sanda had dropped. She had blond hair, and the back of her head was dark and wet. He crouched beside her and took her by the shoulder and turned her partway over so he could see her face.

Most of it was gone. The bullet had gone in at the back of her head and come out through the front, and it had taken with it the bridge of her nose and both of her eyes and most of her forehead. What was left was not a face. He looked at it anyway, longer than he should have, because he had to be sure, and he was. It was Sarah, with the pug. Sarah, who had told him her name on Monday morning with her cold thin hand in his. Sarah, who ate ramen so her dog could eat salmon. Beside her right hand, on the asphalt, lay a short, shiny revolver.

He thought: she was two hundred feet from my front door. She was starving two hundred feet from a family eating prosciutto. And I did not know her name until Monday.

He stood up and turned around and pointed the Sig at the man's head.

"What the fucking hell!" John shouted.

"Whoa — whoa, whoa, whoa, John," the man said, holding up his good hand. "John. It's me. It's Trevor. From the store."

It was. Under the blood and the dirt and the gray in his face, with his sand-colored cap gone somewhere and his short beard full of grass, it was Trevor. The compass rose on his left forearm. The pale eyes, not amused at all now.

"Explain to me, Trevor," John said, keeping the red dot in the middle of his forehead, "what the fuck-ass hell is going on."

"It's cool, man. It's cool. Let me explain." Trevor did not move. "I was walking out to the dumpster with some trash. From way back, I recognized your wife. I saw she had her hands up, and that lady" — he tipped his head toward Sarah, without looking — "had a gun in her face. Yelling at her. I couldn't hear what. My rifle has a long-range scope. So I braced against that tree" — he nodded at a big live oak across the lawn, at least eighty yards away — "and I put the crosshairs on the back of her head. I knew I could make the shot. So I took it. And I was running over to check on your wife when you shot me in the back."

John lowered the Sig a few inches. He looked at the oak. He looked at Sarah. He looked at Trevor.

"If that's what happened," John said, "then I'm sorry I shot you."

It did not sound sorry. He could hear that it did not.

"I get it, man," Trevor said. "You come out, you see a guy with an AR running at your wife, and she's covered in blood. I'd have done the same thing." He tried to move his shoulder and winced. "Just curious. Where were you aiming?"

"Your spine," John said.

"Huh." Trevor looked at him for a second, and something came back into his eyes, a little of the old amusement. "I'm lucky you're a bad shot, then."

"I'm not a bad shot," John said, slightly insulted. "I haven't fired this gun in a while. The red dot's off."

"Sure," Trevor said.

"Stay here," John said. "With my dad. I need to talk to my wife. Then we'll get you inside, clean that up. I've got some antibiotics."

"I'll be right here," Trevor said, and leaned back on his good elbow on the grass.

* * *

Sanda was sitting up in bed with the blanket around her, holding a glass of water in both hands, when he came in. Dorina sat on the edge of the bed, stroking her hair, which was still damp and pink at the ends.

"How are you, baby?"

"I think I'm okay." Sanda's voice was very quiet. "It all happened so fast. I couldn't — I was walking to the dumpster with a bag of shit, and this lady stuck a fucking gun in my face. She kept saying about her baby. Her baby was out of food." She stared at the glass. "Then there was a gunshot and I couldn't see. I thought the bitch shot me. I thought I was going to die. I think I fell down? I heard somebody yelling. Was that you?"

"That was Trevor," John said. "From the gun store. He shot her. To save you. And then I shot him."

Sanda looked up. "You *shot* him?"

"I didn't know it was him."

She put a hand up to her hair and smelled her fingers and made a face. "My fucking hair smells like blood," Sanda said, and started to cry, not hard, the way you cry when it is over.

He brought Trevor inside. Jim walked behind him with the left-handed rifle and the shiny revolver, which he had picked up off the asphalt. Charlie went into what Sanda called Intruder Alert, standing in the middle of the living room with every hair on his back straight up, barking and growling at Trevor in alternation.

"Charlie, no bark."

John put a kitchen stool in the guest bathroom and sat Trevor on it.

"Dorina," John called. "Can you help us?"

Dorina came. She looked at Trevor, sitting on the stool in his bloody green shirt, and something happened to her face that John had never seen before. It went quiet and flat and very sure. She pushed up the sleeves of her housecoat.

"Alcohol," Dorina said. "I need alcohol. Clean towels. And—" She turned to Sanda, in the bedroom doorway, and said something in Romanian.

"Pads," Sanda translated, after a second. "She wants my pads. For the bleeding."

"All we have is whiskey," John said. "For the alcohol."

"If is all you have," Dorina said, "is good."

The Wild Turkey 101 was in the pantry, on the bottom shelf, where it had been since Black Friday, in its big dusty jug, next to a plastic water bottle with a masking-tape label that said AGHEASMĂ. John had bought it to trade. He had bought it for the end of the world, so that he would have something valuable to give a man in exchange for food. Jim brought it to the bathroom.

Dorina cut away the green t-shirt with Sanda's kitchen scissors. There was a small, neat, bleeding hole high on the back of Trevor's right shoulder, and a larger, uglier one in the front, below the collarbone.

"Can you pour into a glass?" Dorina asked.

John poured half a crystal water glass of Wild Turkey and handed it to her.

"Lean this way." Dorina pressed Trevor forward with a hand on the back of his neck, firmly, the way you would push down a dog that wanted to jump. She poured whiskey into the hole in his back. Trevor's whole body went rigid, and he made a sound through his teeth like a kettle.

"Now this way." She pushed him back. She held the glass over the hole in the front and poured the rest of it in, slowly.

"*Ahhhh* — fuck-shit-ass-*mother*fucker!" Trevor screamed, and Charlie fled down the hall.

Dorina set the empty glass on the sink and pressed a folded maxi pad hard over the front wound with the heel of her hand and held it there, and she did not look the least bit sorry. "Good," she said. "Is clean now."

John went through the medicine cabinet until he found an old bottle of Vicodin from when he had thrown his back out moving the recliner into the Chicago apartment. It had expired years ago. He shook one out. "Take this."

Trevor took it from his fingers with his left hand and threw it in his mouth and swallowed it dry, and leaned his head back against the bathroom wall, breathing fast.

Dorina taped the pads in place, front and back, with medical tape from the first aid kit, and wrapped the whole shoulder in an Ace bandage she found at the bottom of the same kit, still in its plastic. Her hands moved like someone else's hands, fast and certain.

"You," she told Trevor, pointing at his face. "Sit. Don't move. No more shooting."

"Yes, ma'am," Trevor said faintly.

They put him in John's recliner. He was asleep in five minutes.

* * *

Sanda was asleep too, when John looked in on her. He backed out and closed the door and went and sat on the arm of the couch beside his father.

"Dad," John said quietly. "Sanda said that woman kept talking about her baby. That her baby was starving."

Jim looked at him.

"I can't stand it," John said. "Thinking there's a baby over there. Alone. Hungry. In that apartment. With her lying out there." He swallowed. "I think we have to go get it."

Jim pulled at his mustache for a long time. Then he nodded once.

"Let's go do it," Jim said.

* * *

They crossed the street in broad daylight, John with an AR-15 slung across his back and Jim carrying the shotgun pointed at the ground. The street was empty. It looked like a ghost town. From halfway across they could smell the dumpster, and the bags around it, and something else under those, which was Sarah.

Every blind in Sarah's apartment was shut. They went around to the back and stood listening. John could not hear much of anything over the ringing in his ears. *From now on*, he thought, *I wear earplugs. Every time.*

"Find a rock," Jim said. "We'll break the window and climb in."

"We're in the middle of Florida, Dad," John said. "Where the hell am I going to find a rock?"

They looked at the ground. There were oak leaves and magnolia leaves and sand. There were no rocks. There had never been rocks. John spotted a big terra-cotta pot by the patio, full of dirt and the dry brown skeleton of a plant that had died a long time ago, and he picked it up — it was heavy — and swung it back to throw it through the bedroom window.

"Wait, son," Jim said. "What if the baby's right there? On the other side of the window?"

"Dammit," John said, and put the pot down very carefully.

"The sliding door," John said. "Around back. People always leave those unlocked."

The patio was screened in. John took out the Benchmade, pressed the button, and the blade snapped open, and he cut a long slit straight down the middle of a screen panel. He stepped back to admire it, and saw that his father had already walked over to the screen door at the corner of the patio, opened it — it had not been locked — and was standing inside the patio looking back at him with both eyebrows slightly raised.

John stepped through his slit.

The sliding glass door was locked. Behind it the vertical blinds were closed. But John could see that the security bar — the metal rod that folds down to block the track — had not been set. "She didn't put the bar down," he said. "We can jiggle it." He took hold of the handle and shook the door back and forth, gently at first, then harder, then much harder.

"I used to arrest people for doing this," Jim said.

"Thank you, Dad."

"I'm just saying. Breaking and entering. Third-degree felony."

"*Open*," John said, "you *fucking bitch*," and slammed the handle sideways with all his weight, and the door did not open. He stepped back, breathing hard, and put his hands on his knees.

Jim stepped forward, took the handle, and slid the door open.

"Really?" John said.

The lock must have given out on the last slam. They pushed through the clacking vertical blinds into the dark. John had forgotten his flashlight. He had forgotten his flashlight every single time he had left the apartment for three days. He slid the door shut behind them and dropped the security bar into place.

And then the smell hit.

It came at them from the back of the apartment like a wall. It was worse than the dumpster. It was worse than anything. John gagged.

"Jesus Christ, what is that?"

"Smells like shit," Jim said. "Literally."

They checked the living room, mostly empty, a couch and a television and a stack of magazines, and the kitchen, where every cabinet stood open and empty. The smell came from down the dark hallway. There were two doors. John drew the Glock 26 and put his left hand on the knob of the first, and his heart was hammering, and he threw it open.

He gagged again. He could taste it. It was a bathroom, and he could see, in the dimness, a toilet, and the toilet was full. It was more than full. Three days without water, alone, with a toilet that flushed once. It was piled above the rim. It ran down the sides.

He slammed the door.

"I really, *really*," John said, "regret opening that door."

"I've seen worse," Jim said. "And smelled worse."

"Great, Dad. That's great. Because it's your turn. Door number two."

Jim drew the Glock 29 and held it in both hands, the old way, and stood beside the door frame instead of in front of it, the way they had taught him at the academy more than forty years ago, and reached across and opened the door with his left hand. John swung the AR around off his back and turned on the optic and brought it up to his shoulder, covering him. Jim swept the room with the pistol.

An unmade bed. A cheap dresser. A lamp. No crib. No baby toys. No baby things anywhere.

John lowered the rifle. Something on the floor beside the bed caught his eye, and he bent to look at it. It was a dog toy. A little plush hedgehog, chewed, with a squeaker in it.

"Okay," John said. "I'm officially an idiot."

"What?"

"It's the dog." He held up the hedgehog. "She has a dog. A pug. Charlie plays with it. *That's* the baby. The dog is the baby."

Jim holstered his pistol. "Well. Then where is it? What's its name?"

John shook his head. "I don't know. She told me once. I just found out *her* name on Monday."

They searched the apartment calling *Here, puppy, puppy. Here, little pug.* Under the bed, something whined.

* * *

It took a flashlight, a Ziploc bag of Charlie's treats, a mattress, and a box spring. John ran back across the street for the flashlight and the treats, past his mother and Dorina, who looked at him and did not say a word, past Trevor asleep in the recliner. Back under Sarah's bed, the flashlight showed him boxes and bags and shoes and, far back in the corner, a small patch of fawn-colored fur, shaking.

He put a treat on the floor at the edge of the bed. "Come on, girl. It's okay."

Two small paws inched forward. Then a flat black face. The pug took the treat, chewed it twice, swallowed it, looked at him — and when he reached for her, scooted straight back into the farthest corner.

"I don't have time for this," John said, getting up. "Dad. Mattress."

They lifted the mattress off and leaned it against the wall. They lifted the box spring off and leaned it against the mattress. And there she was, in the middle of the bare bed frame on the carpet, shaking.

John picked her up. She weighed almost nothing. Under her fur he could feel every one of her ribs, like the tines of a fork.

"Man, this dog stinks," John said.

"We all stink," Jim said.

"There's stink, and then there's *stink*."

"Well, we're low on water."

"She's small," John said. "A bowl'll do it."

On the way back across the street, carrying the pug against his chest, John looked up at the sky. It was a hard, clear, cloudless blue. "You know, any other time of year it rains here every afternoon," he said. "It's been weeks. We ought to put some buckets out, in case it rains." He thought about it. "Of course, we don't own a bucket."

"We don't?"

"We have four Ralph Lauren wine chillers."

They walked a few steps.

"You shot a man in the back today, son," Jim said quietly.

John kept walking. "I missed."

"You hit him," Jim said.

They did not talk about it again.

* * *

"Oh my *word*!" Granny said, when he came through the door with the pug.

Sanda was up. She was sitting on the couch in John's Bears hoodie, pale, with her hair pulled back. "Where did you find him? Why does he smell like that?"

"She's a she," John said. "Charlie plays with her. She was living in her own filth over there." He paused. "She's the dog of the lady who pulled a gun on you."

Sanda's eyes went wide. "Where's the baby?"

"You're looking at her."

Sanda stared at the pug in his arms. Her mouth opened.

"So that crazy bitch," Sanda said slowly, "pulled a gun on me because of her *dog*?"

"What if it was Charlie?" John asked. "What if you were all alone, and you were out of food, and it was Charlie?"

Sanda looked at him. Then she looked down at the floor, and got up, and went and sat in the armchair by the window, and did not say anything else.

John bathed the pug in the guest tub with one small bowl of water and a squirt of Sanda's body wash, and she shivered the whole time, and he could feel every rib under the lather. He dried her with a towel. Sanda, without a word, got up and poured some of Charlie's kibble into a metal bowl and set it on the kitchen floor, and the little dog heard the pellets rattling into the bowl and tilted her head. John put her down. She shook herself, spraying the cabinets, and walked to the bowl and ate everything in it in under a minute.

Then she looked up at John and burped.

* * *

He and his father went out to the patio and set the four Ralph Lauren wine chillers in a row along the low wall, silver and shining, open to the sky. They looked ridiculous. They looked like a table set for a party nobody had been invited to.

When John came back in, Trevor was awake. He had gotten up out of the recliner by himself and was standing in the kitchen with his bandaged shoulder and his left hand on the counter, swaying a little.

"Got any water, man?" Trevor said. "I'm dying."

"Sure," John said. "Help yourself."

Trevor picked up the nearest pitcher. It was nearly empty. He picked up the next one. Empty. He went down the row along the back of the counter, twelve pitchers and the punch bowl and the mixing bowl, and they were all empty, every one, until he came to the rooster at the end. There was water in the rooster. He poured it into a glass. It filled the glass about two-thirds of the way. Then the rooster was empty, too.

Trevor lifted the glass and drank it. All of it. His throat moved, swallow after swallow.

John stood in the kitchen doorway and watched the last of their water go down another man's throat, the way he had stood on Monday morning and watched the tub go down the drain.
