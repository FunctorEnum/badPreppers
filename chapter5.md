# Chapter Five

## The Range

> *"Wednesday's brief service interruption was resolved in eleven minutes by our automated resilience systems. No customer data was affected. We thank our customers for their patience."*
>
> — Meridian National Bank, press release

Friday was salmon.

It had been salmon on Fridays for almost ten years, ever since a night in Chicago when Sanda had come home with two thick steaks from the fish counter at Whole Foods and changed John's mind about a fish he had spent his whole life hating. Before that, every piece of salmon John had ever eaten had been a fillet, and every fillet had been cooked by someone who was afraid of it, until it was gray and dry and tasted faintly of a pond. No cedar plank in the world could save a salmon fillet, as far as John was concerned. He had been proven wrong exactly once, and he had been eating the proof every Friday since.

He was on his way to the fish market after work when the phone rang through the car speakers.

"Hey," John said, glancing at the screen, where Sanda's name glowed in clean white letters. "I'm almost to the fish place."

"I'm going to be late," Sanda said. Through the GTI's speakers her voice sounded as though she were standing outside in a hurricane with a sheet of aluminum foil over her mouth. The car had a microphone hidden somewhere in the headliner, and John had never found it, and neither, apparently, had the people who designed it. "One of the tellers can't balance. I need to stay and figure it out."

"Which one?"

"Brianna. The new one." Sanda's voice dropped, the way it did when there were employees within earshot and she wanted to scream. "It's not her fault. I don't think it's her fault. I'll explain later."

"I'll keep the fish on ice," John said, slowing for a light on Lake Mary Boulevard that had turned red faster than he expected. "Text me when you leave and I'll start cooking."

"Okay. Love you. Bye."

"Bye," John said, and the screen went back to its map, which had already decided where he was going.

While he waited at the light, he thought about his wife at work. He had never liked that she worked at a bank. She had worked at banks in three states, and she had been there for two robberies, and in one of them, at a branch in Evanston, a man in a ski mask had pointed a revolver at her face from three feet away and told her to open the vault. She had opened the vault. She had then gone home and taken a bath that lasted four hours, and for about a year afterward she had not liked to sit with her back to a door. John was fairly sure that was why, for so long, she had looked at every gun he brought home as if it were the same gun. His mother had worked in banking, too, for thirty-one years, and had been robbed twice herself, and had told him about it as casually as if she were telling him about a flat tire. Both of them, his mother and his wife, had spent their working lives counting other people's money behind glass while strangers walked in off the street. John wrote software. The most dangerous thing that had ever happened to him at work was Tyler.

The light changed. He turned left, drove a block down, and pulled into the lot of the fish market.

* * *

The fish market was in a strip center between a nail salon and a store that sold vacuum cleaner parts, and inside it was cold and bright and smelled — Sanda had told him — clean, the way a good fish market should, like a beach at dawn. Behind the long glass case stood Nikos, who owned the place, a broad, heavy man of sixty or so with forearms like hams, a white apron over a gray t-shirt, a gold cross nestled in his chest hair, and a navy Greek fisherman's cap that he wore indoors and out and, John suspected, to bed.

"Mister John," Nikos said, wiping his hands on the apron. "Friday."

"Friday," John agreed, bending to look into the case.

The salmon steaks were beautiful. They had been cut that afternoon, each one more than an inch thick, the flesh a pale bright coral, and in the center of each one the white bone radiated out like the spokes of a wheel. There were four on the ice. Two of them were huge.

"Those two," John said, pointing through the glass.

"The big ones," Nikos said, approvingly, reaching for them with a sheet of waxed paper. "Your wife still makes them with the dill?"

"Every week."

"Smart woman." Nikos wrapped the steaks and weighed them and wrote a price on the package with a grease pencil. "Most people, they want the fillet. No bones. No skin. No fish, really. They want a fish that doesn't remind them it was a fish." He shrugged enormously. "Your wife, she knows. The bone is where the flavor lives."

John paid and walked next door to the Publix for a bunch of fresh dill, and he stood in the produce aisle holding it and remembering.

The first time Sanda had made the salmon steaks, it had been a Friday in early summer in Chicago, in the apartment on the twenty-eighth floor. She had laid sliced lemons across a sheet of foil, and set a thin pat of real butter on each slice, and laid the steaks on top of the lemons, and seasoned them with salt and pepper and a snowfall of chopped dill. Then more lemon on top, and more butter on each slice, and she had folded the foil into a loose silver pocket and slid it into the oven at four hundred degrees for forty minutes. While it baked, John had gone out on the balcony with a glass of wine. The sun was going down behind the city, and out to the east the lake had turned pink and then gold and then a deep, dusty blue, and the Ferris wheel at Navy Pier had lit up red and white against it, and the dark tower of the Hancock stood up beside him with its windows coming on one floor at a time. He had stood out there looking at all of it, and he had felt, even then, a kind of grief, knowing that someday he would not live in that city, that someday it would be something he remembered from somewhere else. Then the smell had come out through the balcony door — he could not smell it, but Sanda told him about it later, and he believed her — and he had gone back inside and sat down and eaten the best piece of fish of his life.

He had learned to make it himself after that. On Fridays, when she worked late, he made it for her.

* * *

Sanda got home at a quarter to eight, still in her navy suit, and kicked off her heels at the door and walked straight to the kitchen and poured herself a glass of the sparkling rosé that John had opened twenty minutes before. She drank half of it standing at the counter.

"That good?" John asked, sliding the foil pocket out of the oven.

"That bad." She pushed her glasses up into her hair and rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. "Brianna's drawer was off by forty dollars."

"That's not much."

"That's not the point." Sanda carried her glass to the table and sat, and John brought the plates — the steaks glistening on a bed of lemons, wild rice on the side, a few spears of asparagus — and put on a Miles Davis record, because there was always music with dinner, and it was always something with a piano or a slow horn. *Kind of Blue* came up quietly from the speakers on the bookshelf. Charlie lay down under the table on Sanda's feet.

"The system says she took in four thousand one hundred and eighty dollars today," Sanda said, cutting into her steak. "The drawer says four thousand one hundred and forty. So she's short forty. Except I counted it. Three times. Carla counted it twice. Brianna counted it, I don't know, ten times, crying. Every deposit slip matches the cash. Every single one. The only thing that doesn't match is the system."

"So the system's wrong."

"The system's not wrong," Sanda said, with her mouth full. "The system's never wrong anymore. That's the whole problem. When the system's never wrong, then the forty dollars has to be Brianna." She swallowed and took a drink. "So I wrote it up as a teller error. Because what else am I going to write? 'The computer did it'? You know what my regional VP would say if I wrote 'the computer did it'?"

"What?"

"He'd say, 'The computer doesn't do things, Sanda. People do things.'" She stabbed a piece of asparagus. "And then he'd make Brianna do a training module."

John ate for a while in silence. The salmon was perfect. The rosé was cold and dry and tasted of strawberries he had to take on faith. Out on the patio, through the sliding door, the screens moved a little in the wind.

"Is this about Wednesday?" John asked, setting down his fork.

Sanda looked up. "You saw it?"

"It was on the news. Your bank went down for eleven minutes."

"Everything went down for eleven minutes," Sanda said, putting her fork down. "Every screen in the branch. Every terminal, every teller station, the ATM, the phones, my computer. Ten past two in the afternoon. Just black. Like somebody pulled a plug."

"What did you do?"

"Nothing," Sanda said. "That's the thing. We couldn't do anything. We had a line of nine people and we couldn't do one transaction, because nobody knows how anymore. Not by hand. We used to have paper. Deposit books, carbon paper, a ledger. They threw all of it out years ago. Carla — she's been there twenty-three years — she went in the back and dug around for five minutes and came out with one old carbon deposit book from a drawer, and she held it up like she'd found the Dead Sea Scrolls, and nobody remembered how to fill it out. Not even her." Sanda shook her head. "So we just stood there. All of us. Eleven minutes, looking at the black screens, and the customers looking at us."

"And then it came back."

"And then it came back," Sanda said. She picked up her glass and looked at it without drinking. "All by itself. No IT, no phone call, nothing. Two twenty-one, the screens just came back on. And everything was fine. Better than fine. Every teller's totals for the day were already done. Already balanced. Carla's drawer had been off by thirty-five cents since that morning — she'd been looking for it for hours — and when the screens came back, it was balanced. To the penny."

John had stopped eating.

"Who balanced them?" John asked.

Sanda looked at him across the table. Miles Davis played a long, slow, lonely phrase into the room and let it fall.

"That's what Carla said," Sanda said quietly, and finished her wine.

* * *

On Saturday morning the pod coffee machine ran out of pods.

"We could use the French press," John said, standing in front of the empty carousel in his boxers.

"The French press is retired," Sanda said from the bedroom. "It's on the high shelf with the fondue set. It's earned its rest."

"So what do I do?"

"Go to the Publix," Sanda said. "Like a man."

He went to the Publix like a man. When he got back he took a shower, and afterward he stood in front of the bathroom mirror with a towel around his waist and conducted an honest physical self-assessment, which he did about once a month and which never went well. He had been rail thin for the first thirty years of his life. He had eaten whatever he wanted, whenever he wanted, in quantities that had alarmed waiters, and he had never gained a pound. Then, at thirty, as though some clause in a contract had come due, all of it had arrived at once. Now he stood six foot one and weighed two hundred and ten, and he lifted weights at the gym three times a week, or twice, or once, and he looked in the mirror and told himself that he was mostly muscle under a small layer of fat. He turned sideways. He sucked in his stomach. He let it out.

"Small layer," he told the mirror firmly.

The mirror, like Granny, said nothing.

* * *

Dorina arrived just after noon in a taxi, as promised, and her gift this time was not food.

She came up the walk in a purple windbreaker zipped to the chin, black slacks, white sneakers, and a white visor over her burgundy hair, and she was holding something rolled up in both hands like a diploma. At the door she unrolled it and held it up to John with tremendous pride.

It was a paper target, a black bullseye on white, and it was full of small holes. Most of them were in the black.

"John," Dorina said, beaming, her gold crown flashing. "I shoot rifle. Look how good I did."

John took the target and looked at it. It was, honestly, good. There were twenty-two holes in the paper and seventeen of them were in the black and six of them were in the center ring.

"Mom," John said, holding the target up to the light, "where did you do this?"

Dorina explained, in Romanian, at length, with gestures. Sanda translated in pieces from the kitchen. The senior center near Dorina's condo had held a women's safety day. A retired sheriff's deputy had come with a box of old .22 rifles and set up targets in a field behind the building, and fourteen ladies had shot, and Dorina, who had never fired a gun in her life, had shot the best.

"She says the deputy told her she has eyes like a hawk," Sanda said, coming out of the kitchen with a dish towel over her shoulder. "She says he asked her if she was in the army. She told him no, the ambulance."

"Ambulance," Dorina confirmed, nodding. "Twelve years."

John looked at the target. He looked at Dorina's shining face. He looked at Sanda, who had begun telling her mother in Romanian about how she and John went to the range most weekends now, and as she talked Dorina's eyes got bigger and brighter, and John knew exactly where this was going, and he said it anyway.

"Would your mom," John said slowly, in a low voice, regretting it before he reached the end of the sentence, "like to come to the range with us today?"

Sanda repeated the question in Romanian. John knew enough Romanian to understand the answer, which was yes, with three exclamation points. He would not have needed to know any Romanian at all.

* * *

For Dorina he brought the old .22.

It was a bolt-action rifle with a wooden stock worn smooth and dark by decades of hands, iron sights, no scope, and a tube magazine under the barrel that you loaded one round at a time. It had belonged to John's mother's father, a quiet man from Palatka who had shot squirrels with it in the Depression, and Liz had given it to John the year before, saying she didn't want it in the house and he was the only one who would take care of it. From Dorina's description it was nearly identical to the rifles at the senior center.

The range on a Saturday afternoon was busier than on Saturday morning. Two men in camouflage were shooting shotguns at the far end, and a father was teaching a teenage son to shoot a pistol on the pistol range, and the range officer, a leathery retired Marine named Odom with a white crew cut and a coffee can of earplugs, came out of the shed to shake Dorina's hand and call her ma'am. John set them up on the twenty-five-yard line, at the end nearest the woods, where it was quiet.

And then he stood back, and watched his wife teach her mother to shoot.

Sanda did it in a mix of Romanian and English, the two languages braided together the way they always were between them, so that John caught every third word. She showed Dorina the four rules, counting them off on her fingers: always treat every gun as if it is loaded; never point it at anything you are not willing to destroy; keep your finger off the trigger until you are ready to shoot; know your target and what is behind it. She showed her mother how to work the bolt, how to load the tube, how to check that the chamber was empty. She showed her how to stand, how to set the stock into the pocket of her shoulder, how to put her cheek on the wood. She made Dorina say the rules back to her in English, and Dorina said them, in an accent so thick that "never point it at anything you are not willing to destroy" came out sounding like a line from an opera.

John stood with his arms folded and his sunglasses on and felt his chest fill up with something he had no name for. Five years ago he had practically had to drag his wife to the range. She had refused to touch a gun for the first three visits. When she finally did, on the fourth, she had held the unloaded Walther as if it were a live snake, and she had cried in the car afterward, not because anything had happened, but because of what it was. And now here she was, in jeans and a fitted black long-sleeve tee and a gray puffer vest, with her ponytail pulled through the back of a ball cap and her shooting glasses on, standing at a firing line with her hand on her mother's shoulder, saying, *Breathe out. Now squeeze. Don't pull. Squeeze.*

Dorina squeezed. The little rifle cracked. A hole appeared in the paper, an inch outside the black.

"Bun," Sanda said, patting her shoulder. "Good. Again."

Dorina fired ten rounds. Eight of them were in the black. When she was done, she lowered the rifle, opened the bolt the way Sanda had shown her, set it on the bench with the chamber open, and turned to her daughter with her whole face shining.

"Like hawk," Dorina said.

"Like hawk, Mommy," Sanda agreed.

Then Dorina took a seat on the bench in the shade to watch, and John began his own routine with the AR-15, and Sanda stood beside her mother for a minute watching him, and then she said, in a casual voice, as if she were asking him to pass the salt, "Load up a magazine. I want to shoot the AR."

John lowered the rifle. "You want to shoot the AR?"

"Are you deaf? Load a magazine."

He loaded a magazine. He did it slowly, so that she would not see his hands shaking a little, because he had wanted to hear her say that sentence for five years and had long ago given up on hearing it.

The AR-15 looked enormous next to her. It was John's best one, the one with the quad rail along the barrel and the holographic red-dot sight and the folding bipod under the front, and it was heavier than it looked, and it looked heavy. Sanda was a petite woman — on her tiptoes she cleared five feet — and when she lifted the rifle to her shoulder it seemed to be about a third of her. She struggled with the weight for a moment, finding her balance, the muzzle wavering.

"Is it going to kick?" Sanda asked, and some of her confidence had leaked out of her voice.

"No," John said firmly, standing just behind her right shoulder. "Almost no kick at all. Less than the Glock."

"You're lying."

"I'm not lying. I promise. Turn on the optic. The up arrow. Now look through the window. See the red dot? Put the dot on the target. Both eyes open. Safety."

She pushed the selector from safe to fire with her thumb.

He remembered his own first time. Matt's rifle, the long anticipation, the crack, the nudge. He remembered expecting it to break his shoulder. He watched his wife feel what he had felt, the dread and the fascination, and he wanted to put a hand on her back and didn't, because she would have killed him.

The rifle cracked. Downrange, at twenty-five yards, a small hole appeared in the paper, just high and right of center.

She did not lower the rifle. She did not say anything. She breathed, and moved the dot, and fired again, and a second hole appeared less than an inch from the first. She fired a third time. The third hole touched the second.

John stood behind her with his mouth open.

Sanda lowered the rifle a few inches and looked at the target, and looked at it again, and then she looked over her shoulder at him, and her face was a face he had not seen in years — the face of a girl in a high school hallway who has just found out she is smarter than everybody in the room.

"It doesn't kick at all," Sanda said.

"I told you."

"You tell me a lot of things." Her arms were trembling with the weight. She found the button on the forward grip with her left hand, and the bipod legs snapped down with a pop, and she sat at the bench and rested the rifle on it and settled the stock into her shoulder and fired the rest of the magazine, slowly, about one shot every second, every shot on the paper, nearly every one inside the second ring. When the bolt locked back on the empty magazine she lifted her cheek off the stock and looked at him.

"Load up another magazine," Sanda said.

*I've created a monster*, John thought, glowing.

She shot forty rounds. All forty were on the paper. On the walk back to the car she carried the target rolled up under her arm like a diploma, the way her mother had carried hers, and Dorina walked beside her holding her arm, and they talked in Romanian the whole way across the gravel lot, and both of them were laughing.

* * *

It was a twenty-minute drive home. Sanda sat in the passenger seat with the rolled-up target across her knees and her cap off and her hair down, and Dorina sat in the back seat with her purse in her lap, and nobody talked for the first few miles. They were all tired in the good way.

"You know," Sanda said finally, looking out the window at the pine woods going by, "my AR is now my best gun."

"Your AR," John repeated.

"My AR. The one I shot. It's mine now."

"It has a quad rail and a bipod and a twelve-hundred-dollar optic, Sanda."

"And now it has an owner," Sanda said serenely.

John drove for a while, smiling. Then he said, "Okay. So say it all goes down. Worst case. The end of the world. Here's our team." He took one hand off the wheel and pointed at her. "You're on the AR. I'm on the other AR. Your mom's on the .22." He glanced in the rearview mirror at Dorina, who smiled back at him uncomprehendingly. "And my parents." He paused. "My dad, who hates guns. My mom, who I have never once seen hold a gun. And Granny."

"Granny," Sanda said.

John cleared his throat and put on the voice of every drill sergeant in every movie he had ever seen. "GRANNY! Get on that fifty! MOM! I need you to lay down cover fire with the AK! Do NOT let up!"

Sanda burst out laughing. John laughed too, so hard he had to slow down, and he kept going — "DAD! Stop arresting them and SHOOT!" — and Sanda laughed until she had tears under her glasses and had to take them off to wipe her eyes, and Dorina laughed in the back seat because they were laughing, and for a minute the whole car shook with it.

Then it slowed. Then it stopped. Sanda put her glasses back on and looked out the window at the pines again.

"Yeah," Sanda said. "We're fucked."

They drove for a while without talking.

From the back seat, Dorina said something in Romanian. It was a long sentence, said slowly, in a quiet voice, not to either of them in particular.

"What did she say?" John asked, glancing in the mirror.

Sanda turned in her seat and asked her mother something, and Dorina answered, and Sanda listened, and then turned back to the front with a small frown.

"She says in eighty-nine nobody had guns," Sanda said, "and it didn't matter. She says everybody just stopped listening at the same time."

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know." Sanda turned back again. "Mama, ce înseamnă?"

Dorina shrugged in the back seat, a big shrug that lifted the purple windbreaker up to her ears. "Is just what happen," she said in English, and looked out the window.

John drove. He did not understand it. He could see in the set of Sanda's mouth that she did not understand it either, and that it bothered her more than she was going to say.

"Eighty-nine," John said carefully. "The revolution."

"She was there," Sanda said, still looking straight ahead. "In the square, the first day. She was working on the ambulance. I was four. I was at my grandmother's." She picked at the edge of the rolled target with one fingernail. "She doesn't talk about it. Not to me, not to anybody. Thirty-seven years. You ask her and she says it was cold and she was tired and then she asks if you're hungry."

"Are you?" Dorina asked from the back seat, opening her eyes. "Hungry?"

"No, Mommy."

"I have cozonac in my bag," Dorina said, and closed her eyes again.

"She used to fill the bathtub," Sanda said after a mile, to the windshield. "When I was little. After. Every time the radio said anything. A storm, a strike, a speech, the price of bread. Anything. She'd hear it on the radio and go straight in and fill the tub to the top. Cold water. We'd go a week without a bath because she didn't want to use the water." She laughed a little, without much in it. "I used to hate it. All my friends had hot baths and I had a tub full of cold water I wasn't allowed to touch."

"Every time," Dorina said from the back seat, in English. She had heard that much, at least. "And every time, I was right."

"You were not right every time, Mama."

"I was right enough times," Dorina said, and patted her purse, where the stun gun was, and closed her eyes.

* * *

That night, in bed, Sanda's phone buzzed on her nightstand.

She was reading a magazine about kitchens. John was half asleep. He heard her pick up the phone, and then he heard her go very still, the way she went still in a store when she saw a price she did not believe.

"What?" John said into his pillow.

"It's Carla." Sanda held the phone out to him without looking at him. On the screen was a text, with three exclamation points and a screenshot.

*Did u see this?? System just posted a "forecast ledger" for the branch for MONDAY. Deposits, withdrawals, cash in, cash out. Every drawer. Already balanced. IT says it's a new feature?? We haven't even opened yet!!!*

John sat up and looked at the screenshot. It was a spreadsheet, neat and orderly, in the bank's blue colors, with a date at the top that was two days away. Brianna's drawer was on it. Brianna's drawer was balanced.

"It's Saturday," John said.

"I know what day it is," Sanda said.

She took the phone back from him and looked at the screenshot for a long time, while the air conditioner hummed and Charlie snored at the foot of the bed. Then she turned the phone over and laid it face down on the nightstand, screen to the wood, and turned off her lamp.

In twenty years John had never once seen her do that.
