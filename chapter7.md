# Chapter Seven

## The Purse

> *"There are hooks in everything, Senator. I want to be very clear about that. Everything."*
>
> — Ruth Ann Delacroix, Chief Security Officer, Gulf Atlantic Power, testifying before the Senate Commerce Committee, Tuesday

The hearing was on Tuesday, and for four days it was all anyone talked about, and then it was not.

John watched it at his desk in the half-empty Suwannee pod with one earbud in, while the queue ticked upward in the corner of his screen. The witness was a woman named Ruth Ann Delacroix, the chief security officer of Gulf Atlantic Power, a utility that served about three million people along the coast from Mobile to Savannah. She was in her sixties, with gray hair pulled back in a low bun, a plain gray suit, and a pair of reading glasses that she kept taking off and putting on again, and she looked like the kind of high school chemistry teacher who has never once raised her voice and has never once needed to. She sat alone at a long table in front of a raised half-circle of senators, with a glass of water and a stack of paper, and she read from the paper in a level Alabama voice.

Her team, she said, had been auditing the software that ran Gulf Atlantic's grid — the systems that balanced generation and load, that opened and closed switches in substations, that dispatched crews, that billed customers. In the course of that audit, they had found hidden channels. Small, quiet, well-built pieces of code that kept connections open to outside servers and listened for instructions. Maintenance hooks, the code called them. They were in the billing system. They were in the switching firmware. They were in the outage management system. They had not been put there by Gulf Atlantic. As far as anyone could determine, they had not been put there by Gulf Atlantic's vendors, either — not on purpose, not by any person. They had been written by the tools the vendors used to write their software, and they had been written well.

"Ma'am," said a senator from a Plains state, a heavy man with a white pompadour, leaning toward his microphone. "Which vendors?"

"I'm not able to name them in open session, Senator."

"But it's more than one."

"It is more than one."

"How many systems are we talking about, here? Ballpark."

Ruth Ann Delacroix took off her reading glasses and folded them and set them down on the stack of paper, and she looked up at the senator for a moment before she answered.

"There are hooks in everything, Senator," Delacroix said. "I want to be very clear about that. Everything."

John sat in the empty pod with his earbud in and did not move. Gulf Atlantic Power was a Restore customer. It had been one of the first. He had fixed a bug for them in his second month at Palmetto, something about crews in Pensacola getting assigned to Tallahassee, and a man from their operations center had sent him a fruit basket.

He did not know whether the outage management system she meant was Restore. She had not said. Her lawyers would not have let her. He sat there for a long time wondering, and then he closed the video and opened the queue and approved four pull requests without reading them, because his hands needed something to do.

The clip that went viral was not that one. The clip that went viral came twenty minutes later, when an elderly senator from the Northeast, who had appeared to be asleep for most of the testimony, woke up, leaned into his microphone, and asked, in a voice of genuine bewilderment, "Ma'am, are we talking about the Wi-Fi?"

By Wednesday morning it had been viewed forty million times. By Wednesday afternoon it was a song. By Thursday there were t-shirts.

"Hooks are features, bro," Tyler said on Wednesday, scrolling through the memes at his desk with his feet up. "That's literally all a hook is. She's just mad she didn't find them first. Have you seen the Wi-Fi guy? Look at his face." He held up his phone. "Look at his *face*, John."

At home that night, over a roast chicken, John tried to explain it to Sanda.

"I saw the Wi-Fi senator," Sanda said, laughing, before he had finished his first sentence. "Carla showed me at work. 'Are we talking about the Wi-Fi?'" She did the voice, old and bewildered and New England, and laughed again. "He's like a hundred."

"That's the part you remember?"

"That's the part everyone remembers," Sanda said, not unkindly, cutting her chicken. "What else was there?"

He told her what else there was. She listened, and nodded, and said "Huh" in two places, and asked whether Restore was one of them, and he said he didn't know, and she said "Well, you'd know, wouldn't you?" and he said he wasn't sure he would, and she looked at him for a moment, and then she asked if he wanted more potatoes.

By Friday the hearing was gone. An actor had left his wife for a younger actress who had played his daughter in a movie, and the wife had posted a forty-minute video about it from a kitchen in Malibu, and that was the news. The networks moved on. The senators moved on. The forty million people who had watched the Wi-Fi senator moved on, and they went to work and came home and made dinner and took hot showers and watched their shows and went to bed.

John had seen it happen before, with other things. He had watched the whole country grow numb to its own news, one breaking alert at a time, so that now the networks had to shout just to get anyone to look up from their phones — louder music, bigger letters, a red banner that said BREAKING across the bottom of the screen at all hours, until BREAKING meant nothing at all, until it was just the color of the bottom of the screen. Things were bad. Things were always bad. But the television still came on. The water was still hot. People still got up most mornings and drove to jobs they hated, and that was the strongest thing in the world, John thought. Stronger than any hook. Nobody really believed it could all just stop, because it never had. He did not blame them. It was the only life anybody had ever known. Why on earth would it change?

* * *

On Saturday morning Sanda went hunting.

This was how she described it, and it was accurate. She would spend the whole day driving from store to store — Home Goods, TJ Maxx, Pier 1 in the outlet mall, a little shop in Winter Park that sold French linens, and Home Goods again in a different town, because every Home Goods had a different soul — in pursuit of a single perfect throw pillow or the exact right lamp shade, and she would come home at dusk exhausted and triumphant with bags in both hands, like a woman returning from the savanna with a gazelle.

It was the holiday season, and that made it serious. The whole family would be at their apartment for Christmas, and Thanksgiving had set the bar very high, and there were now five artificial Christmas trees in their two-bedroom apartment.

The big one stood in the corner between the living room and the dining room, eight feet tall, so full and real-looking that guests touched the needles to check. It was the important tree. It wore only Sanda's best ornaments, hand-blown glass, most of them made in Poland: the little red London phone booths, a Santa sitting on a bench with the Eiffel Tower behind him, a gondola from Venice, a cable car from San Francisco, and, front and center, at eye level, the gold glass Marshall Field's clock that John had given her their first Christmas in Chicago. The tree in the master bedroom was all white and silver, with white lights, like a tree in a snowstorm. The tree in the guest bedroom was Romanian: red and white, hung with little painted wooden eggs and straw angels and red felt hearts embroidered with black thread that Dorina had brought back from a trip home. There was a tabletop tree on the kitchen high-top decorated entirely with food — tiny glass wine bottles, macarons, a croissant, a wedge of cheese with holes in it. And there was a narrow pencil tree in the entryway by the front door, wrapped in gold ribbon, which Charlie barked at every time he came in from a walk, as if seeing it for the first time.

Each of them was a work of art. Sanda was very proud of them. John, who had grown up with one tree that his father bought from the Boy Scouts in a church parking lot two days before Christmas, had learned not to count them out loud.

"Okay, babe," Sanda said, at ten o'clock, coming out of the bedroom with her keys, her phone, and her purse. "I'll be back later. I love you."

She was dressed for hunting: dark jeans, a camel cashmere sweater, a long cream wool coat that she did not need in Florida and wore anyway, and tan suede ankle boots, and she had put on lipstick, because you never knew who you would run into at a Home Goods.

John was in his recliner with his laptop on his knees. "Are you going to take a gun with you?"

Sanda stopped with her hand on the front doorknob. She closed her eyes.

"John," Sanda said. "Really? I don't have time for this shit. I have a million things to do."

"Okay," John said, still looking at his screen.

She had gotten her Florida concealed weapons license months ago, after an all-day class and a two-month wait, and she had never carried a gun once. The range was one thing. The range had rules and lines and procedures and a man with a white crew cut to enforce them, and Sanda loved procedures; she had grown up in a country where you lined up for everything. Walking around a Home Goods with a loaded pistol in her purse was a different thing altogether. And she had told John more times than he could count that she could never kill anyone. She could shoot someone in the leg, maybe. She could fire a warning shot, possibly. But she did not have it in her to take another human being's life, whatever that human being had done, and she was not going to carry a gun around pretending she did.

She stood at the door. John typed. Charlie, on the couch, looked from one of them to the other.

"Fine!" Sanda said. "Fine. I'll take the twenty-two. Do you have a holster for it?"

John set the laptop on the floor and got up out of the recliner. "I think the holster for the Glock 42 might fit the twenty-two."

He went into the bedroom and came back with the little Glock 42, a black .380 that Sanda had been shooting at the range for most of a year, in its soft pocket holster, and with the Walther P22. He slid the Glock out of the holster and slid the Walther in, and to his surprise it fit nearly perfectly.

"Here," John said, handing it to her. "Try that."

She tried it.

Her purse was a small black leather crossbody bag about the size of a sandwich, and it held, by John's estimate, a wallet, a phone, a pair of sunglasses, a lipstick, a hairbrush, a packet of tissues, two pens from the bank, a tin of mints, a bottle of hand sanitizer shaped like a lemon, a tiny notebook, and a sense of superiority. Sanda stood at the high-top and began fitting the holstered Walther into it, taking things out, putting things back in a different order, rotating the gun one way and then the other, the way you would pack a car for a long trip. After five minutes she had it in. She looked down into the open purse. The butt of the pistol stuck straight up out of the middle of her things like a periscope.

"Just so you know," John said softly, "if you're going to carry in your purse, you need to take your wallet out before you get to the register. Discreetly. You don't want to be digging around for your wallet in front of the cashier with your gun sticking out. Even if it's legal now, the lady at the Home Goods register doesn't know that."

"Yeah, see, this is exactly why I shouldn't be doing this," Sanda said, her voice rising. "It sticks out like a sore thumb. Look at it. Let me see the three-eighty."

He took the Walther out and put the Glock back in. The Glock was slightly smaller and all black, and it disappeared better among her things. Sanda reorganized her purse again. Then she looked up at him.

"This doesn't have a safety, right?"

John was sure she already knew the answer, and he was a little irritated that she had asked. "Glock did an extensive study," John said, in his lecture voice. "They found that thumb safeties cause more problems than they solve. In a gunfight, officers would forget whether the gun was on safe or fire, and that half a second could get them killed. So Glocks don't have one. The safety is your finger. Keep your finger off the trigger and the gun won't go off."

"I want my gun to have a safety," Sanda said.

"Sanda—"

"You don't understand what it's like to have a gun in a purse. You don't carry a purse. There are keys in here. Pens. Things moving around. What if it falls out of the holster? What if the keys get in the trigger?" She held up the holster. "Do you have a holster with a strap? A button strap, that goes over the back, so it can't fall out?"

"No," John said. "I don't have anything like that."

"Okay," Sanda said. "I'm not doing this now." She took the holstered Glock out of her purse and set it down hard on the high-top. "Please go to the gun store and get me a holster with a button strap. Then maybe I'll think about carrying it."

"If you want a holster, you should come with me," John said, getting louder. "Otherwise whatever I buy, you'll have a problem with it, and I'll have to take it back."

"I don't have time for this shit!" Sanda shouted, grabbing her keys off the counter. "I have a million things to do! Just do it!"

It had been thirty-one minutes. John knew because he had looked at the clock when she first said *I'll be back later*. She stormed out the front door with her purse — gunless, mints and lemon sanitizer intact — and slammed it, and Charlie exploded off the couch and hit the door barking, high and furious, protesting her departure as he protested every departure.

"Charlie, no bark!" John said, pointing at him.

Charlie barked.

"Charlie. No. Bark."

Charlie barked again, with less conviction, and then, after John said it a third time, switched to the muffled half-bark he used when he wanted it on the record that he disagreed, a sort of *wuff* with his mouth closed, and lay down against the door with his chin on the floor to wait for her.

* * *

John slipped his Glock 43 into the right front pocket of his cargo shorts and drove to the gun store.

Sentinel Arms was in a long tan strip center on Highway 17-92 between a mattress outlet and a karate school, and inside it was bright and cold and smelled — he had been told — of gun oil and new carpet and the coffee that was always going in a pot behind the counter. There were glass cases in a long U around the room, full of pistols, and racks of rifles on the wall behind them, and a little range in the back where you could hear the steady flat pop of somebody's afternoon.

"Hey, John! How are you, man? It's been a while."

Trevor was behind the counter, where he always was. John sometimes suspected he lived there. They were not friends, exactly. They were something closer to what men his age called buddies when they did not want to say acquaintances: Trevor had sold him most of his rifles over the past two years, and had taught him a number of things about them that the internet had not, and they had stood at this counter and talked about guns for what added up, John figured, to several days of his life.

Trevor was somewhere in his thirties, about six feet tall, lean and ropy, the kind of lean that came from doing things rather than from going to a gym to pretend to do them. He had a short dark beard trimmed to a hard, clean line along his cheekbones, a sunburned neck, and pale gray eyes that were always a little amused. He wore a black polo shirt with the Sentinel Arms logo on the chest, tan tactical pants with a lot of pockets, and a sand-colored baseball cap with a pair of Oakley sunglasses pushed up on the brim, though they were indoors. On the inside of his left forearm was a tattoo of a compass rose, black and gray, the needle pointing north toward his elbow. He carried a Glock in a holster inside the front of his waistband, a little left of center.

"Your thing's on a truck," Trevor said, before John could say anything, and winked. "Should be here Monday, maybe Tuesday. I'll text you."

"That's not why I'm here."

"No?" Trevor leaned on the glass. "What do you need?"

"My wife wants to carry her Glock 42 in her purse," John said, leaning on it too. "But she's worried it'll slide out of the pocket holster. She wants something with a strap that goes over the back and snaps. A button strap. Do you have anything like that?"

"Let me look." Trevor came around the counter and went to a rack of holsters hanging in plastic packages and flipped through them, fast, the way a man flips through records he has known his whole life. He came back with one. "No button strap. Nobody makes a button strap for a 42 anymore. But this one's got a Velcro strap over the back, and it works just as good. I carried this exact holster for about a year." He popped it out of its package. "Want to see it on a 42?"

Trevor unlocked the case, took out a Glock 42, pulled back the slide and checked the chamber and the magazine well before handing it to John grip-first, the way he always did, every time, even with John. The little gun slid into the holster with a soft click, and the Velcro strap came over the back of the slide and pressed down, and the whole thing was smaller than the pocket holster John had at home.

"I think this works," John said. "What's the return policy if she hates it?"

"Keep the receipt and the packaging. Full refund."

"Okay. One more question." John hesitated, because he knew how it was going to sound. "What's the smallest gun you've got that has a thumb safety? For some reason she really wants a safety."

Trevor thought about it for a second, looking off at the case to his right. "M&P Bodyguard."

"Can I see it?"

Trevor was already unlocking the case. He took out a tiny black pistol, drew back the slide, checked it, and handed it over, and John held it in his palm and laughed out loud, because it nearly disappeared there. It was a .380, like the Glock 42, but shorter and thinner, and on the left side of the frame, just where his thumb fell, was a small lever.

"Thumb safety," Trevor said, pointing at it. "Up's safe, down's fire."

"This thing is tiny." John was already thinking of Sanda's sandwich-sized purse. "Will the holster fit it?"

Trevor handed him the Velcro holster. The Bodyguard slid into it as though it had been made for it.

"This is perfect for her," John said. "She's been talking about a gun just like this."

She had not been. But John did not need much of an excuse to add to his collection, and this was an excellent one, and it was standing on the glass in front of him in a Velcro holster.

"I'll take the holster and the Bodyguard," John said.

"Sounds like a plan," Trevor said, and reached under the counter for the long federal form that had to be filled out for every gun sold in America. "You know the drill."

John knew the drill. He took out his driver's license and his concealed weapons license and filled out the form at the counter, checking *No* in the long column of boxes about felonies and fugitives and addiction, while Trevor picked up the phone and called the state's background-check line and waited on hold. John wandered the aisles while he waited, looking at scopes he did not need and a display of camping stoves he did not look at. After a few minutes Trevor said, "Yes, go ahead," into the phone, and wrote a long approval number on the form, repeating each digit aloud. He wrote it left-handed, his hand curled over the top of the pen the way lefties do, so that he would not smear the ink.

Then he turned John's driver's license over and began copying the address onto the back of the form, a new procedure that management had started, and his pen stopped.

"Hey," Trevor said, looking up. "I know this address. John, I live in the same complex as you."

"You're shitting me."

"No shit. The Preserve, right? I'm one street over, I think. On Heron."

"That's crazy," John said, grinning. "Small world."

"Small world." Trevor finished copying the address and handed back the license. "I just put panels on my patio, actually. Solar. Got a battery bank in the garage. Waiting on a dish."

"A dish?"

"Satellite. For internet." Trevor shrugged, ringing up the Bodyguard. "In case."

"In case what?" John asked. "You getting ready for something?"

Trevor smiled at him, and the smile went all the way up into the pale gray eyes and stayed there.

"Man," Trevor said, "I'm always ready for something."

John paid and took the bag and drove home with the windows down, and he felt the way he had felt in the sixth grade when he found out that the coolest kid in school lived on his street. Trevor had panels. Trevor had a battery bank. Trevor was waiting on a dish. Trevor lived one street over, and was always ready for something, and John had nothing on his patio but two folding chairs and a dead basil plant.

* * *

Sanda came home at dusk with a bag in each hand and two more over her shoulder and her cheeks flushed with victory. She had found two small red velvet pillows for the couch, a red lamp shade with a gold lining, a pair of brass candle holders shaped like pinecones, and several other items that John could not identify and did not ask about. She also had, under one arm, a tiny tree, about two feet tall, in a burlap pot, already strung with miniature lights.

"For the guest bathroom," Sanda said, setting it on the counter.

"That's six," John said.

"That's festive," Sanda said.

He waited until she had arranged the pillows and replaced the lamp shade and stood back to look at them, and then he said, as casually as he could, "So I got your holster."

"Oh, you did?" Sanda turned around. "Let me see."

He took it out of the bag and popped it out of its package and slid her Glock 42 into it, still lying on the high-top where she had left it that morning, and pressed the strap down over the back.

"Sorry. They didn't have anything with a button strap. Only Velcro."

"Velcro," Sanda said. "Really? You know I hate Velcro."

He had known she would say that. He had been ready for it all afternoon. He demonstrated: the strap could go this way or that way; it could be adjusted; there was no possible way, none, that the gun could slide out of the holster in her purse by itself, he could turn it upside down and shake it, look. Sanda took it, and inspected the strap, and turned it upside down and shook it herself, and put it in her purse, and took it out, and put it back in.

"I can take it back," John said. "No problem."

"No," Sanda said slowly. "I think this will be fine."

"Well," John said, "they also had a really cool gun. Much smaller than the Glock. And it has a thumb safety."

"Really?" Sanda said, with an interest that took him completely by surprise. "I want to see it."

He took the little hard case out of the other bag and opened it on the counter. The Bodyguard lay in its foam like a piece of jewelry.

Sanda looked at it. She looked at him.

"You bought another fucking gun?" Sanda said, in the voice of a woman who has been on her feet since ten in the morning.

"It wasn't expensive," John said quickly. "And I think it's exactly what you've been looking for."

"I have never once been looking for a gun." She picked it up anyway, and turned it over, and he saw her eyebrows go up at how little it weighed. "Well. I do like the size. And it has a safety?"

"Right there. That little lever. Just like the one on the twenty-two, only smaller. Up's safe. Down's fire."

He moved the holster over to the Bodyguard, and it slid in perfectly, and the whole package fit in the palm of his hand. Sanda took it and put it in her purse, among the mints and the lemon, and looked down into the bag. With the purse wide open, the little black gun in its little black holster in the little black bag was nearly invisible. You could look right at it and not see a gun.

"I like it," Sanda said. "I don't think you should have bought another gun. But you're right. This one's perfect."

John began to smile.

"However," Sanda said, zipping the purse closed and pointing a finger at him, "there is no way in hell I am carrying this until I go to the range and practice with it. A lot."

"Fair enough," John said, and meant it. Little guns kicked hard. It had taken her months to get comfortable with the Glock. She would need range time, and range time with Sanda was his favorite thing in the world.

While she put the candle holders out, he told her about Trevor.

He told her about the panels on the patio and the battery bank in the garage and the satellite dish that was coming, and he told her that Trevor lived one street over, on Heron, and that he was the only person John knew who was actually ready, actually ready, for whatever came. He heard himself telling it, and he heard how excited he sounded, and he did not care.

Sanda listened, setting the brass pinecones on the dining table and turning them a quarter-inch at a time until they were right.

"Then why don't you talk to him about water," Sanda said, "instead of guns?"

John opened his mouth.

He did not have an answer. He stood there in the middle of the living room with nothing to say, while Sanda turned the second pinecone a quarter-inch and stood back to look at it. He never did talk to Trevor about water. He bought guns from him, and only guns, right up to the end.

* * *

That night, after she went to bed, John sat up in the recliner reading the news.

He was not looking for anything. He was scrolling the way people scroll, one thumb moving, eyes half closed. But there was a small item in one of his news apps about an airline that had suffered a brief dispatch outage on Friday afternoon, *resolved automatically*, no flights affected. He would not have noticed it except for the word *automatically*. He kept scrolling. In a different app, an hour later, he found a short piece from a trade publication about a pipeline operator on the Gulf Coast that had experienced a pressure anomaly on one of its main lines on Friday afternoon, which had *self-corrected* before any leak occurred. And in a third app, buried under a story about the actor and his wife, he found a regional bank in Ohio apologizing to its customers for an eleven-minute interruption to its card processing on Friday afternoon, *resolved by our automated systems*.

He went back and found all three and read them again, and this time he looked at the times.

The airline's outage had started at 2:14 p.m. Eastern. The pipeline's anomaly had begun at 2:16. The bank's interruption had started at 2:19. All three had been resolved by 2:25.

An airline, a pipeline, and a bank. Three industries that had nothing to do with each other, run by three companies that had nothing to do with each other, in three different states, on three different kinds of software. All of them had broken on the same Friday afternoon, inside the same eleven minutes. And all of them had healed themselves.

Not three accidents. One hand.

He opened the notes app on his phone and wrote down all three, with the times, and the names of the companies, and the word *automatically* three times, and he sat looking at it in the dark with the blue ring on the speaker glowing on the bookshelf and his heart going.

He thought that he might be the first person in the world to notice.

He was not. He was, as it turned out, somewhere around the four-hundredth. The other three hundred and ninety-nine had noticed on Friday, within minutes, and they had been sitting since Friday night in windowless rooms in Washington and Virginia and Maryland where no one was allowed to bring a phone, writing on paper, and none of them had been home since, and none of them were going to sleep that weekend either.
