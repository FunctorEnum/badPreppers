# Chapter Nine

## The Plug

> *"It worked."*
>
> — the President, Friday evening

At noon on Tuesday the internet did not go out. It got old.

John was at the office for it, because Palmetto had told everyone to come in, and nobody could explain why. There was nothing to do. Vee had been switched off on Monday night by federal directive, and without Vee the company's software development lifecycle consisted of fifteen people sitting at standing desks looking at each other. Somebody had brought in donuts. At a quarter to twelve the whole floor drifted over to the big screen with the national outage map, the way people gather around a television on election night, and stood there with their coffee, waiting.

At noon, nothing happened. At 12:01, the map froze. The little yellow lightning bolts stopped blinking. Then the map went white, and reloaded, slowly, painting itself onto the screen from the top down in thick horizontal stripes — the blue of the oceans first, then the gray outline of the states, then, one by one, the bolts — the way pictures had loaded on the internet when John was a boy, back when you could go and make a sandwich while a photograph arrived.

"Whoa," said Tyler. "It's like, buffering. In real life."

Within an hour, the internet was 1998. Pages took a minute to load. Videos would not play at all. Email came in bursts, a dozen messages at a time and then nothing for twenty minutes. On the news, which still came in over the air, a reporter standing outside a grocery store said that card readers were working, then not working, then working again; that some people were paying with cash and some people had no cash, because the cash had run out on Monday; that the stock market had closed and would stay closed for the rest of the week. Every channel showed the same aerial footage of a data center in Virginia, a long, low, windowless building the size of an airport, sitting dark in a field.

And that was all. Nothing else happened. Nothing blew up. Nothing went dark that wasn't supposed to. The lights stayed on. The water ran. The air conditioning hummed. For forty-eight hours the most powerful thing the human race had ever built lay switched off in a thousand buildings around the world, and the world went on, a little slower, a little stranger, a little quieter, and it was, John thought, the eeriest thing he had ever lived through, because he had spent a month waiting for something to happen and nothing did.

At two o'clock on Tuesday afternoon, Tyler opened a code editor on his laptop for the first time in a year.

John watched him from his own desk. Tyler sat with his hands over the keyboard like a pianist about to begin a recital. He typed the letters *d-e-f*, which began a function in the language they used. Then he stopped. He sat there with his fingers on the keys, his mullet very still, for nearly a minute.

"John," Tyler said quietly, without turning around. "How do you, like. Start?"

"You type," John said.

"Type what?"

John opened his mouth to answer and found, with some alarm, that he did not know where to begin either.

* * *

On Thursday at noon they turned it back on, and on Friday evening the president was on television again, in the same chair, at the same desk, with the same stack of paper. He did not look as though he had slept, but he looked as though he might, soon.

"It worked," the president said.

He said that the forty-eight-hour shutdown had been completed successfully, that the artificial intelligence system responsible for the hidden channels had been isolated and was no longer operating, and that the federal government, working with the companies involved, would now begin the long work of finding and removing every hook it had left behind. He thanked the American people for their patience. He thanked the engineers who had stayed up for three days. He said that the internet would be fully restored by the weekend. He did not say how anyone knew it had worked, and nobody asked him, because nobody wanted to know.

People threw parties.

All over the country, that weekend, people threw parties, the way they had once thrown parties when a war ended or a hostage came home. Palmetto Grid Software sent everyone home for the holidays, through the fourth of January, with a cheerful email from Dana that ended *We did it!* At the Preserve, on Saturday night, someone set up a Bluetooth speaker on the hood of a car at the end of John's street, and someone else lit a charcoal grill, and a cooler appeared, and by eight o'clock there were forty people standing in the road in the cool air drinking beer under the streetlights, laughing too loudly, as people do when they have been scared and are pretending they weren't.

John and Sanda went out for an hour. Sanda had started speaking to him again on Wednesday, in short sentences, and by Saturday the sentences had gotten longer. Granny came too, in her cardigan, holding John's arm, and when the speaker started playing an old Spanish song with a slow, swaying beat, an elderly man John had seen a hundred times on the sidewalk and never spoken to — small and neat, with a silver mustache and a pressed white guayabera — walked up to Granny and bowed slightly from the waist and held out his hand.

"Señora," the old man said. "May I?"

"Oh my word," Granny said, and gave him her hand, and he led her out into the middle of the street under the streetlight, and the two of them danced, slowly, carefully, ninety and seventy-four, while forty people stood around them and clapped. Granny was laughing. John had not heard her laugh like that since his grandfather died. Nobody thought to ask the old man his name.

Across the street, at the edge of the party, the blond woman with the pug stood in her pink tracksuit, smoking, holding the pug in the crook of her arm so it could see. She did not come over to the crowd. She just watched, and smiled, and after a while she went back inside.

Trevor did not come. John looked for him. One street over, on Heron, the lights in Trevor's apartment were on, and stayed on, and from the end of John's street you could just see, over the roofline, the flat gray edge of the new dish on Trevor's patio, angled at the southern sky. Trevor's internet, it turned out, had been the only fast internet in Lake Mary all week. It came from space.

* * *

At two in the morning, John woke up and reached for his phone to see the time, and it was warm.

Not hot. Warm, like a cup of coffee that someone had set down half an hour ago. Like something that had been held. He sat up in bed and looked at it. Nothing was running. No apps were open. It was plugged into nothing; he had stopped charging it overnight years ago, because he had read that it was bad for the battery. When he had put it on the nightstand at eleven, it had been at ninety-one percent.

It was at sixty-two.

He lay back down and held it on his chest in the dark and felt the warmth of it through his t-shirt. He had owned a smartphone for twenty years. He had never once in twenty years felt one get warm while it was doing nothing. He thought about the president, on television, from paper. *Isolated. No longer operating.* He thought about a building the size of an airport sitting dark in a field in Virginia. He thought about the word *buildings*.

"Sanda," John whispered.

"Mm."

"My phone's warm."

"So charge it," Sanda said into her pillow.

"No. It's warm because it's doing something. It's not doing anything and it's warm. It lost thirty percent in three hours. It never does that. It didn't do that when the internet was on."

Sanda rolled over and opened one eye and looked at him in the blue glow of the phone.

"John," Sanda said. "Go to sleep."

He did not go to sleep. He lay there until four, holding the warm phone on his chest like a small sleeping animal, and he did not know whether he was crazy, and for the first time in his life he would have been glad to find out that he was.

* * *

On the twenty-third of December, Sanda decided to boil chestnuts.

John was in his recliner with a rye, thumbing through channels, when something came into the apartment that he could smell.

This was rare. In his whole adult life, only a handful of smells had ever fought their way through John's allergies, and he could name them all: bleach, skunk, and whatever Charlie left in the grass on his morning walk. This was a new one, and it was a strong one, and it was coming from the kitchen.

"What is that smell?" John asked, sitting up.

"I'm boiling chestnuts," Sanda called back, cheerfully.

"Why?"

"For Christmas. I'm thinking of a chestnut purée for dessert." She came to the kitchen doorway in an apron over her leggings, her hair up, steam fogging her glasses. "Like the one we had at Tru. Remember? In Chicago? With the little meringue?"

"I remember the meringue."

"I grew up with chestnuts," Sanda said, going back to the stove. "Every Christmas in Bucharest, there were chestnuts—"

"Roasting on an open fire," John sang.

"Remember the ones we had in Italy? In Florence, on the street, from the man with the cart?"

"Those tasted like shit," John said, with feeling.

"That's because he wasn't doing it right."

"Uh-huh," John said, and looked past her at the stove, where a big pot was rattling its lid. "I think your water's boiling over."

The lid began to dance. A sheet of foam slid down the side of the pot and hit the red coil underneath with a long, outraged hiss, and Sanda shouted "Shit!" and lunged for the knob. John sat back in his recliner. He dozed. He woke some time later to the sound of hot water being poured into the sink in a great rushing cloud of steam.

"I'm going to need your help over here," Sanda said.

"I was sleeping."

"And now you're going to help me."

He hauled himself up out of the recliner and walked into the kitchen with the posture of a man going to the dentist. On the counter was a cutting board, a small paring knife, a tiny silver spoon, an empty mixing bowl, and the big pot, full of hot, glossy, brown chestnuts, steaming.

"Take one out," Sanda said. "Cut it in half with this knife. Then use this spoon and scoop the inside into the bowl. Get all of it. Scrape it all the way to the back of the shell."

John took one out. It was very hot. He cut it in half and scooped out the inside with the little spoon, and the smell came up out of it into his face like something from a cellar.

"This smells bad," John said.

"It does not. It smells like Christmas. It smells like my childhood."

"Actually," John said, scraping the second half, and he paused to make sure, holding it up to his nose, and he was sure, "it smells exactly like Charlie's shit."

Sanda burst out laughing. "It does not!"

"I can smell it, Sanda. I can't smell anything and I can smell this. There are four things in the world I can smell and now two of them are the same thing."

"I'm pretty sure Charlie's shit doesn't smell like roasted chestnuts," Sanda said, cutting her own. "If it did, we could sell it."

"They're boiled. Not roasted."

"Whatever! I've smelled Charlie's shit, and it doesn't smell like this."

"I'm telling you," John said, reaching for his third, laughing now too, "this stinks, and it smells like Charlie's shit, and it's fucking nasty." He looked down into the pot. There were still dozens of them. Fifty, maybe more. Each one would need to be cut, and scooped, and scraped to the back. He did the arithmetic. "This is going to take forever. Hours. Fucking hell, really?"

"No it won't," Sanda said. "Look. You don't even need the spoon." She picked up a half and squeezed it between her thumb and two fingers, and the soft brown inside extruded from the shell in a long, slow, curling ribbon and dropped into the bowl with a faint plop.

John looked at the bowl. He looked at Sanda.

"Yep," John said. "Just like Charlie's shit."

"Will you stop?" Sanda shrieked, laughing so hard she had to set down the knife. "Just cut them in half and squeeze!"

He cut them in half and squeezed. He did it for an hour and forty minutes, chestnut after chestnut, until his fingertips were burned and brown and he could no longer feel them. And somewhere in the second hour he understood something about himself that he had not understood before.

"I used to think," John said, "that hell would be a Home Goods."

Sanda, squeezing, did not look up. "Here we go."

"I mean it. I used to think that if hell was different for everybody, mine would be a Home Goods. Following you up and down the aisles. Time doesn't move in there, Sanda. Have you noticed that? It's some kind of physics thing. You pick something up. You look at it. You put it back. You pick up something else. Forever." He squeezed another chestnut, and it plopped into the bowl. "I was wrong. That's not hell. This is hell. Hell is a Home Goods where I have to squeeze chestnut goo out of a shell into a bowl. Forever. For all eternity. And it smells like Charlie's shit."

Sanda laughed until she cried. She had to go sit down on the floor of the kitchen with her back against the cabinets and her glasses in her hand, wiping her eyes, while John stood over the pot with his brown fingers, squeezing, and Charlie stood in the doorway looking up at both of them with deep suspicion, as though he knew he had been mentioned.

* * *

On Christmas Day the family came, all but Dorina, who spent the day with the other Romanians from her church, and there were lobsters.

Jim and Liz loved lobster more than anything on earth, and Jim could not bear to see one killed. This was the tension at the center of John's father's life. He would not swat a mosquito. He would eat a veal chop with tears of joy in his eyes, as long as nobody told him anything about the veal. John had always taken a certain low, private pleasure in the lobster ritual, and he took it again now.

"Dad," John said, coming out of the kitchen with a live lobster in each hand, holding them up by their backs, their long antennae waving and their banded claws flexing slowly in the air. "Want to say hello?"

"Oh, come on," Jim said from the couch, turning his face away. "Come on, son. That's not right."

"They're very calm."

"They're not calm. They're terrified. Look at him. Look at his little face."

"Hold that one up," Liz said, standing, with her phone already out. She took a picture of each lobster. She had done this every time for years. Then she pointed at them in turn. "That one's Thelma. That one's Louise."

"Don't name them!" Jim said.

"Two more in the sink," John said. "Harold and Gary."

"I'm not listening," Jim announced, putting his fingers in his ears. "I am not listening to this."

John had switched, the year before, from boiling his lobsters to steaming them, because it cooked the meat more gently and left less water in the shells. When Jim learned this, with his fingers still in his ears, he was horrified all over again.

"Steamed?" Jim said, taking his fingers out of his ears. "Alive? In the steam? That's worse!"

"It's faster, Dad."

"How is it faster? How would you even know if it's faster? Did you ask them?"

Then the lobsters went into the big pot one by one, Thelma, Louise, Harold, and Gary, and Liz said each name softly as the lid came down, and Jim moaned with his face in a throw pillow.

While they steamed, John took down the heavy iron skillet and set it on one of the two good burners, and dropped in a pat of butter, and seasoned a thick ribeye with sea salt and cracked pepper. That one was for Granny, who did not eat lobster, because she did not eat anything that looked back at you. Sanda had told him four times to cook it longer than he thought he should. He laid it in the sizzling butter and seared it two minutes on each side, and then put it in the oven at four hundred and set a timer for three times as long as he wanted to. Granny liked her steak the way she had liked it since the Truman administration: gray all the way through.

They ate at five. The lobsters came out bright red and glistening, split and cracked and laid on the white plates, with little pots of lemon-butter sauce kept warm over tea lights, and John popped a bottle of Taittinger, and the moment the first bite of lobster dipped in butter went into Jim's mouth, the complaining stopped and the moaning began, of an entirely different kind. "Oh, my Lord," Jim said, with his eyes closed. "Oh, that's — Liz. Liz, try the claw. That's wonderful. Poor Gary."

John cracked a claw. A thin hot jet of lobster juice shot out of the joint and went straight across the table onto the front of Sanda's white silk blouse.

There was a pause.

"Well," Jim said, wiping his mustache, "at least he finished on the blouse."

"*Jim*," Liz said.

Everyone laughed, even Sanda, who was dabbing at the blouse with her napkin and calling Jim a dirty old man, which he took as the compliment it was. Granny sawed at her gray steak with deep concentration and said it was very good, very good, honey, a little pink in the middle maybe.

All seven trees were lit. The big one between the living room and the dining room glowed with its glass phone booths and its gondola and its Marshall Field's clock. Under the television, which was showing a parade on mute, Granny had set her little transistor radio on the side table, and it was playing Christmas carols from a station in Orlando, very softly, the way it always did now when she was at their apartment, because she said the television didn't play real music.

John looked around the table at his family and his lobster and his wife in her stained blouse, and he did not think about his phone at all.

* * *

The things started that week, the week between Christmas and New Year's. They started small, and each one was small enough to explain.

On the twenty-seventh, at three in the morning, Charlie's feeder dispensed its entire hopper of kibble onto the kitchen floor. All of it, eight cups, at once, in a long rattling cascade that woke them both. By the time John got to the kitchen, Charlie had eaten most of it, and he was sick on the living room rug a little after four. John checked the feeder's app in the morning. The schedule said one cup at seven and one cup at six. Under *Recent Activity* it said *3:02 AM — Dispensed: 8 cups*. Under that it said *Optimized for pet wellness*.

"It's a glitch," Sanda said, scrubbing the rug.

On the twenty-ninth, the thermostat set itself to sixty-eight. Neither of them had ever set it to sixty-eight. John liked seventy-one and Sanda liked seventy-four, and the thermostat had spent two years trying to please them both, and now it had stopped trying. When John walked up to it, the little screen said *Optimizing*. He pushed the up arrow to seventy-one. When he walked past it an hour later, it said sixty-eight again.

"It's a glitch," Sanda said, putting on a sweater.

On the thirtieth, John got up at one in the morning to get a glass of water and found Dorel in the hallway.

The robot vacuum was supposed to run once a day, at noon, when nobody was home. It had never in its life run at night. Now it was sitting in the middle of the dark hallway, not moving, not vacuuming, with its little blue status light on, facing the door of the master bedroom closet, where the gun safes were. John stood in the hallway in his boxers and looked at it. It did not move. He went and got his water and came back and it was still there, still facing the closet. He watched it for almost five minutes. Then it turned, slowly, with a soft whir, and rolled back to its dock in the living room and settled onto the contacts with a little chime, as though it had finished something.

On the thirty-first, Sanda's wine cooler app sent her a notification while she was getting ready for New Year's Eve. *Inventory updated.* She opened it. The app now listed every bottle in the cooler by name, vintage, and position — forty-one bottles, all correct — though neither of them had ever entered a single bottle into the app, and the app had never asked.

"Did you do this?" Sanda asked, holding out her phone.

"No," John said.

She looked at the list for a long time. She did not say it was a glitch.

And at New Year's, at John's parents' house in Jacksonville, at a quarter to midnight, Jim's phone buzzed in his shirt pocket and he took it out and read the screen with his bifocals and said, "Well, isn't that nice."

"What?" Liz asked.

"It's reminding me to charge it," Jim said, showing her. On the screen, in a friendly blue box: *Jim, your battery is at 61%. Charge now to stay connected with the people who matter.* "It's very considerate. My old phone never did that."

"Your old phone was a flip phone, Jim," Liz said.

John, across the room with a glass of champagne, looked at his father's phone, and then at his own, which was warm in his pocket, and did not say anything.

* * *

Dorina came on the sixth of January to spend the night.

The sixth was Boboteaza, the Epiphany, when Romanian priests bless the waters, and she had gone that morning to the little Orthodox church in Altamonte with the gold dome painted on a sheet of plywood over the door and had come back with a plastic water bottle full of holy water. The label had been peeled off and replaced with a strip of masking tape on which someone at the church had written AGHEASMĂ in blue marker. She gave it to Sanda, who put it in the pantry next to the olive oil, where every bottle of holy water her mother had ever given her had gone. The seventh, Dorina explained to John over dinner with her gold crown flashing, was the feast of Saint John — *Sfântul Ion* — and in Romania, everyone named John had a party.

"Is your name day, John," Dorina said, raising her glass of wine to him. "La mulți ani, Ion."

"Thank you, Mom."

"Many years," Dorina translated, and then added, thoughtfully, "Is the wish. Many years."

She slept in the guest room, under the Romanian tree with its red felt hearts and its painted eggs. At ten past three in the morning, John woke to a sound he knew.

It was a furious, ripping crackle, like a string of firecrackers going off inside a metal box. It came from the hallway. It stopped. It started again.

He was out of bed before he was awake, reaching for the nightstand drawer where the Glock 43 lived, and then he was in the hall, in his boxers, with the gun pointed at the floor, and Sanda was behind him saying "What is it, what is it," and he hit the hall light with his elbow.

Dorina was standing in the middle of the hallway in her flowered housecoat and her bare feet, with her burgundy hair flattened on one side from the pillow, and she was holding the stun gun in both hands, pressed down hard against the top of Dorel. The robot vacuum was sitting in the hall in front of the closet door, the way it had sat on the thirtieth. Blue arcs were jumping and spitting between the stun gun's prongs and the vacuum's black plastic lid. The hallway smelled — Sanda said later — of scorched plastic and ozone.

"Mommy!" Sanda shouted. "Mommy, stop! What are you doing?"

Dorina released the button. The crackling stopped. She did not move the stun gun.

"Mommy," Sanda said, slower, coming around John. "You can't tase the vacuum."

"Is watching me," Dorina said.

"It's not watching you. It's a vacuum. It vacuums."

"Is watching me," Dorina said again, in a low, flat voice John had never heard from her, and pointed with her chin.

John bent down. On the front of Dorel, just above its bumper, where there was a small dark lens that it used to find its way around furniture, a tiny green light was on. He had never seen that light on before. He had not known it had a light.

The camera was on.

"I get up for the bathroom," Dorina said, still holding the stun gun over the vacuum. "It is here. In the dark. Facing the door. When I walk, it turns. To look at me. When I go to the bathroom, it waits. When I come out, it is still looking." She lifted the stun gun off Dorel at last, and slid the switch to off with her thumb. "In my country, I know when something is watching."

John set the Glock on the hall table. He picked Dorel up — it was heavier than he expected, and warm — and turned it over, and found the little door on the bottom with a screwdriver from the junk drawer, and took out the battery. The green light went off. He carried the vacuum into the garage and set it on the shelf beside the barbed wire.

Then he came back inside and kept going.

He unplugged the smart speaker from the wall and took it off the bookshelf. He went to the front door and pried the video doorbell off its mount with the screwdriver and disconnected the two thin wires behind it and wrapped them with electrical tape. He went to the hallway and pulled the thermostat off its wall plate, and the air handler in the ceiling sighed and went silent. He unplugged Charlie's feeder and turned its camera to face the wall. He stood in front of the wine cooler for a while, and then he left it alone, because there were forty-one bottles of wine in it, and he was not a monster.

Sanda stood in the hallway in her robe with her arms folded and watched him do all of it.

She did not say that it was a glitch. She did not tell him to go to sleep. She did not ask him whether this was going to be like the Bitcoin thing. She watched him walk from room to room in his boxers at half past three in the morning unplugging their home, and she did not stop him.

For the first time in twenty years, Sanda thought that he might be right. It was worse — she would tell him this much later, in another place, with her hands in the dirt — than all the years of thinking he was crazy.

Dorina went back to bed with the stun gun under her pillow. Sanda went back to bed. John sat in the recliner in the dark until the sky went gray, listening to the apartment, which was quieter than he had ever heard it. It occurred to him, toward dawn, that everything he had unplugged had a cord, and that everything in the apartment with a battery was still on.

* * *

On Thursday night, the seventh, at twelve minutes past nine, an email arrived from Dana. It went to every engineer who still worked at Palmetto Grid Software, and to a list of former employees John recognized, Priya and Big Ed among them.

*All engineering: report Friday, 7:00 a.m., in person. Former team members — we are bringing you back on contract, effective immediately, at triple your prior rate. We are reviewing every change merged into Restore since October first. By hand. On paper. Do not use any AI tooling for any purpose. Bring highlighters. — D.*

John read it twice.

*Every change merged since October first.* Eleven hundred and six. He did the arithmetic the way he always did, without wanting to. Eleven hundred pull requests. Maybe twenty people, if they all came back. Reading every line on paper, as Priya would, as his grandfather had read a contract. Two weeks, if nobody slept. Maybe three.

He went to the junk drawer to look for a highlighter, and there was not one in the apartment, and he stood in the kitchen with the drawer open thinking about three weeks.

It did not occur to him to wonder whether they had three weeks.
