# Chapter Eighteen

## The Road

> *NO BUS*
>
> — hand-painted sign, bus stop, Lake Mary Boulevard

He broke his own rule before breakfast.

Someone had to stay with Granny and Dorina and the guns. So at first light on Friday, John and his father went alone, with the Sig and the shotgun and two cooler bags of empty Ziplocs. Sanda stood in the doorway with her rifle and watched them go and did not say *I thought we weren't splitting up*, which was worse than if she had.

Every window of the Publix was broken. Shopping carts lay on their sides across the lot like dead animals, and plastic bags blew along the asphalt. A car at the end of the lot had burned down to its frame. In front of the Publix doors there were long dark stains on the pavement, and John looked at them once and did not look again. There were no bodies. Someone had taken them away. There were no people at all.

"There it is," Jim said quietly.

Behind a row of hedges at the far end of the lot, the motor home was still there.

The door had a dent in the middle of it the size of a man's boot. The huge curved windshield, the face of the whale, was smashed in; it hung in the frame in a sagging white web, and glass was everywhere. Jim stood in front of it with his hands at his sides and looked at it the way a man looks at a house after a hurricane.

"Well," Jim said finally. "They broke out the windshield."

The door was jammed in its frame. John braced his feet and pulled with everything he had, and it came open with a pop. Inside, every cabinet stood open. The refrigerator was empty. The drawers had been dumped on the floor. And all three of the televisions were gone — the one over the dash, the one over the dinette, the one in the bedroom — torn right off their mounts, with the wires hanging out of the walls.

"They took the TVs," Jim said, staring at the empty mounts. "In the middle of all this. Somebody carried off three televisions."

"Where are they going to plug them in?" John said.

Jim brushed the glass off the driver's seat with his cap and sat down and put the key in the ignition and turned it. The big engine coughed once and roared to life. John went to the galley sink and lifted the faucet handle, and water came out. He turned it off fast.

"They didn't find the tank," John said.

The little red pin on Jim's phone had said *Water available*, and somebody had come in the dark and kicked in the door and found no water anywhere they knew to look, and taken the televisions instead. The seventy gallons had been under their feet the whole time.

They filled all eight Ziplocs. Then John stood in the aisle and looked out through the broken windshield at the dead gate across the road.

"Dad," John said. "You have jumper cables?"

"Of course I have jumper cables."

"Can you drive this thing over there? Right up to the gate?"

Jim did not ask why. He swung the motor home around the lot, glass sliding off the dash, and across the empty road and up to the entrance of the Preserve, and stopped with the bumper a foot from the closed steel gate.

* * *

The steel box beside the gate still had no cover. The green Halcyon board was still bolted to the back of it, its little light dead. John did not look at the board. He looked below it, at the gate's old motor, the fat gray one that had been there before Halcyon and would be there after, with its two big brass terminals.

"I'm not going to ask the controller," John said. "I'm going to ask the motor." He looked up at his father, half laughing. "You wouldn't happen to have a spool of wire?"

"No," Jim said, thinking. "But I've got an extension cord. And a pair of scissors."

That was good enough. John cut a foot off each end of the cord, split the wires, and stripped them with his teeth. Jim popped the motor home's hood and clamped the jumper cables onto the battery.

"We're live," Jim called.

John knelt in front of the steel box with the other ends of the jumper cables in his hands, careful to keep the red and black clamps apart. He clipped one short wire to the black clamp and one to the red. He held the black wire against the motor's ground terminal and pressed it there with his thumb. Then he touched the red one to the other terminal, quickly.

There was a fat blue spark and a crack. The motor jumped and hummed for half a second and stopped when he pulled away.

He took a breath. He pressed the red wire back onto the terminal and held it there.

The motor groaned. It was a twenty-four-volt motor and he was feeding it twelve, and it did not like it, and it said so, grinding, slow. But it turned. The big steel arm of the gate shuddered and began to swing open, at half speed, grumbling, toward him, past him, until it stood open all the way.

John pulled the wires away. The gate stayed open.

"Well, I'll be damned," Jim said, standing in the road with his hands on his hips.

John knelt there and looked at the open gate. It was, he would think later, the only thing he did that entire winter that was simply and completely right. He had looked into a box of wires one morning in December because he was the kind of man who could not walk past an open box of wires, and he had remembered what he saw, and now his family could leave.

"Take the cables off," John called, grinning. "Let's bring this beast in."

* * *

Jim drove the motor home through the open gate and down their street at five miles an hour, glass tinkling off the dashboard, and stopped in front of the apartment, and Liz saw it through the peephole.

"Oh my God!" Liz shouted. "They're here! They've got the motor home! It doesn't have a windshield!"

"We need to pack," John said, coming through the door. "Fast. Only what we need. This thing's going to draw attention."

What each of them considered essential turned out to be a fairly complete portrait of each of them.

John carried out thirty-one guns. The handguns went in a duffel bag; the rifles he stacked in the hallway of the motor home like firewood. He did not carry out any food. Sanda noticed this. She did not say anything. She took a box and filled it with the cans and the jerky and Trevor's rice and beans and the case of canned chicken herself.

Sanda carried out a cardboard box of wine glasses, wrapped one at a time in dish towels — only the Pinot Noir glasses, she said, because they were the most versatile, and she was being practical. She carried out both French presses. She went to the big tree in the corner between the living room and the dining room and stood in front of it for a moment and unhooked the gold glass Marshall Field's clock from its branch, front and center, and wrapped it in a napkin and put it in her jacket pocket, the left one, the one without the gun. Then she turned and looked at John, standing in the doorway with an armful of AKs, for one beat longer than necessary. Then she went back into the kitchen and carried out every pitcher they owned, all thirteen, the rooster last.

Granny packed her transistor radio and her Sucrets tin of batteries and a cardboard church fan on a wooden stick, with a picture of Jesus in a garden on one side and an advertisement for a funeral home in Jacksonville on the other.

Dorina packed the stun gun, and a jar of gogonele.

Liz packed her reading glasses, which were already around her neck on their beaded chain, and a spiral notebook from Home Goods with a cover printed all over with little yellow pineapples, and two pens.

Jim packed his phone charger.

"For when it comes back," Jim said, when everybody looked at him.

* * *

The ammunition was a problem. There was more of it than there was of anything else, in heavy steel cans and cardboard cases, and John stood in the bedroom looking at it and doing arithmetic.

"Dad," John said. "This is a long shot. You don't have a hand cart in the motor home?"

"Nope. Got one at home." Jim scratched his head under his cap. "There was one in that fella's garage, though. Red one."

"I'll get it," John said. "From Shithole's apartment."

He jogged over to Heron in the sun. Through the jagged hole of Trevor's front window he could see the shape under the gray throw blanket. The apartment smelled of death — he could smell it; he wished he couldn't — and he took the red hand cart from the garage and got out as fast as he had ever gotten out of anywhere.

It took ten trips. John loaded the ammunition on the cart at the apartment door and rolled it to the motor home, and his father stood in the doorway and took each box from him and set it somewhere in the motor home, and then moved it, and then moved it again, trying to keep the weight even side to side, like a man packing the car for a trip to the beach and worrying about the tires.

On the eighth trip, Jim looked down at the long black case in the hallway of the motor home, the one John had wheeled out on the hand cart by itself.

"What do you plan to do with *this* thing?" Jim asked.

John looked at the Barrett case. And then, slowly, his face changed.

"Actually," John said, "I just remembered. I bought a vehicle mount for it."

Jim looked at his son for a long moment.

"Of course you did," Jim said.

* * *

"Is that a skylight?" John asked, looking up at the ceiling of the motor home. "Does it open?"

"It's a roof vent. Swivel that lock to the side and push up. It's on hinges."

"That's perfect," John said. "That's actually perfect."

"Care to fill me in?"

"We're about to drive into God knows what," John said. "So we need to turn this thing into something that can protect itself. I'm going to mount the Barrett on the roof, next to that hatch. With the mount, the recoil goes into the roof, not into you." He looked at his father. "Once it's up there, anybody can shoot it. Hell, even Granny could shoot it."

"I heard that," Granny said from the couch, where she was sitting with her radio and her church fan and the pug.

The vehicle mount was still in its box, never opened. Inside was a folded sheet: *Installation Instructions*. Six pages of small print. He held it out at the end of his arm, and then farther, and squinted with his one good eye, and could not read a word. He had not read printed instructions in more than ten years.

"Mom," John said, without looking up. "Can I borrow your glasses?"

Liz lifted the beaded chain over her head and held them out to him. "Bring them back," she said.

He put them on. The words came up off the page like something surfacing in a pond. *Step 1. Locate the mounting surface.* He read all six pages, one step at a time, the way his grandfather had taught him to read a contract. Then he took his cordless drill — the last charged battery, as far as he knew, in Lake Mary — up the ladder on the back of the motor home and installed the mount beside the hatch. It took twice as long as he expected.

"Dad. Hand me the Barrett."

Jim grunted as he fed the thirty-pound rifle up through the hatch, and John settled it into the mount and locked it in. It sat on the roof above the shattered windshield like a figurehead on the prow of a ship.

He loaded the ten .50 BMG cartridges he had bought for educational purposes into one of the big steel magazines, and screwed a small step ladder to the floor under the hatch, and climbed it. Standing on the top step, the top half of his body was out on the roof. He leaned to his right. His hands fell on the rifle exactly where they needed to be. He checked the safety. He seated the magazine. He pulled back the charging handle and let it go, and it slammed home.

"Barrett is ready to go," John called down through the hatch. "Chambered. Safety on."

He climbed down and stood in the aisle and looked at his family.

"Dad. You're driving. You've got the shotgun and the forty." Jim nodded. "Sanda, you've got your AR and the Bodyguard." Sanda nodded. "Mom. Your AR, and my Glock 26." Liz nodded. "Dorina, you've got the twenty-two." Dorina held up the .22 and the stun gun together, like a woman holding up two winning tickets. "I've got the Sig, and the forty-five, and the .308 for distance." He looked at Granny, sitting on the couch with the pug and the radio and the church fan.

"Granny," John said. "You've got the radio."

"I've got the radio," Granny agreed.

"Everybody ready?"

Everybody was ready. Liz came out of the apartment last, carrying Charlie under one arm and the pug under the other, and set them down inside the motor home, and the two dogs went straight to the shower stall at the back, where Sanda had put down a blanket, and curled up together in it as though they had been born there.

* * *

Jim drove them out at five miles an hour, the long way around the complex, because the motor home could not turn around on their street. They went past the dumpster with the bags piled around it, and past the place where Sarah had been, which was only a dark stain now on the asphalt. They went past a car parked in a driveway on Egret with a man sitting in the driver's seat. The driver's window was cracked open an inch, and the inside of the window was painted dark red, and in the middle of the glass was a hole about the size of a quarter. Nobody said anything. John wondered how many of the people in the Preserve had walked out, and how many had simply stayed inside, and waited, and waited.

At number eleven, Sanda said, "Stop."

She got out with a box. In it were the Korean grill and the last green can of propane, and half of Trevor's beans, and half of the case of canned chicken. She set it on Mr. Alvarez's front step and knocked twice and got back into the motor home, and as they pulled away, the blinds in Mr. Alvarez's front window parted, and a small hand in a white sleeve lifted, and stayed lifted until they were around the corner.

They drove out through the open gate. On the main road, they passed the bus stop where the two men had stood on Monday morning. Nobody was there. Someone had taken a piece of brown cardboard from a box and written on it, in black paint, in big careful letters, and taped it to the pole under the sign: NO BUS.

* * *

At forty miles an hour, with no windshield, the wind came into the motor home like a fist and did not let go. Nobody could hear anybody. Jim drove with his cap jammed down to his ears and his eyes slitted. John sat in the passenger chair with the Sig across his lap. Sanda rode in the dinette with her rifle. Granny sat on the couch holding her hair down with one hand.

"We should go to Jacksonville!" Jim shouted, over the wind. "The house! Our house!"

"A million people," John shouted back. "No water! It'll be worse than here!"

"The house has city water!" Liz shouted.

"Exactly!"

"I'm going home," Granny said.

She did not shout it. She said it in her ordinary voice, from the couch, and somehow they all heard it over the wind, and that was the end of the discussion.

They went west on 46 and north on 441, through little lake towns that were dead in a way John had never seen a town be dead. People walked along the shoulder carrying jugs and children. People sat on porches and watched the motor home go by with the rifle on its roof, and nobody waved.

North of Ocala, on the ramp to I-75, there were trucks across the road.

They were the old trucks, the canvas-backed ones, three of them, parked in a V to make a chute, and in front of them stood a sergeant with a clipboard. He was in his fifties, heavyset, with a gray mustache and reading glasses on a cord around his neck, wearing an old olive-drab field jacket over his uniform that looked as if it had come out of the same depot as the trucks. His name tape said PETTAWAY. Behind him six young soldiers in helmets stood with rifles.

Jim stopped. He held his badge out the side window before the sergeant could ask for it.

"Retired, Jacksonville Sheriff's Office," Jim said. "Thirty-five years. This is my family. My mother's ninety. We're going to her home place in Alabama."

The sergeant looked at the badge and handed it back. He walked slowly along the side of the motor home, looking in the windows at the rifles, at Granny with her church fan, at the two dogs in the shower stall.

Then he stopped, and looked up. He looked at the Barrett on the roof for a long, long moment.

"Sir," Sergeant Pettaway said. "Is that a…"

"Yes," Jim said.

The sergeant looked at it for another moment. Then he looked at Jim. Then he looked at his clipboard, and decided, visibly, that there was not a box on it for this.

"…Drive safe," Sergeant Pettaway said, and waved them through.

* * *

They ran low on gas outside Lake City.

There was an old blue Ford pickup from the nineties dead on the shoulder, nose-down in the ditch. Jim pulled over beside it.

"Modern cars have anti-siphon valves in the filler neck," John said, climbing down with the hose they had found in the motor home's storage bay and Trevor's red gas can. "You can't get a hose past them. I saw a video."

"Old trucks don't," Jim said. "Kids used to siphon out of these all the time at the Regency Mall. We'd catch 'em three a night."

John fed the hose down into the truck's tank until he felt it hit bottom, and put the gas can on the ground below the tank, and put the end of the hose in his mouth, and sucked.

Nothing happened. He sucked harder, as hard as he had ever sucked on anything in his life, and the gasoline came up the hose all at once like a snake out of a hole and filled his mouth.

He spat and coughed and spat on his knees on the shoulder of Interstate 75, while the gas poured out of the hose into the red can exactly the way it was supposed to.

His father stood over him with his hands in his pockets.

"Four thousand hours of YouTube," Jim said, "and none of it was this."

* * *

They spent the night in a church parking lot in south Georgia, outside a little town called Quitman, behind a white wooden Baptist church with a short steeple.

Every light in the town was out. But there were people inside the church, singing in the dark, a hymn Granny said they had sung on the riverbank when she was a girl, *Shall we gather at the river, where bright angel feet have trod*. Nobody inside had a light to read a hymnal by. Nobody needed one. They had known the words their whole lives.

Granny sat on the step of the motor home in the dark with her church fan and listened to the whole thing, all four verses, without moving.

Afterward, she took her radio out of her housecoat pocket and turned it on and found the station, and they all came and sat around her on the steps and in the doorway in the dark. The voice was not the president this time. It was a man reading from paper, flatly. Deaths in the cold in the northern cities, which he said were *estimated in the hundreds of thousands*. Hospitals operating on isolated generators. Water distribution points opening in every state. And then: *Coordinated network activity was detected yesterday in eleven states, down from forty-one states on the first day. Officials describe the trend as encouraging.*

"See," Jim said quietly, in the dark. "It's working."

John looked up at the sky, which was so full of stars it looked like spilled salt.

"Yeah, Dad," John said.

* * *

On Saturday morning, twenty miles from the dirt road, there was a truck across the highway.

It was a two-lane blacktop running straight through planted pine, flat and empty, with the trees close on both sides like the walls of a hallway. The truck was a silver Chevy Silverado, a newer one, crew cab, parked crossways across both lanes. A man was standing in front of it in the road, waving a red plastic gas can over his head with both arms, like a man flagging down a plane. He was skinny, in a camouflage jacket and a camouflage cap, with a scraggly red beard, and even from a quarter mile away John could see him smiling.

"He's out of gas," John said, leaning forward. "Dad, slow down. We've got gas. We've got a can. We can give him—"

He had learned, somewhere between the bus stop and his own front door, to want to stop. He did not know when. He wanted to stop.

Jim stopped. But he stopped short. He stopped a hundred and fifty yards back, in the middle of the road, and put the motor home in park, and did not take his hands off the wheel.

"That's a setup," Jim said.

"How do you—"

"Because I've seen it a hundred times." Jim's voice was very quiet and very level. "Truck across both lanes so you can't go around. One fella out front, friendly, waving. Something you'll want to give him. Look at the trees, son. Both sides. Look at the trees right at the truck."

John looked at the trees.

The tree line opened up.

It was short and loud and ugly and nothing about it was glorious. The pines on both sides blinked with yellow flashes, and the front of the motor home began to ring and pop like a tin roof in a hailstorm. The man with the gas can dropped it and ran for the truck.

"*Down!*" John shouted. "Everybody *down!*"

Sanda and Liz were already down, between the front seats, on their knees, and then they were up again, with the barrels of their rifles resting on the dashboard where the windshield used to be, and firing. John fired the Sig out of the empty windshield frame at the flashes in the trees on the left, and could not tell if he hit anything. Jim was flat across the front seats with his hands over his head. Something punched through the side of the motor home behind John's head and went out the other side. Dorina was on the floor of the aisle with the two dogs pulled under her, covering them with her body.

Something hit John in the left shoulder.

It felt like a red-hot iron bar swung by a strong man. It knocked him sideways into the dash. He did not fall. He sat there looking at it. Blood was coming out through his jacket, high on the shoulder, quickly, more than he would have thought.

"*John!*" Sanda screamed.

"I'm okay," John said, and he was, more or less. He was conscious. He was surprised to find he was conscious. "I'm okay. Keep shooting."

Nobody told Granny to do anything.

Nobody would remember afterward seeing her get up off the couch. She just did. She was ninety years old and she was not going to be shot at in her own son's motor home. She set the pug down on the couch and the church fan beside it, and she walked down the aisle between the boxes of ammunition and climbed the three steps of the ladder under the roof hatch, one at a time, holding on with both knotted hands, the way she climbed the steps of her church.

At the top step, the upper half of her was out on the roof, in the wind and the noise. She put her hands where she had seen John put them. She leaned to her right. She looked down the long barrel at the silver truck.

She pushed the safety the way she had seen him push it.

She fired once.

The sound of it was not like a gunshot. It was like a building falling down. The whole motor home rocked on its springs. And a hundred and fifty yards down the road, the front of the silver Silverado came apart. The hood flew up and folded in half. The engine block cracked open like a dropped melon, and black oil and green coolant and white steam blew out of it in every direction, and one of the front wheels sagged sideways, and the truck settled down onto the road on its front end like a shot horse.

The firing from the trees stopped.

There was a moment of perfect silence. Then the man in the camouflage jacket came out from behind the dead truck running, and two more came crashing out of the pines on the left side, and one on the right, and they ran, all of them, down the road and into the trees, as fast as they could go, without looking back.

Jim sat up slowly behind the wheel. He looked at the Silverado, steaming in the road. He looked up at the roof hatch, where his mother's slippers were standing on the top step of the ladder.

"Told you those things could take out a tank," Jim said.

John was holding his shoulder with his right hand and blood was coming out between his fingers.

"It's a Silverado, Dad," John said.

Granny came down the ladder one step at a time, holding on with both hands. At the bottom she stood in the aisle and straightened her housecoat and touched her hair.

"Oh my word," Granny said.

She went back to the couch and picked up the pug and her church fan and sat down, and she never spoke about it again, not one time, for the rest of her life.

* * *

Sanda opened the door of the motor home and stepped down onto the road with her rifle at her shoulder.

There was one man left by the truck. He had not run. He was sitting on the asphalt against the Silverado's back tire with both hands clamped on his thigh, and there was blood all down his jeans. He was a boy, really. He could not have been more than nineteen. He wore a tan Carhartt jacket too big for him and a camouflage cap, and his face under it was white, and his mouth was open.

Sanda walked toward him down the center line with the red dot on his chest, the way she had walked toward Trevor across the broken glass. One step. Another. Fifty yards. Thirty. Twenty. She could see his face clearly now. She could see that he was crying.

She stopped.

She stood in the road with the rifle on him, and she knew exactly what she could do, because she had done it, three nights ago, kneeling on a floor with two fingers on a man's neck. She knew how it felt. She knew that she could. That was the thing she had been carrying in her chest since Wednesday night: not that she had done it, but that it had been so easy.

She lowered the rifle.

She lowered it until it pointed at the asphalt. And then she turned to the pines, to the place where the men had gone, and she screamed. It was not a word. It went on and on, long and terrible, and it went out across the road and into the trees and through them, and it did not stop until there was nobody left in those trees to hear it.

Then she walked back to the motor home and climbed in and closed the door.

* * *

Dorina had John on his back on the floor of the motor home between the boxes of ammunition before the door was closed.

She had Trevor's red trauma bag open beside her, and she had cut away his jacket and shirt with shears faster than he would have believed, and found the hole, and the hole on the other side, where it had gone through. Her hands moved over it quickly and surely, the way they must have moved in the back of an ambulance in Bucharest in a cold December long ago, and her face had gone quiet and flat and certain.

She threaded a curved needle from a sealed packet.

"Hold still," Dorina said, in English.

John held still. He had never been so still in his life.

She stitched him on the floor of the motor home with the Barrett still pointing at the steaming truck, while Jim drove them slowly around the dead Silverado on the shoulder of the road, glass sliding on the dash, and the boy in the Carhartt jacket watched them go by. When she was done, she taped a pad over each side and sat back on her heels and looked at him for the first time.

"Is good," Dorina said. "You live."

* * *

Granny gave the directions for the last twenty miles. She sat up straight on the couch with the pug in her lap and said them from memory, without a map, without having been there in more than thirty years, and every one of them was right. *Left at the Primitive Baptist church. Three miles. Right on the dirt road past the big water oak.* The water oak was still there. It was enormous.

The dirt road was red. The motor home lurched and swayed on it, and branches scraped down both sides, and pine needles came in through the empty windshield. A mile through planted pine. Then the old longleaf, tall and far apart, with the ground beneath them carpeted copper.

The road ended at an embankment.

It stood up in front of them six feet high, red clay streaked with off-white sand, and the road simply stopped at the foot of it. Jim turned off the engine. The silence came in through the empty windshield like water.

John lifted himself up on his good elbow on the floor and looked out. To the left, back in the pines, there was a tin roof, rusted to the color of the road, and part of a chimney. In the clearing in front of it, on its short iron pipe, stood the pump, rusted solid, exactly where it had been when he was twenty-three, and when he had looked at it and thought, *I doubt it*, and forgotten it for nineteen years.

"We're home," Granny said.

Behind them, back down the road, someone fired a shotgun into the air. It was one shot, deliberate, the way a man knocks on a door.

And a voice they did not know called out, slow and level, from somewhere back in the pines:

"*That's far enough.*"
