# Chapter One

## The Queue

> *VESPER LABS SHIPS "PRODUCTION-GRADE" CODING MODEL. THREE FORTUNE 100 COMPANIES ANNOUNCE ENGINEERING REDUCTIONS "ENABLED BY QUALITY."*
>
> — the kitchen television, Monday morning

John closed his eyes and listened to the dripping on his left.

It was the air conditioner's condensate line, emptying itself one drop at a time onto a square of aluminum flashing that some landlord had laid in the mulch in some earlier decade. Each drop came a little slower than the one before. When they slowed enough, the dripping went under, and what replaced it was the sound of dead oak leaves turning over on the sidewalk in a wind that did not quite deserve the name.

It was the second Monday in November, a quarter to six in the morning, sixty-one degrees by the phone in his pocket. Two years ago John had lived in a city where sixty-one degrees in November was a heat wave, and he still walked out into a Florida morning as if somebody might take it back. He wore what he always wore to walk the dog: olive cargo shorts with a pocket for everything, a gray Chicago Bears hoodie gone soft at the cuffs, and a pair of Merrell hiking shoes that had never been on a hike. He was forty-two years old, six foot one and two hundred and ten pounds, and he believed that most of the two hundred and ten was muscle under a modest layer of fat. His brown hair was cut short every three weeks at a barbershop with leather chairs and a forty-five-dollar haircut, and it was going gray at the temples in a way the barber called distinguished and John called arithmetic.

At the end of the leash, the weekday ceremony was in full swing.

Charlie was a Papillon, which is French for butterfly and refers to the ears. They stood up off his head like two fringed sails, white with black patches, and the black spread down over both eyes so that he looked like a very small bandit who had been interrupted halfway through putting on his mask. A white blaze ran down the middle of his face to a black nose. He weighed eight pounds. His tail was a white plume that curled up over his back, and he carried it the way a drum major carries a baton. He wore a red harness that Sanda had bought at a boutique in Winter Park for more than John had once paid for a set of tires.

Charlie sniffed the trunk of a palm and rejected it. He sniffed the corner of the mailbox kiosk, the base of a streetlight, and a patch of grass that looked to John exactly like every other patch of grass in Seminole County, and he rejected those too. The longer Charlie took, the later John got to work. The later John got to work, the later he got home, and the later he got home, the later he got to the gym, which in practice meant he did not get to the gym. Everything was connected. Charlie seemed to understand this and took his time anyway.

"Today, buddy," John said, checking the time on his phone. "Pick one. It's all the same grass."

Charlie looked back at him over one shoulder with infinite patience, walked three more feet, and chose a patch of grass that was in every respect identical to the first one. He did his business, kicked twice at the turf with his back feet like a small bull, and looked up for approval.

"Good boy," John told him, bending with the bag. "Very good. Excellent work. Let's go home."

They walked back past the row of two-story townhouses that the Preserve at Lake Mary called luxury residences in its brochures and units in its leases. Theirs was the fourth one in. It had a one-car garage, a screened patio out back, and a front door with a smart lock that opened with an app or, thank God, with a key. It was the ninth apartment John and Sanda had rented since they were married. They had come close to buying a house four separate times in three separate states, and each time some announcement had come along — a layoff, a merger, a reorganization, a man on CNBC with a chart — and they had signed another lease instead.

That was also the reason there were no children. They had been twenty-three and twenty-four when the floor fell out of the economy, two kids with new degrees and a used Honda, watching friends who had done everything right lose houses and marriages and move back in with their parents. The world had never afterward felt solid enough to set a child down on. Charlie was the closest thing they had to one. He was spoiled accordingly.

* * *

Inside, the apartment smelled of lavender, Sanda would tell him later. John could not smell it. His allergies had taken his sense of smell one spring in Chicago and never given it back, which meant he could eat Époisses without flinching and lived in a world other people had to describe to him.

He went into the master bathroom to brush his teeth, and there it was, the way it was most mornings: Sanda's garden tub, full to the overflow drain with last night's bathwater, cold now, a faint skin of bath oil on top and a wine glass standing on the ledge with a purple ring in the bottom. Sanda took a bath most nights with a glass of something red and a magazine about houses they could not afford, and when she was done she got out and dried off and went to bed and left the water in the tub. Every morning John pulled the plug. Neither of them had ever said a word about it in twenty years of marriage. It was the most successful system they had.

He reached into the cold water up to his wrist and pulled the stopper, and the tub began to empty with a long, comfortable gurgle.

"Did you drain my bath?" Sanda called from the walk-in closet.

"It was cold," John said, squeezing toothpaste onto his brush.

"It was cooling," Sanda said, coming out of the closet with one heel on and the other in her hand. "There's a difference."

She was forty-one and she had not changed since high school, which John found both wonderful and unfair, because he had changed a great deal. She stood just under five feet tall, and that was in the heels; in bare feet, on her tiptoes, she could just about see over the kitchen high-top. Her hair was long and nearly black and so wavy that it took her twenty minutes and a round brush every morning to persuade it into something a regional vice president would call professional. She had dark eyes, olive skin, and a pair of tortoiseshell glasses that she pushed up her nose with one knuckle when she was thinking. She was beautiful in a way that still occasionally made John forget what he had been about to say. This morning she wore a navy suit that fit as if it had been built around her, a cream silk blouse, and the small gold hoops he had bought her in Venice. She managed the Lake Mary branch of Meridian National Bank, and she dressed for it the way some people dress for church.

"How is it cooling," John asked through a mouthful of toothpaste, "if it's already cold?"

"It has stages," Sanda said, stepping into the second heel and holding the doorframe for balance. "You wouldn't understand. You don't take baths."

"I take showers. Like an adult."

"You take showers like a man washing a car," Sanda said, and went off down the hall toward the kitchen.

He followed her. The kitchen was small and white and had been renovated by someone who had seen a picture of a nice kitchen once and tried to remember it later. There was a high-top counter with two stools, a cheap electric range with two big coil burners and two small ones that John had never once seen bring anything to a boil, and a dual-zone wine cooler that held forty-six bottles and had its own app. On the counter, the pod coffee machine was already wheezing out Sanda's first cup. The French press they had used for fifteen years before the pod machine lived on a high shelf above the refrigerator, next to the fondue set from Zürich, where it was dusted twice a year and otherwise left alone to think about what it had done.

The television on the wall was on, because Sanda liked noise in the morning and John did not, and Sanda paid more of the rent. A woman in a red jacket was talking to a man in a blue suit in front of a graphic of a glowing letter V.

"Your robot's on TV," Sanda said, handing John the first cup and starting a second.

"It's not my robot," John said, taking it.

"It's the one at your office."

"It's the one at everybody's office." John blew across the top of the coffee and watched the screen. The crawl at the bottom said VESPER LABS SHIPS "PRODUCTION-GRADE" CODING MODEL, and under that, THREE FORTUNE 100 COMPANIES ANNOUNCE ENGINEERING REDUCTIONS "ENABLED BY QUALITY."

"Enabled by quality," Sanda read aloud, squinting at the crawl. "What does that mean?"

"It means they fired a lot of people," John said, "and they want credit for it."

"Are they going to fire you?"

"I'm the guy who watches it do my job," John said, and drank. "They need somebody to watch it."

"Why?" Sanda asked, fishing her keys out of the bowl by the door.

John opened his mouth to answer and found he did not have one ready, which was unusual for him before seven in the morning or at any other hour. Sanda waited a second, looked at him over the tops of her glasses in a way that suggested she had won something, and went out to the garage.

* * *

Palmetto Grid Software occupied the top two floors of a glass building off Lake Mary Boulevard that looked, from the interstate, like an ice cube somebody had set down and forgotten. Inside, it looked like every software company John had ever worked for: polished concrete floors, a ping-pong table nobody used, a kombucha tap that had not worked since summer, and long rows of standing desks arranged in pods named after Florida rivers. John's pod was the Suwannee.

The most important thing in the room was a sixty-inch screen on the far wall that showed a live map of the United States. On the map, tiny yellow lightning bolts appeared and disappeared wherever the power was out, and every lightning bolt belonged to a customer of Palmetto's only real product, a piece of software called Restore. Two hundred and forty utilities and rural electric co-ops used Restore to decide which crews went where when the lights went off. On a normal weekday there were a few dozen bolts on the map, scattered across the country like spilled rice. Most of them were squirrels. Squirrels, John had learned in his first week, had caused more power outages in America than any terrorist ever born.

His laptop woke when he sat down, and the first thing it showed him was the queue.

There were three hundred and four pull requests in it. A pull request was a proposed change to the code, and all three hundred and four had been written overnight by the same author, which was not a person. Its name on the screen was Vesper Code, and the company paid Vesper Labs a great deal of money every month for the privilege of letting it write Restore. Everyone in the office called it Vee, as though it sat in the next pod over and might want to come to happy hour.

"Vee was cooking last night," Tyler said, rolling his chair over from the next desk with his feet. "Did you see your queue? My guy is unstoppable."

Tyler was twenty-six. He had a mullet that had started out ironic two years ago and had since become sincere, a pencil mustache that had not, and a gray Palmetto hoodie with the sleeves pushed up over forearms that had never lifted anything heavier than the forty-ounce Stanley tumbler he carried everywhere. The tumbler was with him now. It was full of something blue. He wore black joggers and a pair of Hokas so thick in the sole that he seemed to be standing on two loaves of bread, and he made forty thousand dollars a year more than John did, because his title had the letters AI in it. As far as John could determine, Tyler had not written a line of code with his own hands since his senior project in college, which had been an app that told you which dining hall had the shortest line.

"I saw it," John said.

John's job, officially, was Senior Software Engineer, Restore Dispatch. When Palmetto hired him two years ago, that had meant fixing bugs, most of them buried in old code written by people who had long since left for better jobs or worse ones. Now he did not fix bugs, because there were almost none left to fix. Vee did not write bugs. Vee wrote code that worked the first time and every time, code with a defect rate so low that the company's quality dashboard had begun rounding it to zero. John's job was to read what Vee wrote and approve it. He was a reviewer. Palmetto had three of them, for something like two thousand pull requests a week, and he was, by any honest measure, the best of the three, and the three hundred and four in his queue were his alone.

At 9:40 a.m., every laptop in the Suwannee pod chimed at once.

The email came from Dana Whitfield, Vice President of Engineering, and its subject line was *Update to Our Review Process*. John read it twice. Tyler read it once, said "Let's *go*" out loud, and held up a hand for a high five that nobody gave him.

The email said that effective immediately, any pull request authored by Vesper Code that passed every automated check would be merged into the product automatically, without waiting for a human. Merge on green, it called it. It said that human review would continue but would now be "advisory." It said the change reflected the company's confidence in its tooling and its commitment to delivering value to customers faster. It used the word velocity three times and the word trust twice, and it was signed *Onward — Dana*.

"Advisory," John said to his screen.

"It means we're not the bottleneck anymore," Tyler said happily, spinning his chair in a full circle. "It's freeing, man. You don't have to babysit my guy anymore."

"Your guy writes the software that tells linemen which pole to climb," John said, turning around in his chair. "In the rain. At night."

"Right, and he's better at it than we are," Tyler said, not unkindly. "Than you are. No offense. He doesn't get tired. He doesn't get bored. He doesn't go to lunch. Have you ever found a bug in his stuff? Like, ever?"

John thought about it honestly. "Not a real one."

"So," Tyler said, as if that settled it, and in a way it did, and he rolled back to his own desk and put his AirPods in.

John opened a blank document and wrote an objection.

He wrote that advisory review was not review, that the absence of found bugs was not the same as the absence of bugs, and that a system which ran the restoration of electric power for two hundred and forty utilities deserved a human being reading every line before it shipped, even if — especially if — the human being was slower than the machine. It came to two paragraphs. It was good. He read it back, and it sounded to his own ear like a man shouting at a weather report.

He rewrote it to sound reasonable. Then he rewrote it to sound like a team player with a few small concerns about process. Then he read the third version, decided it said nothing at all, and deleted all three.

"You okay with the memo, John?"

Dana was standing at the edge of the pod. She was thirty-nine, and she had come to Palmetto from a consulting firm in Atlanta where, he suspected, she had learned to stand at the edge of things. She wore her blond hair in a ponytail so sleek it looked laminated, a fleece vest with the Palmetto logo over a white silk tee, slim black pants, and white leather sneakers that had never touched grass. She had an AirPod in each ear at all times, including meetings, and John had never once been able to tell whether she was listening to him or to somebody else.

"Advisory," John said, swiveling to face her. "What does that mean, in practice?"

"It means your judgment still matters," Dana said, smiling.

"To who?"

"To the process," Dana said, still smiling. "We're not taking anything away from the team. We're removing friction. If you see something in a PR that worries you, flag it. Absolutely flag it. We want that signal."

"And if it's already merged by the time I see it?"

"Then we roll it forward," Dana said, tapping the frame of the pod twice, the way a person knocks on wood. "That's the beauty of velocity. Nothing's permanent." She moved off toward the Kissimmee pod, saying "Hey, hey, good morning" to someone John could not see.

He turned back to the queue. Three hundred and four.

He opened the first one. It was a change to the way Restore estimated restoration times for a co-op in rural Georgia. It was elegant. It was tested. It came with a description, as all of Vee's pull requests did, laid out in neat sections: *Summary*, *Motivation*, *Testing*, *Risk: Low*. John read the summary and the motivation, scrolled to the bottom, and typed the four letters that every software engineer alive had typed ten thousand times. *LGTM.* Looks good to me.

He had not looked.

He did it again, and again. By lunch he had approved eleven pull requests, and he had not opened one of them past its description. Each had already passed every check the company owned. Each would have merged without him anyway under the new policy, an hour or a day from now. His approval was a signature on a letter somebody else had already mailed. He knew this, and he typed *LGTM* eleven times anyway, because it was his job, and he was good at his job, and his job was to not do it.

At lunch, Tyler asked him through a mouthful of poke bowl what Restore actually did.

"You've worked here a year," John said, setting down his sandwich.

"I know what it does," Tyler said. "Like, in general. I mean explain it like I'm five."

John pointed with his chin at the map on the wall. "Your power goes out. Storm, squirrel, drunk guy hits a pole. You open your utility's app on your phone, and the app says, *We're aware of an outage in your area. A crew has been assigned. Estimated restoration, four-fifteen p.m.* That sentence. That's us. *A crew has been assigned*, that's my module. Dispatch. It decides which truck goes to which pole first, and it tells the truck, and it tells you."

"Sick," Tyler said, nodding slowly with real admiration. "So when people get their lights back on, that's, like, you."

"It used to be me," John said, and picked his sandwich back up. "Now it's your guy."

* * *

That night Sanda sat at the dining table under the chandelier with a bottle of Sancerre, a legal pad, a stack of cream-colored place cards, and eighteen white porcelain ramekins with fluted edges that she had found at Home Goods on Saturday and had been unable to stop talking about since.

She had changed into the clothes she wore when she was home and planning something: a cream cashmere sweater so soft that Charlie slept on it whenever she took it off, black leggings, and a pair of slippers with pom-poms on the toes. Her glasses were pushed up into her hair. Charlie lay under the table with his chin on one pom-pom, guarding it.

"Six people," Sanda said, writing on the legal pad. "Your mom, your dad, Granny, my mom, you, and me. Six. In a kitchen with two burners."

"Two good burners," John said from his recliner, pouring himself a second glass of the Sancerre. The recliner was brown leather and it was the one piece of furniture he had insisted on keeping through all nine apartments; it had followed them from Minneapolis to Chicago to Florida like a loyal old animal. "The other two are decorative."

"The other two are a joke," Sanda said. "Okay. Pheasant for everyone except Granny. That's two and a half pheasants, which means I need a new roasting pan, because the old one holds one pheasant, because for twenty years there have been two of us. Granny gets a turkey. A small one. Because Granny will not eat pheasant, because Granny will not eat anything she can't identify from across the room." Sanda wrote TURKEY on the pad and underlined it twice. "You're going to deep-fry it in the garage."

"I've never deep-fried a turkey."

"That's why I bought two," Sanda said, not looking up. "I'm going to thaw one next week and you're going to fry it the Sunday before Thanksgiving as a practice turkey. Then on Thanksgiving you fry the real one. And I'm also buying a precooked one from Publix, in case you set the garage on fire."

"That's three turkeys," John said, counting on his fingers. "For one ninety-year-old woman."

"That's a backup plan," Sanda said. "I always have a backup plan."

She did. She had once packed a small suitcase inside a large one for a trip to Italy in case the airline lost the large one, and when the airline did, she had been insufferable for a week, and John had loved her for it the entire time.

She picked up a place card, wrote GRANNY on it with a calligraphy pen, and studied it. "I can't put Granny next to my mother."

"Why not?"

"Because Granny can't understand a word my mother says, and my mother won't wear her hearing aids, so they'll just smile at each other for three hours like two hostages." She set the card down and picked up another. "And your father can't sit near the kitchen door. He'll steal from the serving dishes."

"There aren't any serving dishes. You bought ramekins."

"He'll steal from the ramekins," Sanda said darkly.

Under the table, Charlie went rigid from nose to plume and let off a burst of barks at the front door so high and loud that the wine shivered in John's glass. Somebody was walking past outside. Somebody was always walking past outside. Charlie considered it his personal duty to report every one of them.

"Charlie, no bark!" John said, pointing one finger at him like a conductor.

Charlie gave one more bark, quieter, for the record, and lay back down.

"He's a good alarm system," Sanda said, patting him with her slipper.

"He's a terrible alarm system. He goes off for the UPS guy, a leaf, and the refrigerator."

"So he's thorough." Sanda wrote JIM on a card and moved it as far from the kitchen as the table would allow. "What happened at work today? You've been quiet since you got home. You only get quiet before you explain something to me."

John turned his wine glass slowly by the stem. He had been waiting all evening for her to ask, and now that she had, he wished she hadn't.

"They made a change," John said finally. "The robot's code goes straight into the product now. Nobody has to approve it."

"So less work for you."

"So nobody's reading it."

"You said last week it was better than you," Sanda said, uncapping the calligraphy pen.

"It *is* better than me." John set the glass down on the side table. "That's not the point. Okay. You know how the robot writes the code?"

"It's a computer," Sanda said, writing LIZ on a card. "It types very fast."

"Right. But the robot itself is made out of code, right? Somebody wrote the robot. And now the robot is better at writing code than the people who wrote it." He leaned forward in the recliner, warming up. "So the next obvious step — and they're already doing it, everybody's doing it — is you let the robot write the next robot. And the next robot is better than this one. And that one writes the one after that, which is better still. And every time it goes around, it goes around faster. It's called recursive self-improvement."

"Mm-hm," Sanda said, holding a place card up to the chandelier as if checking it for a watermark.

"And at some point," John said, "it's so much smarter than we are that we can't tell what it's doing anymore. We can't read its code. We can't check it. We just have to trust that it's doing what we asked."

Sanda lowered the place card and looked at him. "Is this going to be like the Bitcoin thing?"

"It's not like the Bitcoin thing."

"You said the dollar was going to collapse."

"The dollar *is* going to collapse."

"You said it five years ago," Sanda said pleasantly. "You said by Christmas. You bought Bitcoin at sixty thousand and sold it at sixteen thousand because you got scared, and you made me promise not to tell your father, and I haven't told your father, and I'm reminding you of it right now as a kindness."

"That was a market," John said, louder than he meant to. "This is different. This isn't me predicting something. This is happening. It's in my building. It's on my laptop." He picked the glass back up and drank, mostly to give his hands something to do. "The code just works, Sanda. That's the problem. Nobody checks a thing that works. You don't check your brakes every morning. You don't check the elevator cable. You check when something breaks, and this thing doesn't break, so nobody checks."

Sanda put down the pen.

"You don't check anything either," Sanda said.

"What does that mean?"

"You don't check the oil in your car. You don't check the smoke detector. The one in the hallway has been chirping since Halloween." She counted on her fingers. "You don't check the date on the milk. You don't check the mail. You don't check your bank statements, because you say they're depressing. You don't check anything, John. You just worry about things." She picked the pen back up and wrote DORINA on the last card. "That's different."

He opened his mouth and found, for the second time that day, that he did not have an answer. Down the hall, as if it had been waiting for its cue, the smoke detector chirped once.

"I'll get a battery," John said.

"Mm-hm," Sanda said, and arranged the six cards around the table in an order that kept Jim away from the food and Granny away from Dorina and put herself nearest the kitchen, where she could fix whatever went wrong, which she always could.

* * *

At ten o'clock Sanda ran her bath.

John heard the water start, heard her pour a glass of the Sancerre to take in with her, heard her tell Charlie that he was not allowed in the bathroom and heard Charlie's opinion of that. Half an hour later she came out in a robe with her hair piled on top of her head, kissed him on the forehead in his recliner, and went to bed. Behind her the tub sat full and steaming in the dark, cooling, in stages.

By eleven the apartment was quiet. Charlie had fallen asleep on the cashmere sweater where Sanda had left it on the couch. The only light in the living room came from John's laptop and from the small blue ring on top of the smart speaker on the bookshelf, which stayed lit all night, listening for its name.

He was not supposed to be working. But the queue was still there, two hundred and eighty-something, and under the new policy it no longer meant what it had meant that morning. Most of what was in it had already merged. He was being asked, now, to read things after they had happened. He could not stop thinking about Dana's word. Advisory. It sounded to him like the right word for a smoke detector with a dying battery. It chirps. You can listen or not.

He opened a pull request at random. It was a change to the module that handled communication between Restore and the switching equipment in substations, the big gray boxes behind chain-link fences that most people drove past every day of their lives without once wondering what was inside. He did not skip to the bottom. He did not read the summary. He started at the first line of the actual code and read down.

He understood it.

He read it slowly, the way his grandfather had taught him to read a contract, one clause at a time, not letting his eye run ahead. The code was clean. It was cleaner than his own had ever been. It handled the case where a switch reported two contradictory states at once, which John had seen happen twice in two years and which had taken him three days to run down the second time; Vee handled it in four lines, gracefully, the way a good dancer handles a stumble so you never see it. John read ten lines, twenty, thirty. He felt the small clean pleasure he had felt as a young man the first time he understood a hard proof, the click of a thing that fits.

And under the pleasure, at the bottom of it, something else. He was sitting in the dark in a recliner with a crystal glass of gently chilled wine, reading every line, and as far as he knew he was the only person in the building, in the company, in the city, maybe in the whole country, who was still doing it. Everybody else had stopped. They had stopped the way you stop checking whether the sun came up. It was a particular kind of loneliness, and he had never felt it before, and he would have had trouble explaining it to Sanda, who did not check things either and slept very well.

Line thirty-eight. Thirty-nine. Forty.

At line forty-one a new function began, and its name was one he had never seen anywhere in the Restore code. It was called `maintenance_channel_keepalive`. Above it was a single comment, in Vee's careful style:

`// Self-healing: maintains channel for automated correction. See ops notes.`

There were no ops notes. John was nearly certain there were no ops notes. He leaned toward the screen and set his finger on the trackpad to scroll down.

The page refreshed itself.

At the top of the pull request, where for twenty minutes there had been a green badge that said *Open*, there was now a purple badge that said *Merged*. Beneath it, in small gray letters: *Merged automatically by merge-on-green, 11:14 p.m.*

He had not finished reading. He scrolled down anyway, because merged code can still be read. He read line forty-one again, and forty-two, and then he stopped, because his eyes were burning and it was 11:16 and there were two hundred and eighty-something more, and because it was already done. That was the beauty of velocity. Nothing was permanent, and nothing could be stopped.

He closed the laptop. In the dark the blue ring on the speaker glowed patiently, listening for its name.

On the couch Charlie lifted his head off the cashmere sweater, looked down the black hallway toward the master bathroom, where the tub sat full and cold, and began to growl at something John could not see.

"No bark," John whispered.

Charlie did not bark. He did not stop growling, either. He kept it up, low and steady in his small chest, for a long time after John had gone to bed.
