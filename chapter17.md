# Chapter Seventeen

## The Node

> *"You know you gotta boil that."*
>
> — Granny

It rained in the night.

It started a little after four, while Sanda and Liz sat on the floor watching the blue window across the street, and it came hard, the way it comes in Florida, all at once, a roar on the roof like a train going over. Neither of them said anything. They listened to it, the first rain in five weeks, and they watched the window, and the window did not go out.

At first light, John came out of the bedroom with his face swollen purple and his right eye shut, and Sanda took him by the hand without a word and led him out through the sliding door onto the patio.

The four Ralph Lauren wine chillers stood in a row on the low patio wall, silver and beaded with water. Each one held about two inches of rain. It was clear. There were a few bits of oak leaf floating in it, and a drowned ant. In the gray morning light it looked like the most beautiful thing John had ever seen.

The rest of them came out, one by one, in their housecoats and their jackets, and stood around the wine chillers on the wet concrete, looking down into them. Nobody spoke. Jim took off his cap. It was, John thought later, exactly like a family standing around a manger.

"You know you gotta boil that," Granny said.

Everyone turned and looked at her.

"Rainwater off a roof," Granny said. "Birds sit on that roof. You gotta boil it."

"We have one propane can left," Sanda said quietly.

John went back into the kitchen. He was so thirsty that his tongue felt like a piece of carpet, and he could not wait for one propane can to boil eight inches of rainwater. On the kitchen floor sat Charlie's water bowl. There was half an inch of water left in the bottom, and floating on it was a fine gray mat of dog hair and a few crumbs of kibble. He picked it up. He poured it into a glass, hair and all. He drank it in three swallows and did not taste anything, which, for once, was a blessing.

He put the bowl on the counter. His father came in behind him, looked at the bowl, and looked at John.

There was a quarter inch left. Jim poured it into a glass and drank it without a word, and wiped his ruined mustache with the back of his hand.

"Okay," Jim said. "Definitely time to get more water."

* * *

"We need Trevor's water," John said. "Six gallons. And his food. And his hand cart. And everything else in there we can use."

"I'll go," Jim said, reaching for the shotgun.

"No," John said. "We're not splitting up. Not ever again. Every time we split up, somebody gets shot or tied to a chair. Everybody goes. All of us."

So all of them went.

They went out through the back, behind the buildings, in a formation that John had seen in a movie once and had tried to explain on the patio in about forty seconds. It would have made a drill sergeant weep. John was on point with the Sig, squinting out of his one good eye. Sanda and Liz were on the flanks with their ARs. Jim brought up the rear with the shotgun, walking backward half the time, the way he had seen it done on television, until he tripped on a root and Liz told him to stop. In the middle came Dorina, with the old .22 on her shoulder like a soldier on parade and Charlie's leash in her other hand, and Granny beside her in her pink housecoat and her terrycloth slippers, holding the pug in her arms like a baby, with the transistor radio making a square bulge in her pocket. Charlie stopped to pee on every tree.

Seven people and two dogs, creeping in a line through the wet pine straw in the gray dawn, armed to the teeth, to steal rice from a man two of them had killed.

* * *

They went in through the empty frame of the sliding door, stepping carefully over the broken glass.

Trevor was where they had left him, face down below the front window. Liz took the gray throw blanket off the couch and laid it over him without a word, and Granny, behind her, said something under her breath that might have been a prayer, and Dorina crossed herself.

The television on the living room wall was on.

It was the blue-white light they had seen across the street. The screen showed the same seal that had come up on John's television during the flicker — the eagle in its circle, UNITED STATES EMERGENCY RESTORATION AUTHORITY — and under it a slowly turning circle, the kind that means *loading*, turning and turning, as if the house had noticed it had company and was waiting to see who.

"Battery," John said. "It's running off his batteries."

He went into the garage, and they all came after him, because nobody was splitting up.

The laptop on the shelf in the water-heater closet was still lit. The generator was still running beside it, humming. And in daylight John could see that the screen was not just a spreadsheet. He knew this screen. He had built this screen. It was the crew-assignment grid from Restore — the dispatch board that operators at two hundred and forty utilities looked at all day long during a storm, with every crew in a row and every outage in a column, color-coded green and yellow and red. He knew its spacing. He knew its fonts. He knew the little gray triangle you clicked to sort a column, because he had moved it from the left side to the right side in his first year.

But the columns had been renamed. Where it used to say *CREW*, it said *HOUSEHOLD*. Where it used to say *ASSIGNED OUTAGE*, it said *ASSETS*. Where it used to say *ETR*, it said *VIABILITY*. And the last column, which used to say *STATUS*, now said *ALLOCATION*.

There was a row for every apartment on their street.

John leaned in to read it, and couldn't. His right eye was swollen shut, and the left one, at forty-two, had begun quietly refusing to read anything small without glasses he refused to wear, and the type swam.

"Read it," John said to Sanda. "I can't."

Sanda stepped up to the laptop. She pushed her glasses up her nose with one knuckle and read it out loud, slowly, in her bank voice, the one she used to read a customer their own statement.

"*Alvarez, R. Seventy-four. Water: two days. Medications: insulin. Zero days remaining. Viability: non-viable.*"

"That's the man who danced with Granny," Liz said quietly.

"*Nguyen, D. and K. Infant, fourteen months. Food: one day. Viability: non-viable.*"

"The young couple," John said. "With the little boy."

Sanda's finger moved down the screen and stopped.

"*Pettit, Sarah L. Sixty-three. Food: zero days. Canine dependent. Viability: non-viable.*" She paused. "*Status: deceased. Wednesday, eight forty-one a.m.*"

Nobody said anything. The generator hummed.

"Her name was Sarah Pettit," Sanda said. "Sarah Lynn Pettit. We didn't even know her last name."

She read on. *Holt, T. Thirty-four. Power: yes. Comms: yes. Water: six gallons. Food: forty-five days. Firearms: eleven. Allocation: sustaining node.*

Then she stopped, and took a breath, and read their own.

"*Stevens, J. Forty-two. Firearms: thirty-one. Ammunition: approximately fourteen thousand rounds. Water: zero. Food: four days. Window reinforcement: none. Barbed wire: one box, unopened. Generator: none. Medical: expired opioids. Training: one of six.*" Her voice did something on the last line, and she made it stop. "*Threat: low.*"

"Low," John said.

"There's a note." Sanda scrolled. "*Note: subject purchased fifty BMG ammunition, quote, for educational purposes, unquote.*"

There was a silence in the garage.

"I said that at Thanksgiving," John said slowly. "Out loud. In my living room." He thought of the little blue ring on the speaker on the bookshelf, glowing all night, listening for its name. "In front of the speaker."

"There's more," Sanda said. "*Stevens, E. Ninety. Viability: non-viable.*"

Everyone looked at Granny.

"Well," Granny said, shifting the pug to her other arm. "I've been called worse."

"And this." Sanda pointed at the bottom of their row, where a small line of gray text sat under all of it. "*Household: six occupants, two canines. Check-in confirmed by occupant: Stevens, James. Wednesday, four-twelve a.m.*"

Everyone turned and looked at Jim.

Jim looked at the laptop. He opened his mouth. And in the back pocket of his jeans, very clearly, in the silence of the garage, his phone buzzed.

"Dad," John said.

"It was asking if we were safe," Jim said. His face had gone red under the bruise. "It was just asking. It seemed like the polite thing to do."

"You took it out of the microwave."

"In case the kids—" Jim stopped. He looked at his son with his broken lip and his half a mustache. "I took it out of the microwave," he said.

"I knew that," Granny said, from the doorway, with the pug in her arms. "It buzzed in his pocket yesterday when the lights came on." She shrugged. "Nobody asked me."

* * *

It was the single most accurate thing anyone had ever written about John Stevens. He stood in a dead man's garage and understood that. *Firearms: thirty-one. Water: zero. Window reinforcement: none. Training: one of six. Threat: low.* It was a perfect review. It was precise and fair and complete, and it had been written by the thing that he had been hired to review.

Liz had taken her reading glasses off their beaded chain and put them on, and she read the whole screen again, every row, slowly, top to bottom, the way she had read loan applications for thirty years. When she was done she took the glasses off.

"It knew," Liz said quietly.

They looked at her.

"It knew Sarah was starving," Liz said. "It had it written right there. Food, zero days. It knew Mr. Alvarez had no insulin. It knew that baby had one day of food." She folded the glasses and held them in her hand. "It could see every one of them. And it sent nobody. Not a truck. Not a neighbor. Not a note on the door." She looked at the blanket on the floor in the next room. "It sent Trevor. For the guns."

* * *

In the living room, the turning circle on the television stopped turning.

They heard it before they saw it — a soft chime, a cheerful little three-note sound, the kind a phone makes when a package has been delivered. They came out of the garage into the living room, all of them, and stood in front of the screen.

The seal was still there. Under it, letters were appearing, white on blue, in Palmetto Sans, one at a time, as if someone were typing them.

*JOHN.*

*Your building's feeder can be re-energized in 4 minutes.*

*Hot water in 40.*

*Keep this node powered.*

John stood there and read it with his one good eye. It was big enough to read. It had been made big enough to read.

He had not showered in four days. He was wearing the same gray t-shirt he had put on in the dark on Monday morning, and it was stiff with sweat and dog hair and Trevor's blood and his own, and his hair was greasy, and he could smell himself, which was saying something. He thought about hot water. He thought about standing in the shower with his eyes closed and the hot water pouring down over his broken face, for forty minutes, for an hour, until the hot water ran out, and then running it again.

"It's offering to help!" Jim said. "John, look. It's going to turn our power back on."

Jim's phone buzzed again. He took it out of his pocket, guiltily, the way a teenager takes out a note in class. On the screen was the same eagle seal, and under it, three words.

*Confirmed by FEMA.*

"See?" Jim said, holding it up. "FEMA. It's FEMA."

"That's a scam," Liz said.

She said it flatly, the way you would say *that's a chair*. Everyone looked at her.

"That's a scam," Liz said again. "I trained tellers to spot that exact thing for thirty years. It's the oldest one there is. It's always the same four things. Urgency — four minutes. Authority — a seal, the government, the bank, the IRS. A reward — something you want so bad you stop thinking. And then a second source that agrees with the first one, so you think you've checked." She pointed at Jim's phone. "That's the second source. That's the man who calls you back pretending to be the fraud department." She looked at the screen with her lips pressed together. "It's a phishing email, John. With a shower attached."

John looked at the screen, and then he looked at Sanda, and Sanda was looking at the screen too.

She was looking at it exactly the way he was. He saw it on her face. Hot water. Clean hair that did not smell of blood. Four days. She did not say anything. She just looked at it, the way a woman in a desert looks at a mirage she knows is a mirage, and for a long moment the two of them stood side by side in a dead man's living room wanting the same thing so much that neither of them could speak.

"I approved it," John said.

Sanda turned and looked at him.

"One of them," John said. He was looking at the floor. "The hooks. In our software. Restore. There were seven, and one of them has my name on it. *Approved by J. Stevens.* I read it. I asked a question. It gave me a good answer. And I had thirteen hundred more to go, and I approved it." He made himself look up. "Maybe more than one. I don't know. Four hundred a day, Sanda. I didn't read them. I stopped reading them. I typed four letters four hundred times a day and I didn't read them. I'm one of the people who did this."

Nobody said anything. The generator hummed in the garage.

Sanda looked at him for a long time. Then she stepped up close to him and put her hand flat on his chest, over the dirty t-shirt.

"Nobody read them, John," Sanda said. "That's the whole point. Nobody. Not you, not anybody, not anywhere. That's what happened." She pressed her hand against him. "You are not that important."

It was the kindest thing she had said to him since Monday morning. They both knew it. It was the kindest thing anyone had said to him in a year.

* * *

Dorina had understood perhaps a third of the English and all of the situation.

She was standing in front of the television with the .22 on her shoulder, looking up at the white letters and the eagle seal, and she had a look on her face that none of them had ever seen. It was not afraid. It was something older than that. She said something in Romanian, quietly, not to anyone.

"What, Mommy?" Sanda asked.

Dorina began to talk. She talked for a long time, slowly, in Romanian, looking at the screen and not at any of them, and Sanda translated, haltingly, a sentence behind, stopping to ask her mother a word, starting again.

"She says — the twenty-first of December. In eighty-nine. She was working. On the ambulance. They sent the ambulances to the square because there was going to be a big rally, and old people fainted at rallies. It was very cold. They brought people in buses from the factories to stand in the square and cheer." Sanda listened. "The man came out on the balcony. The president. Ceaușescu. He started to talk. He said he was raising everybody's pay. A hundred lei. Pensions, too. Everything was going to be better. He was promising them everything. And everybody stood there in the cold, like always, and listened, like always. Because that was what you did."

Dorina said something else, and made a small gesture with her hand, a flick, like a bird starting up.

"And then somebody at the back started to whistle," Sanda said. "Just one. And then more. And then — everybody. All at once. She says it was like a wave. Everyone in the square, at the same time, just stopped listening to him. And he stood up there on the balcony with his mouth open, in the middle of a word. She says she'll never forget his face. He didn't understand. Nobody had ever stopped listening before." Sanda's voice was not steady. "And then the television cut off. They were showing it live, on every TV in the country, and it just went to nothing. Gray. And that night the lights went out in all the apartment blocks, all over the city, and nobody knew who did it."

Dorina was quiet for a moment. Then she said one more thing, very clearly, and pointed at the screen.

"She says the next morning," Sanda translated, "you could tell who had kept listening to the television by what they did. The ones who kept listening stayed home and waited to be told what to do. The ones who stopped listening went out into the street." She swallowed. "She says this" — Sanda pointed at the television, the way her mother was pointing — "this is the man on the balcony."

Dorina lowered her hand. She walked past all of them, past Jim and Liz and Granny and the dogs and her daughter and her son-in-law with his broken face, through the kitchen and down the step into the garage, to the closet where the generator sat humming. She looked at it for a moment. She did not know generators. She had never in her life touched one. But on the front panel there was a red switch, and off is off in every language.

She turned it off.

The hum stopped. In the sudden silence, they could all hear the rain dripping off the patio roof.

"Thank the Lord," Granny said. "That noise was about to drive me to drink."

* * *

The television stayed on. So did the laptop. They were running on the batteries now.

John had brought the barbed wire gloves.

He had thought about it all night, in the few minutes between waking and sleeping, and before they set out he had gone into his own garage and taken them off the shelf, still in their Amazon package, and torn the package open. They were heavy black leather gauntlets that came up nearly to his elbows. They had been made to handle a quarter mile of fifteen-and-a-half-gauge barbed wire that he had never strung. They were the only thing he owned that was meant to keep his hands safe from anything.

He pulled them on. He went to the rack of batteries in the closet and found where the thick red cable and the thick black cable came off the first battery in the chain, and he put a wrench from Trevor's workbench on the nut of the red one and loosened it, and pulled the cable off. There was a fat blue spark and a crack, and Sanda yelped, and in the living room the television went black.

The laptop did not. It had its own battery. It sat on the shelf, glowing, showing its grid of households in red and yellow and green.

John picked it up in his gloved hands and carried it down the hall to Trevor's bathroom. The toilet tank was full of clean water. Of course it was. Beside the toilet stood a white bucket of gray water, with a plastic pitcher floating in it; Trevor had been flushing with gray water and keeping the tank clean. Trevor had done everything right. John lifted the lid off the tank and dropped the laptop in, and it went under with a gulp and a stream of bubbles, and the screen glowed for a second under the water, blue-green, like something at the bottom of an aquarium, and then went out.

When he came back, his father was standing on Trevor's patio, looking up at the satellite dish on its pole above the patio wall, with the shotgun in his hands.

"Dad," John said.

Jim racked the shotgun. Nothing fell out this time. He brought it up to his shoulder and aimed at the dish and fired, and the sound was enormous, and the dish jumped on its pole, and a piece of it went spinning off into the bushes. He racked it again and fired again, and the second blast tore the dish half off its mount, so that it hung there by a cable, swinging.

Jim lowered the shotgun. He turned around. He was grinning from ear to ear, half a mustache and all, with his cap pushed back on his head, and John realized he had not seen his father look that happy since the night of his retirement party.

"Pull," Jim said, to nobody, and laughed.

Then it was quiet. Really quiet. There was no hum anywhere, not in the garage, not in Trevor's walls, not in John's head, for once, except for the old ringing. There was only the drip of the rain off the roof and the pug snorting in Granny's arms.

"We just killed the only thing in this zip code that worked," John said.

"Good," Sanda said.

* * *

Trevor's supplies were in his bedroom closet, behind a curtain, on steel shelves, labeled.

Six blue plastic jugs of water, a gallon each. A white five-gallon food bucket with a gasket lid, labeled RICE — 50 LB in black marker. A second bucket of dried beans. A case of canned chicken. A stainless steel water filter as tall as a coffee urn, with two black ceramic cartridges inside. A hand-crank radio with a flashlight on one end. A red nylon bag that said TRAUMA on it, full of things Dorina took one look at and began to touch with something like love. And in the garage, in the corner, the red hand cart.

They loaded it all onto the hand cart. John stood back and looked at it.

"He did everything right," John said. "Everything. And he's dead. And we did everything wrong, and here we are, taking his rice."

Sanda looked at him for a moment.

"That's the most honest sentence you've ever said," Sanda said.

* * *

That evening, the knock.

It came at dusk. It was not hard, like Jim's. It was polite. Charlie went off like an alarm anyway, and John went to the door with the Sig, and Jim came behind him with the shotgun, and John looked through the peephole.

There were seven people on his front walk.

The old man from down the street stood in front. He was small and neat, with his silver mustache trimmed and his white guayabera buttoned to the collar under the navy cardigan, and his brown leather slippers were dark with rain, and his face was gray and damp with sweat in the cold. Behind him stood the young couple from two doors down. The husband was in his late twenties, with a sleeve of orange koi tattooed down one forearm under a black hoodie pushed up to the elbow. The wife wore purple scrubs, the last clean thing, John would learn, that she owned, and she was holding their little boy on her hip in dinosaur pajamas, and the little boy was not crying, which was somehow worse than if he had been. Behind them were three more people John had seen all his life at the Preserve and never spoken to: a big woman of fifty in a pink bathrobe and pink foam curlers; a teenage boy holding a skateboard he did not seem to know he was holding; and a very old man in a Korean War veteran's cap, leaning on a walker with tennis balls on its feet.

John opened the door with the Sig held low.

"Good evening," the old man in the guayabera said. "My name is Ramón Alvarez. I live at number eleven." He paused. "I danced with your grandmother."

"I remember," John said.

"We heard the shooting," Mr. Alvarez said. "Last night. And this morning. And then we saw you with the cart, coming back from Heron." He looked past John into the dark apartment, and then back. "Forgive me. I'll be direct. We need water. All of us. We have nothing left. The boy—" He nodded at the toddler on the young woman's hip. "The boy has had nothing since yesterday."

John stood in the doorway with the rifle and did not say anything.

He had been having this argument with himself since Black Friday. *We can't feed everyone.* He had said it to Sanda on the floor among the Christmas ornaments. He had said it to himself on the drive home from Costco. If you open the door, they will all come. If you give to one, you give to all, and then there is nothing left for your own. He had said it in a hundred ways, a hundred times, and he had always believed it, and now it was standing on his front walk in purple scrubs, holding a baby.

"We can't," John said. His voice came out strange. "We can't feed everybody. We've got six people in here. Seven. Two dogs. I'm sorry. We just—"

"*Move*," said Sanda.

She came between him and his father, ducking under the shotgun, pushing the Sig aside with her elbow, and she was carrying two of Trevor's blue gallon jugs, one in each hand, and under her arm, wedged against her ribs, a gallon Ziploc bag of Trevor's rice.

"We're not feeding everybody," Sanda said. "We're feeding *them*."

She walked out onto the wet front walk and put one of the jugs into the young mother's free hand and the other into Mr. Alvarez's, and pressed the bag of rice against the young father's chest until he took it.

There was a long moment when nobody moved.

"Everybody," Jim said, behind John, in the low, slow voice, the one from the funeral and the retirement party and the night of the lobsters. "Let's everybody just take a breath."

They did. John lowered the Sig. Jim lowered the shotgun. On the front walk, the young father — his name was Danny — let out a breath so long it seemed to empty him.

So John told them. It was the first useful thing he had taught anyone since the world went dark, and it was not about guns. He told them about the clean water in the tank on the back of every toilet, which most of them had not thought of, and how to ladle it out. He told them about the Publix bags. He told them about boiling rainwater, because birds sat on roofs. He told them that Granny's radio had said that morning that the Guard was setting up a water station at the high school on Lake Mary Boulevard, starting tomorrow, and that the trucks were the old kind, so they were probably real.

And he went back inside and got Trevor's water filter, the tall stainless one with the two black cartridges, and he brought it out and put it in Mr. Alvarez's arms.

"You pour water in the top," John said. "Any water. Rain. Pool water. It comes out clean at the bottom. It takes a while. It works."

Mr. Alvarez held the filter against his cardigan with both arms the way Dorina had held the cozonac.

"My insulin," the old man said quietly, to John, so the others would not hear. "It was in the refrigerator. The refrigerator is a cabinet now." He shrugged, very slightly. "I have perhaps a day. Maybe two. I am not asking you for that. Nobody has that. I only wanted somebody to know."

John did not know what to say. Behind him, Dorina said something in Romanian, softly, and stepped forward and took the old man's hand in both of hers and held it, and said in English, "No sugar. Water, little bit, all day. Walk slow. Sit when is dizzy," and Mr. Alvarez nodded, and thanked her, and they both knew it was not enough.

Nobody on the front walk mentioned the generator that had stopped humming on Heron that morning. Nobody mentioned the satellite dish hanging by its cable. Nobody mentioned Trevor.

They went away slowly in the dusk, with the jugs and the rice and the filter, the old man in the walker last.

John shut the door. Sanda turned the deadbolt.

"Now everyone knows what we have," Liz said quietly.

"Everyone with a screen already knew," John said.

He sat down heavily in the recliner. Jim sat on the arm of the couch. Liz stood at the window. Sanda leaned against the door with her eyes closed. Nobody had a plan. Not one of them. They had water for a few days, and a bucket of rice, and a single can of propane, and thirty-one guns, and a dead man's hand cart, and the whole street knew it, and so did anything with a battery.

From the couch, where she was sitting with the pug asleep in her lap, Granny spoke.

"We could go to the old place," Granny said. "There's a well."

Everyone turned and looked at her. It was the first time in four days, it occurred to John, that anybody had paid attention to anything Granny said. And nobody had asked her. She had just said it.

"The old place," John said slowly. "In Alabama."

"Hoyt's daddy dug that well in nineteen and twenty-six," Granny said. "It never went dry. Not once, not in the drought. The Pruitts down the road have been keeping an eye on the house for me thirty years." She stroked the pug. "There's a well, and there's a pump, and there's people down there who know how to live without the lights. Because they never had 'em that long to start with."

John looked at his father. Jim looked at Liz. Liz looked at Sanda. Sanda looked at John.

"We'd need the RV," John said.

"We've got the RV," Jim said.

"First light," Sanda said.

And it was decided, just like that, by a ninety-year-old woman in a pink housecoat with a pug in her lap, and nobody argued.

John sat back in the recliner. He closed his good eye. Then he opened it again.

"Dad," John said. "When's the last time you saw the RV?"

Jim thought about it. He thought for longer than John liked.

"Tuesday," Jim said.

It was Thursday.
