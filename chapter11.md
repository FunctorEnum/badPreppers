# Chapter Eleven

## The Day

> *DUE TO THE OUTAGE THE OFFICE IS CLOSED. PLEASE SUBMIT REQUESTS THROUGH THE APP.*
>
> — sign, leasing office door, the Preserve at Lake Mary

The car alarm in the dream was his phone.

John opened his eyes in the dark. It took him a second to understand that his grandfather was gone and that it was six o'clock on Monday morning and he had to go read code on paper. He found the phone on the nightstand by feel and tapped at it until it stopped. Then he reached up for the little round knob on the bedside lamp and turned it.

The switch clicked. The lamp did not come on.

"Damn bulb's out," John said. "Can you try your lamp?"

Sanda reached over without opening her eyes and clicked her lamp. Nothing.

"Great," John said. "Power's out."

He felt along the nightstand for his flashlight, a small black tactical light about the length of his hand, and his fingers found the cool knurled metal of it. He tapped the button on the end three times, quickly, with his thumb, which put it on the low setting, and a soft white circle of light opened on the ceiling. Sanda rolled over and pulled the covers up over her head.

He walked into the master bathroom in his boxers to pee and brush his teeth, sweeping the little light ahead of him across the tile. The beam slid over the garden tub and stopped there.

It was full to the brim with water, perfectly still, so clear that the flashlight went straight through it and lit up the white bottom of the tub like a swimming pool at night.

*Sanda left the tub full again*, John thought. He did not think it in words, exactly. It was the thought he had had on a thousand mornings in twenty years, and it arrived the way those thoughts arrive, already finished. And with his mind entirely on other things — on the power, and whether the cell tower would be out, and whether the paper review would still happen without power at the office, and whether the gate would open — he leaned over and put his hand into the cold water up to the wrist, and found the stopper by feel, and pulled it.

The tub began to drain with its long, comfortable gurgle.

He stood there with the flashlight and watched it go down while he thought about the power. Outages happened. Transformers blew. Squirrels. He thought about how long the battery on his phone would last. The water went down past the overflow drain, past the soap ledge, down the curved white sides, with a little whirlpool forming over the drain. It was 6:02.

He turned to the sink to brush his teeth and turned on the faucet.

The faucet coughed. A short burst of air came out of it, and a few spitting drops, and then from somewhere deep inside the wall there came a long, low groan, like a ship settling, and then nothing at all.

John stood with his toothbrush in one hand and the flashlight in the other.

Behind him the tub was making the last sucking noises of a drain at the bottom of an empty tub. He turned and pointed the light at it. There was a quarter-inch of water left, swirling around the stopper hole. Then there was none.

He understood it all at once, the way you understand, halfway down a staircase in the dark, that you have missed a step.

He did not say anything. He stood there for a while. Then he turned off the faucet, which was already off, and set his dry toothbrush on the counter, and went out of the bathroom and closed the door behind him very quietly, and he did not tell anybody, and the knowledge sat in his stomach like a stone.

* * *

He put on his cargo shorts and a gray t-shirt in the dark and slid the Glock 43 into his right front pocket, and he went to the front door and looked through the peephole and could not see anything. He stepped into his sandals and went outside.

The streetlights were out. All of them. The whole street was pitch black, darker than he had ever seen it, and the air was cold — properly cold, the front Trevor had talked about coming through early — and he could see his breath. He had left the flashlight on the dresser. He went back in, got it, came out again, and stood on his front walk in the dark, and then, after a moment, he turned the flashlight off, to let his eyes adjust.

Over the rooftops to the east the sky had gone a deep, bruised purple. There were stars in the rest of it, more than he had ever seen over the Preserve; the glow from the Publix lot and the interstate was gone. Somewhere far off, a single siren was going, rising and falling.

A few doors down, the old man in the guayabera who had danced with Granny was standing on the sidewalk in front of his apartment. He had a navy cardigan buttoned over the guayabera and brown leather slippers on his feet, and he was looking up and down the dark street with his hands in his cardigan pockets, the way a man looks for a bus.

"Whole street?" John called softly.

"Whole street," the old man said. He shrugged in his cardigan. "It'll be back by lunch."

He turned and went back inside. John stood there a minute longer, listening to the siren, which had become two sirens. *The cell tower*, he thought. If it was just a transformer, the cell tower would have its own backup. He took out his phone. In the corner of the screen where the bars usually were, it said *No Service*.

He went back to the bedroom and sat on the edge of the bed.

"I think something's going on," John said.

"What," Sanda said, from under the covers. It was not a question.

"Where's your phone?"

She reached out and handed it to him without looking, still unplugging it from the dead charger. He punched in her code. *No Service.*

"If the cell towers are out," John said, "it's bigger than the street. They have backup generators."

"Go make us some coffee," Sanda said, through a yawn.

"Sanda," John said. "We. Have. No. Power."

There was a pause under the covers.

"Oh," Sanda said. "Right." She pulled the covers down to her chin and opened her eyes and looked at the ceiling. "Well, that sucks." She thought about it. "We have the Korean grill. And the French presses. We can boil water on the grill and make coffee."

"Yeah," John said slowly. "That could work."

He did not say anything about the water.

* * *

In the kitchen, the flashlight showed him the nine pitchers in their row along the back of the counter, glass and ceramic and steel and the rooster at the end, all of them full to the top, and the two French presses full, and the kettle full, exactly where she had left them. He stood looking at them for a long time.

Sanda came shuffling in behind him in her robe, rubbing her eyes, and picked up the kettle.

"No," John said. "Not that."

"What?"

"Nobody touches the pitchers." His voice came out harder than he meant it to. "Or the presses. Or the kettle. The water's out, Sanda. That's all the water we have."

She looked at him in the light of the flashlight. She was still too sleepy to understand what he was not saying, and he was grateful for that and ashamed of being grateful.

"Then what am I supposed to make coffee with?" Sanda asked.

John opened the low cabinet beside the refrigerator. Inside, in its cardboard case, was the Perrier — the second case, from the Costco run. The first case was long gone; it turned out they had been drinking it with dinner, because that was what it was for. There were five half-liter bottles left in the second.

"We have Perrier," John said.

"Will that work?" Sanda asked, entirely serious.

"I'm not sure what happens when you boil sparkling water," John said. "It's full of carbon dioxide." Sanda looked up at him. She did not want a science lesson. She wanted coffee, and she wanted him to make it happen. "Fuck it," John said. "Let's try it. I'll leave the lid off the kettle so the gas can get out."

He poured the tap water from the kettle into a stockpot, carefully, so as not to spill a drop, and covered the stockpot with a plate, and set it beside the pitchers, so that it would be safe. Then he filled the empty kettle with Perrier.

It took three bottles. He brought the Korean grill in from the garage with one of the little green propane canisters and twisted the canister in until it locked with a loud click, and Charlie, who had come to watch, fled back to the bedroom. John found the long lighter in the drawer, turned the gas knob, heard the hiss, smelled nothing, and clicked the lighter. With a soft *whoomp*, a ring of blue flame lit up the whole dark kitchen. He turned it down by half and set the kettle on the iron dome.

The Perrier started fizzing almost at once. Then it began to pop and crackle and spit, like a pan of bacon, and little bursts of foam jumped out of the open top of the kettle and sizzled on the dome. He turned the flame down further. After a few minutes the popping settled into something that sounded more or less like ordinary boiling. Sanda dropped four scoops of ground coffee into the old French press, which she had emptied into a pitcher first without being told, and John poured the boiling Perrier over the grounds and stirred it with a long spoon and set a timer on the stove for four minutes, which did not work, because the stove was electric. He set it on his watch instead.

They stood in the kitchen in the blue light of the grill and looked at each other, and without a word, each of them knew the other was thinking how ridiculous this was.

The guest room door opened, and Dorina shuffled out in her flowered housecoat with her burgundy hair flattened on one side.

"Good morning, John," Dorina said, in her thick accent. "I think the power or something is not working."

"Yeah, I don't know what's going on yet, Mom," John said. "The power and the water are both out. We're making coffee."

"I think maybe was a storm," Dorina said, peering at the blue flame.

"I don't think it was a storm," John said, and his watch beeped.

Sanda pressed the plunger down slowly and poured three cups. John took an ice cube from the freezer, which was still cold, dropped it into his, waited for it to melt, and took a sip. He put the cup down on the counter.

"Yeah," John said. "This tastes like shit."

"I dunno," Sanda said, not ready to give up on the only thing she wanted in the world. She took a sip. She swallowed it hard and set her cup down. "This coffee tastes like ass."

For years John had tried to get his wife to tone down her language and failed, mainly because his own was no better. With no children to set an example for, they had let it slide downhill together, each raising the stakes on the other, until it had become a kind of game. She said she had two settings, home and branch. He was not sure the branch setting was holding.

"Is coffee no good?" Dorina asked, smiling.

"Not really, Mom, but you're welcome to try it."

Sanda gave her mother three packets of sweetener and two little cups of creamer from the drawer. Dorina stirred them in and took a long, thoughtful sip, and considered it, with her head on one side.

"Is not bad," Dorina said, and shrugged, and drank it all.

* * *

"So," John said, and they both looked at him. The sun was starting to come up now, and gray light was coming in through the blinds. "I think we've got a big outage. I'm not trying to panic anybody. But the water's out too, and the cell phones, and it wasn't a storm. I've been outside twice. The ground's dry. It didn't even rain."

Sanda and Dorina looked at him and did not say anything.

Someone knocked on the front door. Charlie came out of the bedroom like a missile, barking, and John put his right hand flat on the pocket of his shorts, over the Glock, and went to the peephole.

It was Granny. She was standing on the mat in her pink housecoat with the little blue flowers and a gray cardigan over it and her terrycloth slippers, looking back over her shoulder at the street. Her white curls were flattened on one side, like Dorina's.

"Charlie, no bark!" John said, and opened the door.

"Is your power out too?" Granny asked. "I tried to call you, but my phone isn't working."

"The power's out in the whole complex, Granny," John said, in a calm, flat voice he did not feel. "Probably more than that. The phones are out too. And the water."

"Oh my word!" Granny said.

"I'm sure it's just temporary," John lied, helping her inside. He looked at his wife. "Sanda. Can you help me in the closet for a second?"

It was code, and she knew it. She followed him into the master bedroom, and he closed the door.

"I'm going to drive around," John said quietly, "and see how big this is. I'll be twenty minutes. While I'm gone, I need you to watch this place. Don't scare your mom or Granny. Get the Bodyguard. It's getting cold in here — put on a jacket and put the gun in the pocket. Keep them calm."

"Okay," Sanda said. And then, looking up at him: "Just be careful."

He clipped the Benchmade, a black spring-loaded knife, into his back right pocket. He did not know what he expected to cut.

He backed the GTI out of the garage, put it in first, and popped the clutch harder than he needed to, and the tires chirped, and he drove to the front gate. The gate was closed. He pulled up to the white line where it always opened for him, and it did not open. He sat there. Inside the open steel box beside it, where the new Halcyon board was bolted to the back wall, the little green light that had blinked so patiently every night for a month was dark.

The gate did not need power to stay closed. It needed power to open. It needed the power and the cell tower and an app and a server somewhere, and all it had now was a steel arm and a motor and gravity.

He threw the car into reverse, swung around, and drove to the back gate over two speed bumps fast enough to scrape the bottom. It was closed too.

The leasing office was beside the front gate, in a low building with a fake stone front. He parked and walked up to the glass doors. The lobby inside was dark. Taped to the inside of the glass, at eye level, was a sheet of white printer paper. It said, in large black capital letters:

DUE TO THE OUTAGE THE OFFICE IS CLOSED. PLEASE SUBMIT REQUESTS THROUGH THE APP.

John read it twice. Then he got back in his car and drove home, past residents standing on their front steps in bathrobes, all of them watching him go by.

"The gates are locked," he told the three women in his kitchen. "Both of them. Nobody can get in or out."

Everyone paused. Far off, through the walls, there were more sirens now, three or four at once.

"I've got to find out what's going on," John said. "I'm going to walk out to the main road. Maybe I can flag down a cop."

"I don't think you should leave us," Sanda said. "You shouldn't be walking around by yourself when we don't know what's happening."

"What's the alternative? The water's out and we just used half our Perrier making shitty coffee. If this goes more than a day, we're fucked." He saw Granny's face, and Dorina's, and he regretted the sentence before he finished it. He made himself slow down. "Everybody stay here. I'm just going to walk to the main road. Thirty minutes."

Nobody said anything. Sanda looked at him without blinking.

He went to the front door. Charlie followed him, and he turned and raised one finger. "No bark." Charlie sat, and whined, and did not bark.

* * *

It was a ten-minute walk to the main road, around the dead gate through a gap in the hedge. He could see his breath the whole way. It was January and he was wearing shorts, and the cold went up his legs and made him think of Chicago. The strip mall on his right was dark, the Publix, the gym that never closed, all of it. On a Monday morning at seven the main road should have been solid. He counted four cars.

At the bus stop across from the strip mall there were two men.

One was about thirty, tall and loose-limbed, in a black-and-teal Jaguars windbreaker and gray sweatpants and slides with socks, with a thin gold chain at his throat. The other was older, in his fifties, heavyset, with gray coming into his short beard. He wore a brown canvas work jacket with an oval name patch sewn over the pocket, the kind from a uniform service, and the name stitched on it in red thread was DESMOND. He was looking at the ground.

A patrol car came around the corner fast, lights and siren, and went past them up the road.

John put his right hand in his pocket on the grip of the Glock and walked toward the bus stop.

"You guys know what's going on with the power?"

"Man, we don't know shit," said the one in the windbreaker. "Phones don't work. Water don't work. Thought we'd catch a bus outta here, but ain't no bus coming either."

Another cruiser came around the corner, green and white, Seminole County Sheriff, slower. John stepped out toward the road and waved both arms over his head. The car stopped beside him and the window came down. The deputy inside was young, with a shaved head and a thick neck and eyes that were red around the rims, as though he had been up all night.

"Sir," the deputy said, "I'm going to need you to go back to your home."

"Is the power out for the whole city?" John asked.

"Sir, I need you to return to your home. It's for your own safety."

John knew the voice. It was his father's voice, the work one. It meant the deputy did not know either, and had been told to say exactly this, and would say exactly this until his shift ended, whenever that would be.

"No problem," John said. "Thank you, officer."

The window went up and the cruiser pulled away. John turned back toward the complex. As he passed the bus stop, the one in the windbreaker stepped out toward him.

"Hey, man. Can you spare a dollar so we can catch a bus outta here?"

"I'm sorry, man," John said, and put his right hand back in his pocket.

In Chicago that had always been enough. Florida was different.

"Come on, man. Just a dollar." The man in the windbreaker was walking with him now, slowly, keeping pace a few yards off. "We gotta get to our grandmama's house in Ocala. Make sure she okay. She don't have nobody."

John held up his left hand. "I'm really sorry. I don't even have my wallet on me. I just walked out here to see what's going on."

"That's cool, man. We'll walk back to your place with you. You get your wallet. Ain't no bus coming anyhow."

John stopped and turned and faced him.

"That's not going to happen," John said, in a low voice he did not know he had. His right hand was in his pocket on the grip of the pistol, and his fist was so tight around it that the pocket bulged, and the man looked down at it. "I'm sorry. I can't help you."

The man stopped ten feet away. He looked at the pocket for a long second. Then he looked at John's face.

"Man, whatever," the man in the windbreaker said. "Fuck off."

He turned and walked back to the bus stop. The older man, Desmond, had never moved. He had never said a word. He was still looking at the ground.

John walked home quickly, but not too quickly. His wallet was in his back left pocket the entire time. He could feel it against him with every step, all the way back to the hedge and the dead gate and his front door, and he would never know — not then, not ever — whether there was a grandmother in Ocala, or whether she was okay.

* * *

"We have a problem," Sanda said, before he could get the door closed.

She was wearing her olive utility jacket, buttoned, and it hung lower on the right side than the left, from the weight of the Bodyguard in the pocket. She looked very serious.

"I had to use the bathroom," Sanda said, "and I flushed the toilet, and it went down and made this gurgling noise and it never filled back up."

"Number one or number two?" John asked.

"Number one."

"Okay." John was relieved, and kept his face serious. "We can't use the toilets until the water comes back. They'll only flush once. That was the once."

"Oh, it's fine," Sanda said, already turning. "I'll use the tub water to flush. That's what I filled it for."

She walked down the hall toward the master bathroom.

John stood in the living room and did not move. Granny was on the couch with Charlie in her lap. Dorina was at the kitchen counter, looking at the row of pitchers. Neither of them knew. John stood very still and listened to the bathroom door open, and then nothing, and then nothing, and then more nothing, for what was probably five seconds and felt like a week.

Sanda came back down the hall.

She did not shout. That was the worst part. She stopped in the living room doorway with her hands at her sides and looked at him, and her face was very calm.

"I filled the tub," Sanda said.

"I thought you left it—" John said.

"I FILLED," Sanda said. "THE TUB. JOHN."

Nobody moved. Granny's hand stopped in the middle of stroking Charlie. Dorina turned around from the counter. In the silence, John could hear the tinnitus in his ears and the sirens far off and his own heart.

"You said *mm-hm*," Sanda said, much more quietly. "Last night. I said don't touch the tub, and you said *mm-hm*."

"I did say mm-hm," John said.

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she let out a breath, and he watched her take the whole thing — all of it, the tub, the night before, twenty years of draining her bathwater — and fold it up, very small, and put it away somewhere, because there was no water to waste on it.

"So what do we do," Sanda said, "about the toilet?"

It was the end of the fight. It was the whole fight. It was over in less than a minute, and it would not be mentioned again for a long time, but John would notice, for the rest of that winter, that whenever he gave an order to anyone in that apartment, Sanda would look at him for one beat longer than she needed to. Everybody would notice. Everybody would know why.

"Bags," John said, after a moment. "The Publix bags. We've got a hundred of them under the sink. You open one up and set it down in the bowl — there's hardly any water left in there now. When you're done, tie it off tight, and I'll take it out to the dumpster."

"This is crazy," Sanda said, shaking her head. "And how do we wash our hands?"

"We can't use the drinking water. Right now that's nine pitchers and a stockpot and whatever's in the freezer and the dog bowls. We don't know how long—"

"We have wine!" Sanda said, and her face lit up. "We have soap. We can wet our hands with wine and use the soap and rinse with wine."

John opened his mouth to object and could not think of an objection. They had forty-one bottles of wine in the cooler, he knew exactly how many, because the cooler had told them. "Okay," he said. "But white. Unless you want blue hands."

"There's a fifteen-dollar Chablis," Sanda said, already at the cooler. She found it, cut the foil, twisted in the corkscrew, and pulled the cork with a soft pop, and John heard the pop and could not believe that they were about to wash their hands with French wine. In ten years of talking about the end of the world, they had worked through hundreds of scenarios. They had never once discussed how you wash your hands. Sanda carried the Chablis into the guest bathroom and set it beside the sink like a bottle of soap.

* * *

It was getting cold in the apartment. John put on a light jacket, mostly so he could wear a second gun under it, the Glock 26, butt forward on his left hip. Eleven rounds on the left, seven on the right. He told himself the power would be back by lunch.

"Granny," John said. "Did you flush your toilet yet this morning?"

"I went," Granny said, "but it was so dark I couldn't find the handle."

"Good. That's good. Don't." He turned to Sanda. "I need two pitchers and a ladle. One for the ice in Granny's freezer before it melts. One for the water in her toilet tank. It's clean. It's the tank on the back, not the bowl."

"We are not drinking toilet water," Sanda said, horrified.

"It's not for us. It's for Charlie."

"Charlie drinks filtered water!" Sanda said, before she could stop herself.

"Not anymore!" John said.

Granny's apartment was smaller and darker than theirs, and he had forgotten his flashlight. Again. On her nightstand he found the little black one he had given her years ago and reprogrammed so it only had one setting, low, and he used it to ladle the clear water out of her toilet tank into a pitcher and to dump her ice trays into the other. When he got back, he set the pitcher of tank water on the floor by Charlie's bowl.

"Okay. Listen," John said, standing in the middle of the living room. "Nobody knows yet what's going on. The power and the water could come back an hour from now. Or it could be days. We have to plan like it's going to be a week. Drink the water slow. If you have to go number one, don't flush. For number two, bags. Wash your hands with the wine." He counted on his fingers. "For food, we've got fruit — apples, bananas, oranges. Meat and cheese in the fridge, warming up. Pasta, but we can't spare the water to cook it. Cans of beans and soup. Tuna. Sardines." He paused. "And forty pounds of jerky. Kangaroo, alligator, buffalo, and wild boar."

Granny made a face as if she had found something in her shoe.

"No way I'm eatin' any sardines," Granny said. "No sir."

"You'd be surprised what you'll eat if you get hungry enough, Granny," John said. "Let's hope we don't find out."

"If we just had a TV," Sanda said. "Or a radio. Some news." She stopped. "Your parents. Aren't they at that state park, with the RV? Wekiwa?"

"Thirty minutes from here." John felt something like hope for the first time that morning. "The RV has a generator. Three TVs. If they hear about this, they'll come. Dad'll come."

"How will they get in?" Granny asked. "With the gate?"

Nobody had an answer for that.

* * *

He took Charlie out at nine, late, far too late, and Charlie peed on the first patch of grass he came to without ceremony of any kind.

Across the street the blond woman with the pug was out too, in the pink velour tracksuit with a gray cardigan pulled over it, and when the dogs saw each other they hit the ends of their leashes, and John crossed over, because Charlie gave him no choice. The pug was snorting and wagging her whole back half.

"Crazy, isn't it?" John said. "This outage."

"I don't know what to think," the woman said. Her scratchy voice was thinner this morning. She was not smoking. "Is your water out too?"

"Power, water, phones. Everything."

"I hope it's nothing too serious," the woman said, and she said it the way people say a prayer they have said too many times to listen to.

The dogs began to circle each other, and the leashes began to braid, and John stepped over one and under the other, and it occurred to him that this was the first time they had ever spoken about anything but the dogs. He held out his free hand. "I'm John, by the way."

"I'm Sarah," she said, taking it. Her hand was cold and very thin. "Nice to meet you, John."

"I'll let you know if I find out anything," John said.

"Thank you," Sarah said. "I really appreciate that."

She untangled the pug and scooped her up and went back inside. John never did come back to tell her anything. He would remember that.

* * *

When he came back in, Sanda had made breakfast.

She had set the dining table with real plates and cloth napkins, and on each plate she had fanned out slices of apple and orange, half a banana, three thin slices of Comté and a wedge of Manchego, and several rose-colored ribbons of prosciutto, arranged in a little nest. It was a beautiful breakfast. It was a breakfast for a Sunday in Napa.

Granny poked at the prosciutto with one finger. "What is this here?"

"It's ham, Granny," John said. "Just a different kind of ham."

He leaned toward Sanda and whispered, "We need to be careful with the food. We don't know how long this is going to—"

"That's enough," Sanda whispered back, sharply. "Everybody's scared. They've been through hell this morning. The least I can do is give them a decent breakfast."

She looked at him. She looked at him for one beat longer than she needed to.

He knew which battles to pick, and this was not one of them. "Fair enough," he said. "I'm going to sit down for a minute and think."

He took a banana from the bowl and peeled it walking to his recliner and sat. Charlie, who had developed a taste for bananas, sat at the foot of the recliner with one paw raised. John ate the banana in big bites, thinking. Nothing fit together. He was disappointed that no brilliant plan arrived. He had spent ten years waiting for this morning, and now it was here, and he had drained the tub.

He put the banana peel in his lap. He closed his eyes for a second.

He heard Sanda cross the room. He felt her lift the banana peel off his lap. He heard her walk away with it, toward the kitchen, and drop it in the trash. He kept his eyes closed. He did not know how long he slept.

He woke all at once, his hand already moving to his left hip.

Someone was knocking on the front door. Hard. Three times, like a fist, like a man who intended to be let in. And Charlie came off the couch in an explosion of high, frantic barking and hit the door, and through it, under the barking, John heard a man's voice outside, and he could not make out a single word of what it said.
