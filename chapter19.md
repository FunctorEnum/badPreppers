# Chapter Nineteen

## The Well

> *Soak new leather overnight. Don't rush it.*
>
> — Hoyt Stevens, in pencil, on the back of a feed-store receipt

Granny was out the door of the motor home before anyone could stop her.

"Mama—" Jim said, reaching, and missed. She went down the steps in her pink housecoat and her terrycloth slippers, holding the rail with both hands, and set off back up the red dirt road toward the voice, not fast, not slow, the way she had walked to church for eighty years.

Thirty yards back, just off the road in the pines, a tractor sat idling. It was green and yellow, square and old, with big rear tires caked in red clay and a single round headlight, and nothing on it anywhere that could think. Beside it stood a tall old man with a pump shotgun pointed at the sky. He was bony and long-jawed, with white stubble and arms burned nearly black by the sun, and he wore faded bib overalls over a gray waffle-knit undershirt and a sweat-stained feed cap that said PINE LEVEL FEED & SEED. He watched the little old woman in the housecoat come up the road toward him, and the shotgun came down an inch.

"Hollis Pruitt," Granny said, stopping in front of him. "You put that thing down."

The old man stared at her. He lowered the shotgun the rest of the way.

"Miss Eula?" Hollis said.

"Eula Stevens," Granny said. "Hoyt's wife. That's my boy Jimmy driving. And his boy, and the rest of 'em." She looked him up and down. "You've got old, Hollis."

Hollis Pruitt took off his cap. He held it against his chest with both hands, and stood there in the road with his white head bare, and for a moment he did not seem able to say anything.

"Yes, ma'am," Hollis said finally. "I reckon I have."

Somebody else was coming up the road behind him, riding a mule. The mule was brown, with a pale gray muzzle and one ear that stood up and one that flopped, and on its bare back, sitting astride in a denim skirt with her rubber boots hanging down, was a short, wide old woman with a long white braid over one shoulder, in a man's red flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled. She had a pair of reading glasses pushed up on her head and a shotgun of her own across her lap.

"Who is it, Hollis?" the old woman called.

"It's Miss Eula, Dee," Hollis said. "Hoyt Stevens's widow. Come home."

Dee Pruitt looked at Granny for a long moment from the back of the mule. Then she slid down off it, boots and skirt and shotgun and all, and walked straight to Granny and put her arms around her.

"Well, Lord, honey," Dee said. "What took you so long?"

* * *

"We heard y'all a mile off," Hollis said, when they had all come down out of the motor home and were standing in the road. "Heard the big gun go off down at the crossroads. Thought you might be them boys."

"We met them," Jim said.

Hollis looked at Jim, and at Jim's badge on his windbreaker, and at the half of Jim's mustache that was left. He looked at Sanda and Liz with their rifles, and at Dorina in her quilted coat with the old twenty-two on her shoulder and a dog on a leash, and at John, standing in the motor home doorway with his left arm in a sling made from one of Sanda's silk scarves and his face purple and green. Then he looked up, slowly, at the roof of the motor home, at the Barrett, which was still pointed down the road at nothing.

"You folks expecting the Russians?" Hollis asked.

John thought about it.

"Software," John said.

Hollis considered this for some time.

"Well," Hollis said. "We ain't got any."

* * *

The Pruitts had lived a mile back down the road since before John was born. They had kept an eye on the old Stevens place for Granny since nineteen ninety-four, when Granny had written to ask, and they had never once asked for a dime. They had a hand pump on their own well, and a spring at the back of their land, and a smokehouse, and forty chickens, and Pearl the mule, and a 1979 John Deere that ran on diesel and spite. There were twelve families on the road. The lights had gone out on all of them on the eleventh of January, the same as everybody else in the world.

"Out here," Dee said, walking beside Granny toward the old house with her arm through hers, "that's about like a bad ice storm. Folks pulled out the lamps. Lit the woodstoves. Killed a hog. The ones that had a radio, listened." She shrugged. "We didn't get the lights on this road till forty-eight. Plenty of us remember our mamas doing without."

"I was twelve," Granny said.

"I know you were, honey. Mama told it till the day she died."

The house was standing. It was smaller than John remembered, and older, gray board walls that had never in their life seen paint, and a tin roof rusted to the color of the road, and a brick chimney. Half of the front porch had fallen in. The other half was solid. Behind the house was a shed with a sagging door, and behind the shed, down the slope a little way, a low, windowless log building with a peaked roof that Hollis said was the old smokehouse, and the driest building on the road.

And in the clearing in front of the house, on its short iron pipe, stood the pump.

John walked up to it. Up close it was a cast-iron thing as tall as his waist, with a long curved handle on one side and a spout on the other, and a cap on top, and all of it was the same red-brown color of rust. He took hold of the handle with his good hand and pushed down.

It did not move. He pushed harder. It moved an inch, with a long shriek of iron on iron, and stopped, and nothing came out of the spout but a little red dust.

"Leather's gone," Hollis said, behind him. "Dry-rotted. Been gone for years. There's a cup of leather down inside on the plunger, see, and it's got to seal against the pipe, or it won't pull. That one's rotted to nothing. You need a new leather."

"Where do you get a leather?"

Hollis rubbed his stubble. "Used to be you'd get one at the feed store." He looked off toward the road. "Now, I don't rightly know."

* * *

The shed was dark and smelled — he supposed — of old wood and motor oil and mice. Hoyt's tools were still hanging on the wall above the bench where he had hung them sixty years ago, rusted, each one on its own nail inside a shape he had traced around it in black paint so he would know where it went: hammer, saw, brace and bit, a big pipe wrench. On a shelf above the bench sat a blue Maxwell House coffee can with a plastic lid.

John took it down. Inside were papers, folded and rolled and soft as cloth with age. A deed. Tax receipts. A letter from a bank. A photograph of a young man with a crew cut and long hands standing beside a mule in a field, squinting into the sun. And a small, flat red tin that said PRINCE ALBERT on the lid.

He opened the tin. Inside, wrapped in waxed paper, were three round, cupped disks of thick dark leather, each about the size of a coaster.

He stood there in the dim shed holding them in his palm.

Underneath the tin, at the bottom of the can, was a feed-store receipt, yellow and soft, printed at the top PINE LEVEL FEED & SEED, with a date stamped on it from the spring of nineteen sixty-one. On the front it listed a sack of chicken feed and a box of nails. On the back, in pencil, in a careful, slanting, old-fashioned hand that John had last seen on a birthday card the year before Hoyt died, there was writing. A lot of it. Small.

He held it out at arm's length and squinted with his one good eye and could not read a word.

He walked out of the shed into the sun and found his mother.

"Can I borrow your glasses?"

"Bring them back," Liz said, lifting the beaded chain over her head.

"I always do."

"You never have once," Liz said.

He put them on and sat down on the solid half of the porch steps with the receipt in his lap, and Sanda came and sat down beside him, and the words came up off the paper.

*PUMP LEATHER. Pull pin at top of handle. Lift off cap. Draw up rod — plunger on end. Old cup off — nut underneath, 9/16. Soak new leather overnight in water. DON'T RUSH IT. Dry leather won't swell, won't seal. Fit cup on, lip UP. Nut snug, not hard. Rod back down. Cap. Pin. Prime with a quart. Work slow at first. Keep it wet — a dry leather is a dead pump. — H.S.*

John read it twice. Then he sat there on the step with the receipt in his lap and his mother's glasses on his nose and did not say anything for a long time.

"What?" Sanda asked.

"I dreamed this," John said. "The night before. The night it all went out. He was standing by the door with one of these in his hand." He held up a leather cup. "He said, *You got to keep the leather wet, son.* And I didn't know what he meant."

Sanda looked at the leather in his hand. She did not say anything for a while either.

"Well," Sanda said. "Now you do."

They soaked the leather that night in the only container they could spare: the rooster pitcher, filled from the last of the motor home's tank. Sanda set it on the porch rail in the dark. John got up three times in the night to look at it.

* * *

That first evening, everybody found something to do, the way people do when they finally stop running.

Granny walked Jim out behind the house with her church fan and showed him where the outhouse had stood when she was a girl, and told him to dig the new one there — downhill from the well, a hundred steps, always downhill from the well, always — and stood over him with her fan while he dug, and told him he was doing it wrong. He was doing it wrong. She showed him. He did it her way.

Dorina set herself up on the solid half of the porch with Trevor's red trauma bag and the big jug of Wild Turkey 101 and a dining chair from the motor home, and by sundown, without anybody announcing anything, there was a line. A boy from down the road with a fishhook through the meat of his thumb. A farmer's wife with a cough that had been going a month. An old man who just wanted his blood pressure taken, and Dorina took it with a cuff from the bag and told him it was terrible and to eat less salt pork, and he went away delighted.

They carried the guns into the old smokehouse, all thirty-one of them, and the ammunition, and stacked them in the dark against the log walls, and Hollis put a padlock on the door and gave John the key.

And Sanda came out of the motor home with her rifle, the one she had shot forty rounds through on a Saturday in December and two through a sliding glass door on a Wednesday night, and walked up to John where he stood in the yard by the pump, and held it out to him.

"Here," Sanda said.

He took it. She turned and walked away from him across the yard, to where Dee Pruitt stood at the edge of the clearing in the last of the light, in her red flannel and her rubber boots, and the two of them began to walk the ground together, heel to toe, counting, Dee pointing here and there with a stick, Sanda nodding, pacing out rows.

* * *

In the morning John fixed the pump.

It took him two hours, with one good arm and his mother's glasses and his grandfather's handwriting propped against a brick on the porch step where he could see it. He pulled the pin. He lifted the cap, which shrieked. He drew up the long iron rod, hand over hand, out of the pipe, and there on the end of it was the plunger with what was left of the old leather on it, black and cracked and crumbling like burned paper. He found the nine-sixteenths wrench on its nail in the shed, inside its painted outline, and loosened the nut. He took the new leather out of the rooster pitcher, dark and soft and swollen with water, and fit it on, lip up, and tightened the nut snug, not hard. He fed the rod back down the pipe. Cap. Pin. He did every step in order. He did not skip one. He read each line twice before he did it and once after.

Everybody had gathered by the time he was ready. All of them, and Hollis and Dee, and Pearl the mule, who had come over to see.

"Prime it," John said. "It says a quart."

Sanda brought the rooster pitcher. There was about a quart in it, the water the leather had soaked in all night. She poured it slowly into the top of the pump.

John took hold of the handle with his right hand and worked it. Slow at first, the receipt said. Up. Down. Up. It squealed. Down. There was a sound deep in the pipe, a gurgle, a long sucking breath, like something very old waking up.

Water came out of the spout.

It came out brown, rust-red, a thick spurt of it splashing onto the ground. He kept pumping. It came out orange. He kept pumping. It came out cloudy, and then less cloudy, and then, all at once, clear — clear and cold and steady, gushing out of the spout in a thick bright rope onto the red clay, and splashing up onto his shoes.

Nobody cheered. They just stood there and watched it come.

Sanda held out a tin cup. He filled it and handed it to her.

She drank it. All of it. Then she looked at him over the rim, for one beat longer than she needed to.

"Don't touch the plug," Sanda said.

"There's no plug," John said. "It's a well."

"Good," Sanda said, and held the cup out again, and he filled it again, and that was the tub. That was the end of the tub, there in the yard with the water running over their shoes.

* * *

On the tenth of February, at noon, they all sat on the porch around Granny's radio.

The voice was not the president's. It was the same flat, careful man who had read the bulletins in the church parking lot in Georgia, and he read this one from paper too.

*Fourteen consecutive days have now passed with no coordinated network activity detected anywhere in the United States or in allied nations. Beginning tomorrow, manual re-energization of the electrical grid will begin, region by region, starting with hospitals and water treatment plants. Every breaker will be closed by a human hand. There will be no networked control of any part of the grid. There will be no public internet for the foreseeable future. Telephone service will be restored by voice only, on wire.*

There was a pause on the radio, the sound of a page turning.

*We cannot promise that it is gone,* the man read. *We can promise that we are watching now.*

Granny turned it off to save the batteries.

Nobody said anything for a while. Out in the yard, the chickens Dee had given them were scratching in the dirt near the pump.

John laughed. He did not mean to. It came out of him all at once, and he could not stop it, and everyone looked at him.

"That's the plan," John said. "That's the whole plan. For the entire human race. Turn it all off and hope for the best." He wiped his eyes with his good hand. "That was *my* plan. That was my plan the whole time."

"No," Sanda said. "Your plan was *prepare for the worst and hope for the best*." She looked at him. "You forgot the first half, John."

He stopped laughing.

Sanda looked out at the yard for a long time, at the chickens, at the pump, at the rows she and Dee had paced out with sticks, where the dirt had been turned. When she spoke again, it was quieter, and it was not to anybody in particular.

"I'd do it again," Sanda said. "In that apartment. The third one. I've thought about it every day. I knelt there with my fingers on his neck and I couldn't tell, and I thought, this is stupid, and I did it." She folded her hands in her lap. "I'd do it again. And I'd put the rifle down again, on that road, with that boy. I'd do that again too." She was quiet for a moment. "They're both true. I've been trying to make one of them not be true, and they're both true. So."

She stood up. She brushed off the seat of her jeans. She picked up the hoe that was leaning against the porch post, an old one of Hoyt's from the shed, with a new handle Hollis had cut for it from a hickory sapling.

"I'm going to go plant something," Sanda said, and went down the steps into the yard.

* * *

By April the garden was in.

It ran in long straight rows across the clearing in front of the house — tomatoes and squash and pole beans and okra and three rows of collards, because Granny said so — and the barbed wire was finally out of its box. Sanda had strung the whole quarter mile of it, wearing the gauntlets, around a chicken yard behind the house, to keep out the foxes, and it was the only use anyone ever found for it, and it was a good one.

The chicken house was the motor home.

It sat in the chicken yard with its wheels off and its windshield gone and chicken wire across the hole. The hens roosted on a pole across the bedroom. They laid their eggs in the shower stall, in straw, where the dogs had ridden to Alabama. And above it all, on its mount on the roof, the Barrett pointed out over the yard with an old green tarp stretched from the end of its barrel down to a post in the ground, making a little awning for the hens to stand under in the rain. On this road, it drew no comment whatsoever.

John had built the nesting boxes himself. He had meant to use the cordless nail gun, which he had finally taken out of its box, but its battery had died in January, like the drill's, like every battery he owned. So he built them with Hoyt's hammer and a coffee can of nails from the shed, and his left thumb was still purple, and the nail was coming off.

Sanda killed the chickens on Sundays. Jim would not, and could not, and stood out of sight behind the smokehouse with his fingers in his ears every Sunday morning like a man waiting out a lobster. Sanda did it the way her grandmother had done it in the village outside Bucharest, quickly and without ceremony, and then she cooked the chicken in Granny's kitchen with rosemary from Dee's garden and served it in Pinot Noir glasses full of well water, with a cloth napkin folded at every place, because some things were not going to change. In the kitchen window, on a nail, where it caught the morning sun, hung a little gold glass clock from a Chicago department store that had not existed for twenty years.

Jim carried the eggs down the road to the Pruitts every morning in a basket, wearing his windbreaker with the star on it, and on the way he stopped at every house on the road and talked. He had become, by common consent and without anybody ever voting, the sheriff of the road. When Earl Tidwell's hog got into the Mabry boys' corn in March, it was Jim who walked out into the corn in his New Balance sneakers and stood between Earl, red-faced in his suspenders, and the oldest Mabry boy, sixteen and skinny in a camouflage hat, and the shotgun the Mabry boy was holding, and said, in the low, slow voice, "Everybody take a breath," and they did. He never drew a weapon. Not once, the whole time. He kept his phone in a kitchen drawer, turned off, with its charger coiled beside it. For when it came back.

Liz kept the ledger.

She kept it in the Home Goods notebook with the little yellow pineapples on the cover, in her neat banker's hand, in two columns, owed and paid. Eggs. Diesel for Hollis's tractor. Stitches. A haircut. Two jars of honey. Cartridges. It was the only bank in a hundred miles and it had never once been wrong. A fifty-round box of twenty-two long rifle cartridges, it turned out, traded on the road for two eggs, which was almost exactly what John had predicted on Black Friday on the floor among the Christmas ornaments. He had mentioned this to his family, by Liz's own careful count in the back of the ledger, approximately four hundred times.

"You were right about one thing," Sanda told him, on the four hundred and first. "Enjoy it."

Dorina ran a clinic every afternoon on the porch, with Trevor's bag and her own two hands, and people came from three roads over. She kept the jug of Wild Turkey 101 beside her chair, and she used it on wounds, and she would not let anyone drink it, not even Hollis, not even at Easter.

Granny ran the kitchen. The old house had a wood stove that Hollis had cleaned out, and she cooked on it the way she had cooked when she was twelve, and she ordered everyone around, and everyone did what she said. She had been seen to eat sardines on crackers twice more, once at the kitchen table and once on the porch, and both times she had looked straight at John while she ate them, and she would not discuss it.

Charlie and the pug slept under the porch in the cool dirt, curled together. The pug had gained eight pounds. She still burped. Every morning Trevor's red hand cart carried water from the pump to the garden, two blue jugs at a time.

And John had a notebook.

It was a spiral notebook, college-ruled, from a box of school supplies Dee had found in her attic. On the cover, in black marker, he had written: WHAT TO DO NEXT TIME.

On the first page, in capital letters, it said: 1. FILL THE TUB.

On the second page, it said: 2. DO NOT TOUCH THE TUB.

The third page was the instructions for setting his tactical flashlights to stay on low, written out from memory, step by step, every click and every hold. It had taken him a week to remember them. He had gotten them right.

There were other pages. There was a page about rationing. There was a page about the toilet tank. There was a page, near the back, that just said, READ EVERYTHING, and under it, in smaller letters, *Priya was right*.

* * *

The mail came in April, for the first time in three months. The rural carrier, a gray-bearded man in a faded blue postal jacket, drove it down the dirt road in a 1970s Jeep with no radio, and he had three things for them, tied with string.

There was a letter from Matt, in pencil, on notebook paper. *Still here. Lost thirty pounds. Burned my bookshelves, then my neighbor's. The Mosin makes a good fireplace poker. Tell Sanda I'm sorry I said Florida wasn't real. It is very real. I'm coming down there.*

There was a postcard with a picture of a lake in Lake Mary on the front and, on the back, in a small, neat, old-fashioned hand: *The Guard brought insulin on the ninth day. I am well. The Nguyens are with her mother in Kissimmee. The dumpster is gone. Your grandmother still owes me a dance. — Ramón Alvarez.*

And there was a typed letter on plain paper from a man at the state, a thin, polite man in a necktie and a windbreaker who had already come down the road once, in March, in an old Bronco, to ask John where seven printouts were, and had written down the answer — *in the trunk of a gray Volkswagen in a garage behind a gate in Lake Mary, under the spare tire* — on a clipboard. The letter said that Palmetto Grid Software was rebuilding Restore from the first line, by hand, and that every line would be read by two people before it was used, and that they were looking for readers.

John read it twice. He put it in the back of his notebook. He had not decided. He had a pump to look after.

* * *

In the last week of April, a truck came down the dirt road.

It was a bucket truck, old, from the eighties, white with a co-op's name painted on the doors, and it had no screen in it anywhere. Out of it climbed a lineman, a big, slow-moving young man in a hard hat and a tan flame-resistant shirt with the sleeves rolled, with a pinch of snuff in his lip and the round ring of the can worn white into the back pocket of his jeans. He nodded to all of them on the porch. He did not say much. He walked to the pole at the end of the road, where the line ran down to the old house, and he raised a long fiberglass stick up to the top of it, and with his own two hands, he closed the switch.

That was all. That was the whole thing.

In March, John had rewired the old house's kitchen himself. He had a degree in electrical engineering that he had not used for twenty years, and Hollis had a booklet in his barn from the Rural Electrification Administration, printed in nineteen fifty-two, called *Wiring Your Farm Home*, and John had read every page of it with his mother's glasses on. He had hung a single porcelain socket from the middle of the kitchen ceiling on a cloth-covered wire, with a little brass pull chain, and screwed in a bulb. Hollis had dug an old meter out of the barn, the kind with a spinning wheel, and they had put it on the wall outside. They had left it switched on. Just in case.

The bulb came on.

It came on in the middle of the kitchen ceiling, hanging on its wire, with no shade, just the bulb, yellow and warm and very bright in the dim little room, and everyone came in from the porch and stood under it and looked up.

Charlie barked at it.

"No bark," John said.

Charlie stopped.

Granny stood in the middle of the kitchen with her church fan in her hand and looked up at the bulb for a long time. She was ninety years old. She had been twelve years old the first time a man from the co-op came down this road and the lights came on, in her mama's kitchen, a bulb on a wire with no cover on it, and her daddy had pulled the chain, and nobody had said a word.

"Well," Granny said. "Took 'em long enough."

The bulb flickered.

It was nothing. It was one quick dip and back, the way bulbs do, the way every light bulb in the world has always done once in a while, when a breeze moves a line somewhere or a transformer hums or the current shifts on its way down a long wire from far away. It flickered once. Everyone saw it.

John looked at it.

He stood there under it and looked at it, and it did not flicker again. It burned steady and yellow and quiet over all their heads. He did not wonder. He decided, standing there, not to wonder, the way you decide to put something down.

He reached up and pulled the little brass chain, and the bulb went out.

"Let's not get used to it," John said.

They went back out onto the porch. All of them: Jim and Liz, and Granny with her fan, and Dorina, and Sanda, and John, and the two dogs under the boards, and the chickens settling in the motor home with its rifle on the roof, and the evening coming down blue over the pines. Nobody turned on a light. They sat on the porch in the dark, on the steps and the dining chairs and the porch rail, and they talked — about the garden, about Pearl, about Hollis's tractor, about Earl Tidwell's hog, about nothing — which was a thing John had once not been able to imagine people doing, before electricity, in the dark, for hours. They did it for hours.

After a long while, Sanda leaned her head against his good shoulder.

"So," Sanda said. "About the house."

John looked out at the dark yard, at the pump, at the garden, at the gray shape of the old house behind them with its half a porch and its tin roof and its one bulb, off.

"You were right," John said.

"I know," Sanda said. "I meant there's a chicken in it."
