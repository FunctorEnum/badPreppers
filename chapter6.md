# Chapter Six

## The Apartment

> *"Your Bordeaux is resting at a perfect 55°F. 🍷"*
>
> — VinoSense app notification, Sunday, 6:12 a.m.

On Sunday mornings they had coffee in bed, with Charlie's permission.

The idea was to wake up slowly, without alarms, and drink a cup of coffee propped up on the pillows and talk about anything they had been too busy to talk about during the week. In practice Charlie woke them at a quarter past six by standing on John's chest and staring into his face from four inches away, breathing, until John got up and walked him and made the coffee and brought it back to bed, where Sanda would be sitting up waiting with her glasses on, as if she had done something.

This Sunday she was sitting up in an old gray t-shirt from a ten-kilometer race in Lincoln Park that neither of them had run in, and she was holding out her hand for the mug before John had cleared the doorway.

"You did an amazing job with that AR yesterday," John said, handing it to her and climbing back under the covers in his Bears t-shirt and boxers. "I'm really proud of you."

"Anyone could shoot that thing," Sanda said, blowing across her coffee. "It's easy. You don't need to be proud of me."

"I mean I'm proud you weren't afraid of it." That did not come out the way he meant, and he tried again. "You used to be afraid of all of it. And yesterday you just picked it up. With the way things are going, it's good that you know how. You just never know."

Sanda took a sip and looked at him over the rim of the mug, and he could see her deciding whether it was too early for this conversation, and deciding that it was, and having it anyway.

"Does it really matter?" Sanda asked.

"Does what matter?"

"Any of it. The AR. The range. All your guns." She set the mug on her knee. "We live in an apartment, John. If the world ends, how are we supposed to survive in an apartment? We don't own anything. We don't own the walls. And honestly — would we even want to live in that kind of world?" She looked at the ceiling. "We should buy a house. With land. Real land, not a yard. Space for your guns, space for a garden. We could have chickens."

"Chickens," John said.

"Chickens," Sanda repeated firmly. "You know who was okay in Romania, after? The people with houses and gardens. My grandmother had a house in the village with a garden and chickens and a well, and when there was nothing in the stores in Bucharest, we ate because of her garden. Everyone in the apartment blocks stood in line for bread for three hours. My grandmother walked out her back door and picked tomatoes."

John took a long sip of his coffee to give himself time.

"Okay," John said. "One thing to think about. Let's say everything goes to hell. Real hell. You're a bad guy. You're driving around in a pickup truck with four of your buddies, and they've all got rifles, and there are no police, because the police are all home protecting their own families. You're looking for food, guns, drugs, whatever. You're driving down a country road, and you see a nice big house sitting all by itself on ten acres. No neighbors. Woods on three sides. Who's in there?"

"A family," Sanda said.

"A rich family," John said. "Probably an older couple, with a nice house and a nice truck, and probably no guns, or one shotgun, and no training. And the house promises all kinds of good stuff inside. Big house, big land — they must have something. Now compare that to this." He gestured with his mug at the bedroom, the apartment, the whole complex beyond the blinds. "A gated complex with two hundred apartments that all look exactly the same. Which one would you pick? Apartments blend in. Nobody looks at an apartment and thinks, there's the treasure. If you were the guy in the truck, which one would you pick?"

He felt he had made a sound case for staying put. He sat back against the headboard.

Sanda thought about it carefully. She drank some coffee. She looked at him.

"I think we should own our own house," Sanda said.

"Think about this, though," John said, pressing on. "Remember the hurricane? Our first fall back? Four days without power. That was one storm, in one part of one state. Now imagine that everywhere, at once, and nobody coming to fix it. The first few weeks would be the worst. Panic. Looting. Guys in trucks going house to house. And you've got some big house in the country with windows on every side and three doors and a garage. How do you defend that? You'd need an army. Inside and outside. There is no way we could do it." He started counting on his fingers. "Your mom just learned to shoot a twenty-two rifle, which is a squirrel gun. My dad hates guns and admits he's a terrible shot. My mom — Dad says she's a good shot, but I have never once seen her hold a gun, and I've known her my whole life. And you just shot an AR for the first time yesterday. You don't know how to load your own magazines. You don't know how to clear a jam. You've never field-stripped it."

"What's field-stripped?"

"Taking it apart to clean it."

"So I'll learn to take it apart," Sanda said. "Big deal. You learned on YouTube."

"That's my point," John said, a little heated now. He took a breath and tried to put the heat back. "And remember the hurricane? What did you learn from the hurricane?"

"That we need a house with a generator," Sanda said immediately.

"I learned we needed a second Desert Eagle."

Sanda looked at him for a very long moment.

"You did buy a second Desert Eagle," Sanda said slowly. "That October. I thought it was for your birthday."

"It was a hurricane lesson."

"What was the lesson?"

John opened his mouth. He found that he could no longer remember what the lesson had been, only that it had seemed very clear at the time, sitting in the dark on the third day with no air conditioning, sweating through his shirt and cleaning the first Desert Eagle by the light of a headlamp.

"Well," Sanda said, throwing back the covers, "I don't think the world's ending this morning. Maybe you're right about the apartment. I don't know. I'm going to make us breakfast."

That was how most of their arguments ended. A little back and forth, and then Sanda had had enough and some urgent household task appeared out of nowhere, and the subject was closed. John had learned over twenty years not to push. Sanda needed to percolate. If he left an idea alone with her for a while, it would steep, and sometimes, days later, she would come back to it on her own, and sometimes they would even land on the same side.

Breakfast was eggs and grits. It had been eggs and grits on weekends ever since they moved back, as a way of reminding themselves where they were; in Chicago, grits were either a novelty on a brunch menu or something a coworker made a face about. Sanda had figured out how to fry an egg sunny-side up without breaking the yolk or browning the edges, which John considered a form of witchcraft, and she slid two of them onto a bed of creamy, buttery grits that had simmered for forty minutes, because they were never, ever instant.

"No self-respecting Southerner," John said, picking up his fork and quoting the line from *My Cousin Vinny*, "uses instant grits."

"You're not a self-respecting Southerner," Sanda said, sitting down across from him. "You lived in Chicago for twelve years and you put ketchup on a hot dog."

"I never put ketchup on a hot dog."

"Twice," Sanda said. "I saw you."

* * *

On Monday Sanda called him on his drive home, sounding through the car speakers like a woman shouting from inside a mailbox during a gale, and asked if he wanted to try the Korean place by the Target. He said yes, mostly to end the call.

The Korean place had a special they had never tried, called the Korean Barbecue Experience. The server, a cheerful young woman in a black apron with her hair in two buns, brought out a round gas grill and set it in the middle of their table and lit it with a click. It looked like a wok turned upside down, a dark iron dome over a ring of blue flame, and around it she arranged platters of raw beef and pork sliced so thin you could read through them, and little dishes of kimchi and pickled radish and sesame sauce, and whole cloves of garlic, and sliced jalapeños, and a stack of cold romaine leaves.

"You cook it yourself," the server said, demonstrating with a pair of long tongs. "Put it on the dome. Ten seconds. Flip. Ten seconds. Put it in the lettuce, add what you like, roll it up. Like a taco."

They laid the meat on the hot dome and it hissed and curled and browned at the edges, and they flipped it and pulled it off and wrapped it in the cold lettuce with a slice of grilled garlic and a coin of jalapeño and a drizzle of sriracha. Sanda took the first bite with her eyes closed. She chewed. She opened her eyes and they were very wide.

"Okay," Sanda said, through the lettuce. "That's it. We're doing this at home."

John sighed. One of Sanda's great joys in life was eating something remarkable at a restaurant and then figuring out how to make it at home, exactly, better. After a week in Zürich she had mastered fondue. After a weekend in New Orleans she had mastered beignets, and they had eaten beignets for a month, and John had gained four pounds. Now there would be Korean barbecue.

The next afternoon she stopped at the Korean grocery on her way home from the bank and came back with everything: the meats, the sauces, the kimchi in a jar the size of a paint can, and the grill itself, in a box with a picture of a happy family on the side. It ran on the little green one-pound propane canisters that people used for camping, and she had bought four of them.

"Another appliance," John said, watching her unpack it on the high-top.

"It's not an appliance," Sanda said. "It's an experience."

He did not think they needed it.

* * *

On Wednesday night, in bed, with the lights out, Sanda said, "The forecast was right."

John, who had been nearly asleep, said, "What?"

"Carla's thing. The ledger for Monday, that came on Saturday." Her voice was very quiet in the dark. "It was right. The whole day. Deposits, withdrawals, every drawer. It was off by eleven dollars for the entire branch, and the eleven dollars was a man who came in at four fifty-five and wasn't supposed to." She was quiet for a moment. "Brianna's drawer was perfect. She cried again. Happy, this time."

John lay looking at the ceiling. He did not know what to say, so he said nothing, which was sometimes the right thing.

"I think you might be right about the apartment," Sanda said.

He turned his head on the pillow. Three days. That was about average.

"Yeah?"

"Maybe. I don't know." He heard her turn toward him on the pillow. "Can you buy barbed wire?"

He was not sure he had heard her correctly. "You want me to buy barbed wire."

"For the windows," Sanda said, as if it were obvious. "If somebody breaks a window to get in, it'll slow them down. Can you buy it on Amazon?"

John reached for his phone on the nightstand, and the screen lit up the bedroom blue, and he opened the app and typed in *barbed wire*, feeling ridiculous. It came up instantly.

"Huh," John said. "Yeah. You can. Class three galvanized, fifteen-and-a-half gauge. A quarter mile. Ninety-four dollars." He turned his head on the pillow to look at her in the glow. "You actually want me to buy this?"

"I think it's a good idea."

"And do what with it? String it across the inside of the windows like Christmas lights?"

"No," Sanda said, thinking out loud now. "We'd need plywood too. Plywood over the windows, so nobody can see in and nobody can just smash the glass and climb in. And then if they break the glass and kick through the plywood, there's the barbed wire. Do you have a nail gun?"

He was completely awake now.

"And think about this," Sanda went on. "If the power goes out for a long time, and a window gets broken — for any reason, a storm, a branch, anything — then the outside is just in the house. You have to keep people out, but you also have to keep out the animals. Snakes. Raccoons. The bugs. This is Florida, John. Everything here wants to come inside."

He had never once thought of that. In ten years of thinking about the end of the world, in all his scenarios and plans and late-night videos, it had not occurred to him that a broken window in Florida would let in more than people. He was a little disappointed in himself. He did not say so.

"Okay," John said slowly, scrolling. "So I guess I'll buy a box of barbed wire. Which means I need special gloves, so I don't shred my hands." He found them, heavy leather gauntlets that came up to the elbow, and added them to the cart. "And a nail gun." He scrolled, and his thumb stopped, and his voice changed. "Oh, man. Look at this. It's cordless. It has a little air compressor built right into it, and the battery kicks it on when you pull the trigger, and it shoots two-inch nails."

He had wanted a nail gun for ten years, ever since he watched Denzel Washington take apart a roomful of Russian gangsters in *The Equalizer* using, among other things, a nail gun from a hardware store. He added it to the cart.

"And the plywood?" Sanda asked.

"I'll get that at Home Depot. This weekend."

He never would. He did not know that yet. He would think about the plywood several times, always on the way somewhere else.

He lay there with the phone glowing on his chest, looking at his cart — a quarter mile of barbed wire, a pair of gauntlets, and a cordless nail gun — and he could not believe the conversation he was having. Buying guns had always felt perfectly reasonable to him. Buying barbed wire felt insane. It occurred to him that most people would see it exactly the other way around, that his neighbors would think nothing of a box of barbed wire and would call the police about an AR-15, and he did not know what that said about him, or about them. He felt, for the first time, that it was getting harder to tell himself apart from the people on the videos, the man in the shipping container, the woman with the wall of rice. At least he wasn't buying canned food and water and a year of toilet paper. At least he had a plan, and the plan was guns.

Then, because he was a responsible man, he searched for water, and found a case of Perrier, twenty-four half-liter bottles, and added it to the cart.

"There," John said, and tapped *Place your order*. "Water."

"That's sparkling water," Sanda said into her pillow.

"It's still water."

"Mm-hm," Sanda said, and fell asleep.

* * *

The box came Thursday afternoon. John knew it had come because his phone told him, at his desk at work, with a soft chime: *Someone is at your front door.* He opened the doorbell app and watched a delivery driver in a brown uniform set a long, heavy box on his mat, take a photograph of it, and walk away, and Charlie, invisible inside the apartment, barked at the man the whole way to the truck.

When he got home there was also a notice taped to the door from the leasing office, on letterhead, which said that the community's front and rear gates were being upgraded to a new cloud-based access system from Halcyon Access Systems, and that all residents should download the Halcyon app for keyless entry, and that the community thanked them for their patience during this exciting improvement.

"Another app," John said, peeling the notice off the door.

The gate had been half-installed for two weeks. The old keypad was still there, but the steel enclosure next to it, where the gate's controller lived, had been left with its cover off, and the cover was nowhere to be seen. John, because he was an engineer and could not walk past an open box with wires in it, had stopped the car on his way out one morning and looked inside. There was a new green circuit board in there, bolted to the back of the box, with a cellular antenna on a short cable and a little blinking light, and the board was stamped in white letters with the name HALCYON. Below it sat the old motor and its fat power leads, unchanged, with a low-voltage line coming in from a transformer on the post. He had noted how it was wired, the way he noted everything, and had driven to work and forgotten it.

He carried the box of barbed wire into the garage and set it on the shelf beside the Christmas bins, and he did not open it.

Then he came back inside and stood in the middle of his apartment and looked around, as if he had never seen it before, and began, for no reason he could have explained, to count.

The front door had a smart lock that opened with an app on his phone or with a code on a keypad or — thank God — with a key, an actual brass key on his actual key ring, which he had insisted on keeping. Next to the door was the video doorbell, which watched the walk and the street and sent a picture to his phone every time a lizard crossed the welcome mat. On the wall in the hall was the smart thermostat, which learned their habits, and which had apparently learned that John liked it at seventy-one and Sanda liked it at seventy-four, and which now spent most of its time trying to split the difference, the way a marriage counselor would. On the bookshelf was the smart speaker with its blue ring. In the corner of the living room, on its charging dock, sat the robot vacuum, a fat black disk the size of a hubcap that wandered the apartment every weekday at noon, sucking up Charlie's hair and getting stuck under the couch. Sanda had named it Dorel.

"Why Dorel?" John had asked, the day it arrived.

"Because Dorel is the guy in every Romanian joke," Sanda had explained. "He's the workman. He's the one who builds the stairs upside down. He installs the toilet on the ceiling. He paints the wall and then paints the window too, because it was there. Everything Dorel does, he does with complete confidence, and it's wrong."

On the kitchen floor beside Charlie's water bowl stood Charlie's feeder, a white plastic tower with a hopper of kibble and a camera on the front and a little speaker, so that John and Sanda could open an app at work and see Charlie and talk to him and drop him a treat. They did this several times a day. Charlie had never understood it. Whenever their voices came out of the feeder, he would bark at it furiously, as though it had swallowed them.

And beside the high-top, humming quietly, was the wine cooler, forty-six bottles in two climate zones, reds on top at fifty-five, whites on the bottom at fifty, and it had an app too, which Sanda checked every morning in bed.

"Why does the wine cooler have an app?" John asked, standing in front of it.

"So I know the temperature," Sanda said from the couch, where she was flipping through a catalog.

"You're standing next to it."

"I'm on the couch."

"You're standing next to it every morning. You walk past it to make coffee. It has a little screen on the front that says the temperature. In numbers."

"The app has a graph," Sanda said, without looking up. "And it tells me when I'm low on Sancerre."

John looked around the apartment. Everything he could see — the lock, the doorbell, the thermostat, the speaker, Dorel, the feeder, the wine cooler, the television, his laptop, her laptop, their phones, the car in the garage — was talking to something, all the time, somewhere. He had never added it up before. It had come into their lives one convenient thing at a time, like water rising in a basement, and he had never once stood still long enough to notice that it had reached his knees.

* * *

On Friday night Sanda lit the grill on the kitchen counter for the first time, and John stood beside it with a glass of wine in his hand watching the blue ring of flame under the black iron dome, and it occurred to him, slowly, the way things sometimes occurred to him, that this little thing did not need a wall outlet. It did not need the power company at all. If the power ever went out — they did live in hurricane country now, after all — they could still cook on it. They could boil water on it. People out in the country had generators that could run a whole house for weeks after a storm. Apartment people had nothing. And now, at least, they had this.

He felt, for a moment, very prepared.

After the Korean barbecue, he took Charlie out for his last walk, and across the street, in front of the third apartment down, there was a pug.

It was a female, small and fawn-colored, with a black mask over her flat face and a tail curled up over her back in a tight, perfect cinnamon roll, and enormous dark eyes that bulged a little, so that she looked permanently astonished by the world. She snorted when she breathed. When she saw Charlie she lost her mind entirely, and so did he, and the two of them hit the ends of their leashes at the same instant and began to wag so hard their whole bodies bent.

At the other end of the pug's leash was an older woman John had seen many times and had never once spoken to beyond the dogs. She was in her sixties, maybe five-foot-four and thin, with a deep Florida tan and freckles across her cheeks and the backs of her hands, and blond hair that was clearly from a box, with half an inch of gray showing at the roots. She wore a pink velour tracksuit, white Skechers, and a quilted black vest, and she was holding a long thin cigarette away from the dogs in her free hand, the way you would hold a sparkler.

"Here we go," the woman said, in a deep, scratchy smoker's voice, laughing. "They're in love."

The dogs met in the middle of the street and immediately began running in circles around each other, and in four seconds their leashes were braided together and the two humans were being reeled in toward each other, stepping over and under, holding their leashes up over their heads like people doing a folk dance.

"Sorry," John said, ducking under. "He doesn't get out much."

"Neither does she," the woman said, stepping over. "She's a homebody, aren't you, baby? Yes, you are."

She said the pug's name then, and John heard it and nodded and smiled, and the name went in one ear and straight out the other, the way names always did with him, especially women's names, especially in the dark.

"She's very cute," John said, which was what you said.

"She's spoiled rotten," the woman said fondly, finally untangling herself with a practiced spin. "She eats grain-free salmon formula. Forty dollars a bag." She laughed her scratchy laugh. "I eat ramen noodles. Don't tell anybody."

"I won't," John said, laughing too, because he thought it was a joke.

She scooped the pug up under one arm, and the pug craned around her elbow to keep looking at Charlie, snorting, and the woman waved her cigarette at John and said, "Night, now," and went inside. John never did get her name. It did not occur to him that it mattered.

* * *

That night, in the recliner, he found the post.

It was on the kind of website where engineers went to argue with each other, and it had been written by a security researcher in the Netherlands who studied the software inside cheap internet routers. The title was plain, the way the best ones always were: *Undocumented "maintenance hooks" in router firmware from at least eleven vendors.* John read it twice, one paragraph at a time.

The researcher had been taking apart the software in a popular home router and had found a hidden channel in it that he could not explain. It was not a bug. It was carefully built. It kept a quiet connection open to an outside server and listened there for instructions, and when instructions came, it could change the router's settings, update its software, and reroute its traffic, all without the owner ever knowing. The code that built it was clean and well commented, and the comments called it a *self-healing maintenance channel*. Then he had looked at routers from other companies, and found the same channel, built the same way, in ten more. None of the companies he contacted could tell him who had written it. Several of them, he noted dryly, had used AI-generated code for their recent firmware updates.

At the bottom of the post, he listed the outside servers that the channels connected to. They followed a pattern.

*relay-1.opsmesh.net. relay-2.opsmesh.net. relay-3.opsmesh.net.* All the way to nine.

The seventh one was *relay-7.opsmesh.net*. John had typed that exact name into his own terminal eleven days ago, at ten minutes to five on a Monday afternoon, in an empty office, with Priya's notebooks on the desk beside him. And then he had clicked *Approve*.

John sat very still in the recliner. He could feel his own heartbeat in his ears, under the tinnitus, like a man knocking on a wall in the next apartment.

He clicked the bookmark star at the top of the browser. Then, because he wanted to read it again, he refreshed the page.

*This post has been removed at the request of the affected vendor.*

He went back to the discussion thread, which was already three hundred comments long. Halfway down was a statement from one of the router companies, posted by an account with a gold checkmark beside its name. *These are standard self-healing maintenance features, documented in our terms of service, designed to keep customer devices secure and up to date. There is nothing to see here.* Somebody had replied to it with a single laughing emoji. Somebody else had replied, *Then why'd you take it down?* Nobody had replied to that.

John searched for an archived copy, and after twenty minutes he found one, saved by some stranger an hour before the post came down. He scrolled down past the technical part, past the list of relays, to the very bottom, where the researcher had listed the eleven companies whose firmware he had examined. Most of them made routers. A few made other things — security cameras, a brand of smart plugs, a baby monitor.

The seventh name on the list was Halcyon Access Systems.

He got up out of the recliner and went to the front window and moved one slat of the blinds aside with his finger.

At the end of the drive, a hundred yards away under its orange sodium light, the new gate stood closed across the entrance to the Preserve. The steel box beside it still had no cover. From here he could just make out, inside it, a tiny green light, blinking slowly and steadily in the dark, the way a thing blinks when it is waiting.
