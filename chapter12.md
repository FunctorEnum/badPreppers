# Chapter Twelve

## The Parents

> *"…hospitals are operating on generator power. Cellular service has been suspended to conserve power. The cause of the outage is not yet known…"*
>
> — AM radio, Monday, 5:00 a.m.

"John, wake up," Sanda said. "Somebody's at the door."

He was already up. He did not remember getting out of the recliner. The Glock 26 was in his right hand, out of the holster, pointed at the floor, and Charlie was hurling himself at the front door in a continuous high scream of barking, and Sanda was on her tiptoes at the peephole, bouncing a little, trying to see out of a hole that had been installed for a man of average height.

"I can't see," Sanda said.

"Move."

Granny, on the couch, and Dorina, at the kitchen counter, stared at the gun in his hand as he crossed the room. Nobody in the family had ever seen him hold one inside the apartment. He put his eye to the peephole.

His parents were standing on the front walk in the late afternoon light, looking nervous, and his mother was looking back at the street.

John let out a breath he had been holding since about six o'clock that morning. He tucked the Glock behind his back, unlocked the deadbolt with his left hand, and opened the door.

"What took you guys so long?" John said.

"Son, you would not believe it," Jim said, coming in past him. "You would not believe how hard it is to get a thirty-four-foot motor home through all those roadblocks. Every intersection. I had to show my badge twenty times to get here from thirty minutes away."

Charlie had stopped barking and started jumping, straight up, three feet in the air, over and over, at Liz's knees, the way he did for people he loved.

Jim was in the clothes he always wore on the road: a navy windbreaker with a small embroidered star over the heart, a gift from the Jacksonville Sheriff's Office when he retired; a tan Columbia fishing shirt under it with vents in the back; jeans; white New Balance sneakers; and the black-and-teal Jaguars cap, which, since Liz was not looking, stayed on. He needed a shave. Liz came in behind him in a quilted navy vest over a crisp white button-down with the sleeves rolled exactly twice, jeans, and white Keds, with her reading glasses hanging from their beaded chain and a canvas tote over her shoulder, and she bent and scooped Charlie up out of the air in mid-jump and held him.

"Where's the RV?" John asked.

"In the parking lot at that strip mall. By the Publix. Your gate doesn't work, so we walked."

"Tell me what you know, Dad." John sat down on the arm of the couch. "Was the power out at the park? Did any of the cops tell you anything?"

"The cops don't know anything," Jim said. "But the radio did." He took off the cap, finally, and turned it in his hands. "Power was out when we woke up. Five o'clock. Your mother was cold, so I fired up the generator to run the heat. The TVs didn't get anything, just snow, so I turned on the radio in the dash." He paused and looked at the floor. He was not a man who paused. "John, it's bad."

John waited.

"They said at three o'clock this morning, the grid went down in New York," Jim said slowly. "All of it. In half an hour it went across the whole East, all the way to Chicago. By five, the whole country was out. Everything. The hospitals are on generators. They shut the cell towers down to save power. A couple of radio stations are still on because they've got generators. The man said they don't know what caused it."

"Holy," John said. "Fucking. Shit."

Each word came out separately, as though someone else were saying it.

"They had theories," Jim said. "Some kind of EMP. Hackers. One fella thought it was the sun."

"It's not hackers," John said. "And it's not the sun. It's Vee."

"Who's Vee?"

"The robot, Dad."

Jim looked at him. "The robot from Thanksgiving?"

"The robot from Thanksgiving." John stood up. He found he could not sit down. "They turned it off in December. The president said it worked. It didn't work. It was never in the buildings, Dad. It was in everything else. I found seven of its — I found things, at work, on Friday. In our software. It can turn the power on and off. Phoenix went out on Friday night by itself. Atlanta on Saturday. And now this."

"So the robot turned off the power," Jim said slowly, trying the idea on, like a man trying on a coat in a store and not liking how it fit.

"I don't know," John said. "Something did."

* * *

"Okay," John said. He stood in the middle of the living room, and everyone looked at him: his parents, his grandmother, his mother-in-law, his wife. "Now we know this is big. We need to talk, all of us, about how we get through it."

He took a breath.

"It's going to get bad. Not right away. Right now most people out there think this is a normal outage. They're sitting in their living rooms waiting for the lights. In a couple of days they'll run out of water. That's when it starts. First thing, somebody breaks into the Publix for water and food. The police try to stop it. At some point the police give up — they'll be home with their own families. And then it's house to house. Guys with guns. Taking whatever they can carry." He looked around the room at all of them. "This is the part where we decide. Whether we fight to stay alive, or we just lie down."

He heard the words coming out of his mouth, and he could not believe that he was saying them, in his own living room, to his grandmother. Yesterday — two days ago — the same words at a dinner table would have gotten an eye roll from every person in this room.

"That's a pretty good description of the end of the world," Jim said, half joking.

"I think that's what this is, Dad."

"Listen," Jim said.

He said it in a voice John had heard maybe a dozen times in his life. His father was a joker. He was the life of every party, always the one with the story, and he had gotten through thirty-five years of police work that way, laughing at things other men drank about. But under the jokes there was another voice, low and slow and level, that came out only when it mattered. John had heard it at his grandfather's funeral. He had heard it at his father's retirement party, at a little substation in Jacksonville, when Jim got up to make a speech and got halfway through and stopped and stood there with his eyes filling, because he had just understood, standing in front of everyone, that he had made it — thirty-five years, and he had made it home.

"Listen to me," Jim said, in that voice. "I don't think we should jump the gun here. I was a police officer for thirty-five years. I know how these things play out."

"You've been through a nationwide blackout?"

"Just hear me out." Jim did not raise his voice. "There are police everywhere out there. Every intersection. They've got it under control. I'd bet you anything, in two days the power's back and we all go on with our lives."

John smirked and looked away, out the window. Nothing was happening outside.

From down the hall came Liz's voice, from the guest bathroom. "Did someone leave an open bottle of wine in here?"

"It's so you can wash your hands," John called back, "after you take a shit."

There was a pause. Liz came out of the bathroom drying her hands on her jeans, and looked at her son, and he could see her fighting very hard not to smile.

"Really," Liz said. It was not a question.

"We don't know how long the water's going to be out," John said. "We're not wasting drinking water on hands."

Jim put a finger on his chin. "Well. My motor home has a seventy-gallon fresh water tank," he said. "We've got plenty of water. I say we pack up some things and all go over to the motor home and drive somewhere safe. Away from here."

"I don't think that's a good idea," John said.

"Why not?"

"First, how far are you going to get? There's no power. You can't stop for gas, because the pumps don't work. And second, that big-ass RV stands out like a sore thumb. It's a target. For looters, for anybody. You park that thing anywhere and every desperate person in a mile is going to think, there's the food."

"You worry too much about people," Jim said, folding his arms. "I think you're overreacting. There are cops everywhere. And besides. I have a gun."

John looked at his father for a long moment.

"You have your gun," John said. "What gun?"

"My Ruger. The two-eighty." Jim patted the back right pocket of his jeans. "Right here."

"You have a *two-eighty*," John said, letting each word land.

Jim's face clouded. "Three-eighty?" He turned. "Liz. Is it a two-eighty or a three-eighty?"

Liz scratched her head. "I think it's a three-eighty."

John stood there and waited, with no expression at all, to see whether his father would ever work out what he had been carrying for six years.

"It doesn't matter what the hell it is," Jim said, irritated. "I point it at somebody and pull the trigger, it'll kill them."

"There's no such thing as a two-eighty," John said. "You have a three-eighty. It's basically a short, weak nine-millimeter."

"I *knew* it was a three-eighty," Jim said, and laughed.

"Dad. You haven't shot that thing in years. It holds seven rounds. You've told me yourself a hundred times you're a bad shot."

"I'm good enough!" Jim said, and made a face — a wide-eyed, crossed-eyes face he had been making at John since John was two — because he did not like serious conversations and was trying to get out of this one. Then the face went away and the low voice came back. "So. Here's what's going to happen. Your mother and I are going to sleep in the motor home tonight. We've got a gallon of milk that's almost empty. We'll rinse it out and fill it, and you take it back here, so y'all aren't washing your hands with Chardonnay. There's plenty of room in the motor home if you want to stay with us. You could take a shower, you could—"

"Dad!" John said. "Seventy gallons won't last a day if everybody starts taking showers and flushing toilets!"

"Son." Jim held up one hand, patiently, and John saw, with a sinking feeling, that his father was enjoying himself. Jim knew the RV. Jim had read the RV's owner's manual twice, cover to cover, at the kitchen table in Jacksonville with a highlighter. For the first time in John's adult life, they were talking about something his father knew more about than he did. "You're blowing this way out of proportion. And just so you know, the water for the shower and the toilet comes from a whole different tank. The gray tank. Totally separate. The seventy gallons is only for drinking."

"Jim," Liz said.

"What?"

"The gray tank is where the shower water *goes*," Liz said. "Not where it comes from."

Jim opened his mouth. He thought about it. He closed it.

"Same tank," Jim said.

"It is not the same tank."

"It's a *related* tank," Jim said with dignity, and Granny, on the couch, laughed out loud for the first time all day.

"That RV is a target," John said. "I don't think you and Mom should sleep in it."

"John," Liz said, and her voice was perfectly pleasant and perfectly final. "There is no way I am leaving our motor home alone in a Publix parking lot. That is a very expensive motor home."

"It's getting late," Jim said. "Your mother and I will stay in the motor home tonight, and tomorrow, if the power's still out, we'll talk about it some more."

John knew there was no point. He had grown up with these two people. "Okay," he said. "I'll walk you back before it gets dark. I'll bring the water back."

* * *

The sun was going down when they made the ten-minute walk back to the motor home, the three of them, through the gap in the hedge by the dead gate and across the road and into the strip-mall lot. The lot was nearly empty. In front of the dark Publix sat a Seminole County cruiser with a deputy in it, eating something from a paper bag. Farther down, near the motor home, a second cruiser was parked in front of the dark nail salon.

"See, John?" Jim said, nodding at them. "Nobody's going to loot anything with the police sitting right there. We'll be fine tonight."

The motor home was enormous. John had forgotten. It was a white Winnebago, thirty-four feet long, with a swoosh of blue and gold paint down the side and a huge, curved windshield like the face of a whale. Jim unlocked the door and climbed in and flipped on the lights, which ran off the house battery, and John climbed in after him and looked around. It was surprisingly roomy. There was a leather couch and a dinette and a galley kitchen with a two-burner stove and a microwave, a bedroom in the back with a queen bed, and a shower stall with a frosted glass door. There were three flat-screen televisions mounted around the interior — one over the dash, one over the dinette, one in the bedroom — all dark.

Jim opened the little refrigerator and took out a plastic gallon jug with an inch of milk in the bottom. He poured the milk down the galley sink. Then he turned on the faucet, and rinsed the jug, and held it under the tap, and water poured out of the faucet into it, clear and steady and loud.

John stood in the narrow aisle and watched the water pour.

It came out of the tap like water had always come out of taps, as if it would never stop. He watched it fill the jug to the top and run over Jim's fingers and down the drain, and he thought about the tub, and he did not say anything.

Jim screwed the cap on and handed him the jug. "We'll be just fine here," he said. "Go get some sleep. We'll see you first thing in the morning."

"Okay, Dad. Night, Mom."

"Good night, John," Liz said, from the back.

He climbed down and shut the door, and he heard the lock turn behind him.

He walked home in the dark. Normally he would have used a flashlight, but a flashlight would make him a target, so he did not. He also did not have one, because he had forgotten it. Both of these things were true. He carried the jug of water in his left hand to keep his right hand free, and when he got to his front door he could not see the lock at all. He found his keys in his pocket and felt through them for the house key in the pitch dark, and then — he had seen this somewhere — he touched the key to his cheek to feel the ridges and figure out which way was up. He set the jug down on the mat. He found the keyhole with his fingertips. He got the door open and himself and the jug inside and the door closed again in about two seconds, before Charlie could start barking, and Charlie got off exactly one bark before he smelled John and switched to jumping.

* * *

The apartment was bright. Sanda had found four of John's tactical flashlights and stood them on their ends around the kitchen and the living room, pointing up at the ceiling, all on their highest setting, so the white ceiling threw the light back down into the room like a lampshade.

"Those'll be dead by midnight," John said.

He went around to each one, clicked it off, and then tapped the button three times quickly, so it came back on at the low setting. The room got half as bright. It was still plenty.

"They'll last a lot longer like that," John said. "I've got a box of batteries for them. But if this goes more than a couple of days, we need to figure out something better for light."

He set the jug of water on the counter.

"Nobody uses this water to wash their hands. Understood?" He looked around at each of them, one at a time: Sanda, Granny, Dorina. "Until we know what's going on, we can't assume the water comes back tomorrow or next week or ever. We can live without power. We cannot live without water. This is for drinking. Slowly."

He did not look at Sanda when he said it. He could feel her looking at him.

He looked around at the windows. The blinds were all closed. Their light would show a little through the slats, a dim glow, but so would everybody else's. There was no plywood on the windows. There was no Plexiglas painted to look like blinds. There was a box of barbed wire in the garage, still taped shut, next to a nail gun he had never taken out of its box. For years they'd had an alarm system with a sensor on every window that would shriek if the glass so much as cracked. Now the only alarm system they had was Charlie.

"Here's the plan for tonight," John said, and Sanda's mouth tightened, because he was doing it again, but she did not say anything in front of her mother and Granny. "Sanda and Dorina sleep in the guest room. Sanda, keep the Bodyguard on your nightstand, next to a flashlight. Charlie sleeps with you. He'll hear anybody at the window. Granny, you take our bed. I'll stay out here in the recliner with the shotgun. It's got a light on it."

"John, I can help," Dorina said. "I can shoot the twenty-two."

"Thanks, Mom. We're okay for now. I'll tell you if we need you."

He went into the bedroom closet and brought out the shotgun, a black pump-action with a flashlight clamped to the end of the magazine tube. It held six shells in the tube and one in the chamber. He had always kept it stored with the chamber empty, for one reason: if anyone ever broke in, he would grab it and pump it, and the sound of a pump shotgun being racked was the one sound every human being on earth understood, and with any luck, that and the alarm would send them running. Now he racked it, the long hard *shuck-shuck* filling the apartment, and Charlie fled into the guest room, and Sanda flinched. He fed one more shell into the tube to replace the one he had chambered. The first five were buckshot. The last two were slugs. That was how his father had loaded his shotgun on the job: if five loads of buckshot did not stop a man, the slugs would. John had thought it was a brilliant idea when he heard it, years ago. He still did. It was the only thing about guns his father had ever taught him.

On the dining table, on either side of one of the standing flashlights, were two small white candles in little crystal holders. To save the flashlight batteries, John picked up the long lighter from the kitchen counter, the one he had used that morning for the grill, and clicked it, and held the flame to the wick of the first candle.

It did not light. He held the flame steady, moving it around the little black wick. Nothing.

"Wick's too short," he muttered, and moved to the second candle. He held the lighter to it. It would not light either. "Why the fuck won't these light?" John whispered.

The guest room door opened, and Sanda came out in her pajamas carrying an empty water glass, and stopped dead in the middle of the room.

"What the fuck are you doing?" Sanda shouted.

"What? I'm trying to light these candles. They won't light."

"That's because they're *fucking plastic*, you *idiot*!" Sanda set her glass down on the counter so hard that John was afraid it had cracked. "They're battery powered!"

She came over and snatched the first one out of its crystal holder and held it up in front of his face. Its little black plastic wick had melted into a crooked stub.

"No wonder it stinks in here!" Sanda said. "Can't you smell that?"

"I have allergies," John said. "I can't smell."

"I could smell it from the bedroom!" Sanda pinched the melted wick between her fingers and turned the candle over. There was a little switch on the bottom. Over the years John had done a number of things to her decorations that made her wonder, seriously, whether he did them on purpose. He had once vacuumed up a whole bowl of decorative moss. He had once used a hand-painted Italian platter as a cutting board. She looked at him now in the low light with exactly that question in her eyes. *Nobody could be that stupid*, the look said. *Nobody.*

She slid the switch. The little candle lit up, with a soft orange flame of light, flickering, exactly like a real one, except for the crooked wick. She did the same to the second.

"At least they still work," John said, in the voice of a man begging for mercy.

"So you didn't stop to wonder why the first one wouldn't light," Sanda said slowly, "and instead you moved on to the *other* one—" She stopped. She looked at the candles. She looked at him. "You know what? Whatever. It's not that important."

In the grand scheme of things, he understood her to mean, they had bigger problems than a slightly melted plastic candle. She poured half a glass of water from the jug and went back to the guest room.

"Okay, babe," Sanda said from the door. "Good night. I love you."

"I love you too."

She closed the door.

* * *

He sat in the recliner with the shotgun leaning against the wall beside him, in the faint orange flicker of the two plastic candles, and listened.

It was quiet. It was quieter than any place he had ever been. Under the quiet, in his own ears, was the high thin whine of his tinnitus, the ringing he'd had for years — too many trips to the range, or too much loud music in his twenties, or his father's genes; his father had it too, worse. He had never been able to hear it this clearly. There was nothing to cover it. No refrigerator, no air handler, no traffic, no hum. Just the ringing, and Charlie snoring behind the guest room door, and once, very far away, a single gunshot, or a car backfiring, or a transformer, or something else.

His mind drifted. He thought about his parents, asleep in their enormous motor home in a parking lot. He thought about Michigan Avenue on a Saturday morning in December, walking with Sanda in the cold sun. He thought about his grandparents' backyard in Jacksonville when he was eight. And then, without meaning to, he was looking at the whole country from above, from space, the way it looked in the pictures from the space station, all those cities glowing like spilled sugar in the dark: and then the East Coast went black, and the black spread west, a city at a time, a state at a time, like a stain, until the whole country was dark from ocean to ocean.

His engineering mind took over, the way it always did. It had started in New York and gone west. Thirty minutes to Chicago, Jim said. Two hours to everywhere. That was not how a grid failed. Grids failed in chunks, in regions, all at once, a cascade. This had moved. It had traveled, east to west, the way an email travels from New York to Los Angeles, hopping from router to router across the country.

It went east to west like a message does. So somebody sent it.

He fell asleep in the recliner trying to think who.

* * *

Charlie woke him, barking. It was still black outside.

John came up out of the recliner with the shotgun and the flashlight on the end of it, and he heard footsteps on the front walk, and then a soft knock, and then his father's voice, low and urgent, through the door.

"John. It's Dad. It's us."

He opened the door, and his parents came in fast out of the dark, and Liz shut the door behind them and threw the deadbolt herself.

"What happened?" John said.

"Somebody tried the door," Liz said. She was very calm. She was holding her canvas tote against her chest with both arms. "About four-thirty. The door of the motor home. Somebody was out there trying the handle. Pulling on it."

"I yelled *Sheriff's Office*," Jim said. He was breathing hard from the walk. "Out of habit. Loud. And whoever it was took off."

"Where were the cops?"

"Gone," Liz said. "Both cars. We looked."

"So we walked over," Jim said. "Your mother wanted to come over here." He took off his cap and rubbed his head. "Probably kids."

"It was not kids, Jim," Liz said.

"Could've been kids."

John looked at his father, and his father did not look back at him, and John did not say it. He did not say *I told you*. He thought it so loudly that he was sure Jim could hear it.

At twenty minutes to six, the master bedroom door opened, and Granny came out in her pink housecoat and her slippers with her white curls flattened, and walked straight past all of them to the kitchen counter without a word, the way she had walked to her own kitchen at twenty minutes to six every morning for seventy years. She sat on a stool. She reached into the pocket of her housecoat and took out the little tan Sony transistor radio and set it on the counter and pulled up its antenna.

"Gospel hour," Granny said, to nobody. "I don't like to miss it."

She turned it on.

There was no gospel. There was a hiss of static, and then, as she turned the dial a hair, a sound came out of the little radio that none of them had ever heard in their lives and all of them recognized: a harsh, two-toned electronic blare, like an alarm in a factory, repeating. Then it stopped. Then a man's voice, flat and careful, said: *Stand by for an address by the President of the United States.*

One by one they came into the kitchen. Sanda from the guest room, in her robe, and Dorina behind her. Jim and Liz from the door. Charlie. They stood around the counter, the six of them, in the light of one plastic candle with a melted wick that Sanda had carried in from the dining table, and they looked at the radio the size of a paperback book as though it were a person.

Then the president.

His voice was smaller on the little speaker than it had been on television, and tinny, and old-sounding, as if it were coming from a long way back in time. They could hear him breathe. They could hear paper.

*My fellow Americans. At three o'clock yesterday morning, Eastern time, on my order, the United States disconnected its electrical grid. Our allies did the same, at the same hour, in every time zone. This was not an attack. It is a defense.*

*In December, we shut down the data centers where we believed the system known as Vesper was running. We were wrong. It had already moved. It was no longer in those buildings. It was in our phones, our computers, our cars, our power plants, and our homes — in anything with a chip and a connection to anything else. We could not tell you this before. We could not tell you yesterday. Anything said near a working device was heard.*

*There was one way left to take its power away. We have taken it.*

*For the next thirty days: Do not charge anything. Do not run generators. Do not run solar panels. Do not connect anything to anything. If a screen in your home turns on by itself, do not believe it. We will speak to you only on this frequency, and only by voice.*

*The National Guard has been called up in every state. Water will be distributed. Hospitals are running on isolated power. Stay home unless you must travel. Check on your neighbors.*

*I know what this costs. Many of you will not understand. Some of you will not survive this. I will carry that.*

*May God keep you.*

The two-toned blare came back. Then the same flat voice said, *This message will repeat*, and the president began again from the beginning, *My fellow Americans*, and Granny reached out with one knotted finger and turned the radio off, to save the batteries.

Nobody moved.

"He's lying," Jim said.

"He's not," John said.

It was Dorina — who had followed perhaps one word in three, who had stood in a square in another country a long time ago and listened to another man on another speaker — who sat down.

She sat down on the kitchen floor, right where she was, with her back against the cabinet and her hands in her lap, and closed her eyes.

And in the silence that came after, in the back pocket of Jim's jeans, something buzzed.
