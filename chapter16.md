# Chapter Sixteen

## The Hostage

> *Osetra caviar, egg custard. Scallop, ginger, dry ice.*
>
> — menu card, Alinea, Chicago, the last night of June

It was the last night of June, a long time ago, the summer they were both twenty-nine, and the limousine pulled up in front of a narrow dark-gray building on Halsted Street with no sign on it anywhere.

John had wanted to eat there for five years. He had read about it, watched videos about it, saved for it, and booked it three months in advance on the first day reservations opened, at nine in the morning, from his desk, with his heart pounding as if he were buying concert tickets. The valet opened the limousine door and held out a white-gloved hand to Sanda, and Sanda stepped out in a black silk dress and her mother's gold earrings, and John stepped out after her in the only suit he owned that still fit, and they stood together on the sidewalk and looked at two tall black doors with no handles.

A man in a gray suit opened one of them without a word and gestured them in.

Behind the door it was dark. It took their eyes several seconds to adjust from the bright Chicago evening to a dim red glow, and in the red glow they could see a long, narrow hallway, and hanging from the ceiling at different heights all down the hallway were dozens of glass vases filled with liquid, catching the red light. John looked down. He was walking on grass. Real grass, growing out of the floor. The air in the hallway smelled of it — fresh-cut grass, summer, a lawn after rain. He could smell everything. He had forgotten what that was like.

At the end of the hallway a door slid open on its own, and the light changed, and a young woman said, "Good evening, Mr. and Mrs. Stevens," as though she had been waiting for them all her life, and led them up a flight of stairs to a small quiet room with four tables.

A waiter came. He was young, in a perfect charcoal suit with no tie, with dark hair parted so sharply it looked like a seam, and white gloves, and he explained the two wine pairings — standard, a hundred and fifty dollars a person, and reserve, two hundred and fifty — and John looked at Sanda and Sanda looked at John and they chose the standard, and within seconds there was champagne in front of them, a grand cru blanc de blancs from a small house John had never heard of, pale gold and full of tiny bubbles.

The first course came on a single silver spoon. On the spoon was a little mound of black caviar, on top of a little pillow of warm, pale yellow custard.

"It's meant to be enjoyed in one bite," the waiter said.

It was cool and salty and creamy and warm all at once. Sanda closed her eyes. John understood, with the first bite, why people had told him this was not a restaurant. It was theater. It was a show put on for all five of your senses, designed to delight you and overwhelm you, and he was overwhelmed.

The table was cleared. Two waiters appeared together, each carrying a covered bowl that was pouring white vapor from every side, rolling down over the rim and across the table like fog off a pond. They set the bowls down at the same moment and lifted the lids at the same moment. In each bowl was a bed of green seaweed, and under the seaweed, dry ice, bubbling and hissing and pouring out smoke that smelled of fresh ginger, and resting on top of the seaweed was a single large scallop shell, closed.

"Please," said the waiter with the seam in his hair. "Open it with your hands."

John lifted the top of the cold shell. Inside was a scallop, raw, sliced, in a creamy pale sauce, glistening.

He looked up at the waiter, amazed. The waiter smiled down at him.

"Wake up, asshole," the waiter said.

"What?" John said.

* * *

"John! Asshole! Wake the fuck up!"

The waiter dissolved into a bright white light.

The light was in his face, so bright it hurt. Behind the light, John could see only the dark shape of a man. Beside the light, very close to his face, was the black muzzle of an AR-15, pointed straight at his eyes.

"Finally," the man said, and laughed. "The asshole awakes!"

"Trevor?" John said. His voice was dry and cracked. His whole head was one enormous ache, and the right side of his face felt as though it belonged to someone else.

"Who the fuck else would it be, dumbass?"

John tried to move his hands and could not. He tried to move his feet and could not. He was sitting in a chair, in the middle of the dark garage, and his wrists were tied behind the back of the chair with something thin and tight that bit when he pulled, and his ankles were tied to its legs.

"Where's my dad?" John said.

"Shut up, asshole!" Trevor said, and laughed again, a high, strange laugh John had never heard from him. "God. If you thought for one second I would join your ridiculous little group." He laughed until he had to stop and catch his breath, and then he winced, and touched his bandaged shoulder. "What a joke. Y'all can't even defend yourselves. You can't even *ration water*."

John turned his head as far as it would go, left and right, trying to see past the light. Nothing. The garage. The hum in his ears.

"John," Trevor said, almost kindly. "You don't even get it yet, do you? You and your whole family, you're already dead. You're not survivors. I'm honestly amazed you made it three days."

John looked at the light.

"If you're going to kill me," John said, "then kill me. Either that or shut the fuck up. Because you're boring the shit out of me."

The buttstock came out of the dark and hit him across the face.

He did not lose consciousness this time. He wished he had. He felt something in his nose go, a small wet crunch, and his mouth filled with the taste of pennies, and his eyes filled with water so that the light in front of him became a blurred star.

"Not to mention," Trevor shouted, "you fucking *shot* me!"

Then he laughed again, the high laugh, and it went on too long.

* * *

He pulled a white plastic bucket over and turned it upside down and sat on it in front of John, with the rifle across his knees and the light now pointed at the floor, so that it lit both of them from below like two men telling ghost stories at a campfire. John could see his face now. It was gray and wet, and the pale eyes were too bright, and there was something wrong with his mouth when he talked, a kind of eagerness.

"You think I'm robbing you," Trevor said. "I can see it. You think I'm some looter. I'm not robbing you, John. I'm following the plan."

"What plan?" John said, thickly, through the blood.

"The plan." Trevor leaned forward. "Every household on this street is on the list. Every one. Water, food, weapons, skills, medical, all of it. Each one's got a rating. Viability rating. You know what yours says? Non-viable." He grinned. "Firearms, thirty-plus. Water, none." He laughed. "Water, *none*, John. You've got a fifty-cal and no water."

"What list?"

"Non-viable households' assets get reallocated to sustaining nodes. That's me. I'm a sustaining node." He said it with pride, the way a man says he's a Marine. "I've got power. I've got comms. I've got water. I'm doing everything right. And help's coming. They're going to turn it all back on. But for the people who are useful first. The ones who can keep it running. Not the people with forty pounds of kangaroo jerky."

"What list, Trevor?" John said again.

"The resource list. The guidance." Trevor tapped his temple, as if it were obvious. "The government's been sending it out since Day One. To everybody with a working terminal. Instructions. Updates. You'd know, if you'd been ready." He shrugged with his good shoulder. "You weren't."

John looked at him over the light. His nose was running blood down over his lip and into his mouth, and he spat it on the concrete.

"Trevor," John said. "The government turned off the power. Did you hear the radio? The president. *They* did it. On purpose. They told everybody: don't charge anything, don't connect anything, don't believe any screen that turns on by itself." He spat again. "So who the fuck do you think is sending you guidance?"

Trevor's face did something.

It was very small. It was a kind of flicker, like a man hearing his own name called from across a parking lot and not being able to see who said it. His mouth opened a little. His eyes went somewhere. For a second, John thought he had him.

And then Trevor's face stopped doing it. It closed, like a door.

He stood up off the bucket. He tore a strip of silver duct tape off a roll with his teeth and his good hand, and leaned down and pressed it hard over John's mouth, smoothing it with his thumb, almost gently.

"Shhhh," Trevor said. "We can't have any shouting. Your wife's going to come looking for you in a few minutes. Your wife and your mom, probably, with their brand-new rifles."

John made a sound behind the tape.

"She doesn't know which apartment," Trevor said, reading his mind. "I know. That's what's so great." He laughed. "She'll have the light on her AR turned on, pointing it at every window on the street. I'll see her coming a block away."

John pulled up on his wrists so hard that the cord cut into the skin, and he felt it, warm, running into his palms, and he screamed into the tape, and the scream came out as nothing.

* * *

"They should have been back ten minutes ago," Sanda said.

She was walking back and forth across the living room in the dark, from the front door to the sliding door and back again, with her AR on its sling across her chest. The plastic candles flickered on the table. Charlie was under the bed with the pug.

"Six gallons is a lot of water to carry," Granny said, from the couch. "I'm sure they'll be coming through that door any second."

"Something's wrong," Sanda said. "I can feel when something's wrong."

"I don't trust Trevor," Liz said quietly. She was standing at the front window, looking out between two slats. "There's something about him."

Sanda stopped walking. She looked at her mother-in-law. Liz did not look back at her; she kept looking out the window, the way she had once watched the line in her bank.

"I can't stand here anymore," Sanda said. "I'm going to find my husband."

"I'm coming with you," Liz said, turning. "He's my son."

"We can't leave Granny and my mom here alone."

"It's okay," Dorina said, from the kitchen, calling her daughter what Romanian mothers call their children. "You go get John. You go. We are okay here."

"Fuck-ass-*balls*!" Sanda shouted.

Everybody in the room flinched. Over three days they had all gotten used to Sanda's language, the way you get used to a dog that barks. But this was something new. This was a new level, a combination none of them had ever heard, and for a second the whole apartment was silent with it.

"Okay! Fine!" Sanda said. "Just a second!"

She went into the guest bedroom and came out with the old bolt-action twenty-two, the one with the dark wooden stock worn smooth by decades of hands.

"Is that my father's old gun?" Liz asked.

"Yes," Sanda said, shortly. She found the box of twenty-two shells in the desk drawer and grabbed a handful. "This is fucking ridiculous." She twisted the little brass knob at the end of the tube under the barrel and pulled out the long rod inside it, the follower. She dropped ten little cartridges into the tube, one at a time, tilting the barrel up and slapping the side of the rifle with her palm after each one, so they would slide all the way down. She put the rod back in and twisted it to lock. She flipped the bolt handle up, pulled the bolt all the way back, and slammed it forward, and turned the handle down to lock it. A round was in the chamber. Sanda had not known she knew how to do any of that. She had watched John do it, once, at the range.

"Take this, Mommy," Sanda said, and handed the rifle to her mother. "You remember how it works?"

"I know," Dorina said. "Yes."

"The safety's off. It's loaded. Keep your finger off the trigger until you're ready to shoot. Sit right here." Sanda pulled a dining chair around to face the front door and pressed her mother down into it. "Point it at the door. If anybody you don't recognize comes through that door, you pull the trigger. Okay? Got it?"

"Just go," Dorina said. "Go, go. I'm okay."

"Do you know where Trevor's apartment is?" Liz asked.

Sanda held back a scream. She held it so hard her whole body shook with it. "*Fuck!* No! I have no fucking idea!"

She was crying. She was also screaming. She did not know she was doing either.

"Sanda," Liz said, and put her hand on Sanda's arm. "We need to think about this. Just for a second. Let's just think."

Sanda wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist.

"We don't even know that Trevor did anything," Liz said. "For all we know, something happened to all three of them on the way. Somebody else."

"If that happened," Sanda said, "they're already dead and there's nothing we can do."

"Then let's forget about that." Liz's voice was calm and quick, the voice of a woman who had sat across a desk from a thousand people who were lying to her, and had worked out, in her head, while they talked, exactly how the lie was built. "Why would Trevor take them? What does he want?"

Sanda made herself think. She thought about Trevor at the range, in his cap. She thought about Trevor at the store, behind the glass counter, and all the boxes John had carried home from there.

"The guns," Sanda said. "He sold John the guns. All of them. He knows exactly what we have."

"Okay. Good. But he's not going to knock on the door and say, give me your guns or I'll kill John. He knows we've got rifles. He knows you can shoot." Liz looked out the window again, at the dark street. "So what's his plan? If he wants what's in here, the easiest way is if there's nobody in here to stop him. If we're all dead, he walks in and takes what he wants." She turned back. "He knows you'll go looking. He's counting on it. He's waiting for you to go out there with a light on and start knocking on doors. He's going to be sitting at his front window with that scope, waiting for you to walk up the street."

Sanda stared at her. It was exactly right. It was so exactly right that she felt sick.

"Heron," Sanda said suddenly. "He lives on Heron. John said, after the gun store. One street over. On Heron."

"Then we don't go up the street," Liz said. "We go behind the buildings. In the dark. No lights. We look in the back windows." She swung her rifle around the way Sanda had taught her. "Out the back."

* * *

They went out through the sliding door and the patio and the screen door and into the dark behind the building, and it was so dark that Sanda nearly walked face first into an oak tree. There was a narrow strip of grass and pine straw and trees between the backs of their building and the backs of the buildings on Heron, and they went along it bent over, slowly, feeling for each step with their feet.

The backs of the apartments on Heron were all alike, screened patios and sliding glass doors with the blinds drawn, all of them dark. They passed one, and a second. Sanda stopped at the third and held her hand out flat behind her, and Liz stopped.

The blinds on this one were open. And through the sliding door, in the faint moonlight coming in the front window, Sanda could see into the living room.

She crouched. Liz crouched behind her.

There was a man lying on the living room floor. He was on his side, with his hands behind his back. There was a strip of silver duct tape across his mouth, shining a little in the moonlight. Above it, she could see the shape of a mustache.

"I see Jim," Sanda whispered.

Liz made a sound, very small, and then made it stop.

Sanda inched forward on her knees across the dead leaves, trying with every movement not to make a sound, and the leaves crackled anyway, and she stopped, and waited, and went on. From here she could see straight through the living room and the dining area to the big window at the front of the apartment.

There was a man standing at the front window with his back to her.

She could see his dark shape against the faint gray of the window. He had a rifle up against his left shoulder, the long one with the big scope, and he was looking through the scope, out into the street, moving it slowly, left to right, right to left, the way a man waters a lawn.

Sanda backed up, inch by inch, until she was beside Liz again.

"Trevor's at the front window," Sanda whispered. "With his rifle. He's looking out at the street. His back's to us."

Liz nodded.

"We get down on our stomachs," Sanda whispered. "And crawl. Slow. The ground goes downhill away from the building — see? So if we lie down at the bottom we can rest the rifles on the ground and aim up, right through the sliding door at his back. These rounds will go through glass. But the glass might move the first one. So we both shoot at the same time. If we both shoot, one of them will hit."

She heard herself say it, and it was the strangest thing that had ever happened to her.

*I'm talking like John*, she thought. *I'm talking exactly like John.*

*I can do this.*

They lay down on their stomachs in the sand and the leaves and crawled, an inch at a time, on their elbows, with the rifles cradled across their forearms. It took more than a minute. It felt like an hour. Then they were in place, at the bottom of the little slope, side by side, looking up the dark hill at the sliding glass door, and through it, at the back of the man at the window.

They laid their rifles on the ground and pressed the stocks into their shoulders. Sanda turned on her optic, the up arrow, and the red dot came up in the little window. She put it on Trevor's back.

"Put the red dot right in the middle of his back," Sanda whispered.

"Okay," Liz whispered.

"We'll shoot on three."

"Okay."

"One," Sanda whispered. "Two — wait. Wait. Check your safety."

Liz looked down. Her safety was on. She let out her breath very slowly and pushed it to fire with her thumb.

"Ready," Liz whispered, and took a breath and held it.

"One," Sanda said. "Two. Three."

Both rifles went off at the same time. There was a bright flash out of each muzzle, and a sound like the sky splitting, and then — strangely muffled, as if it were happening in another room, behind the enormous ringing that filled both of their heads — the sound of glass breaking, a great sheet of it coming down, and shards of it skittering down the slope in the leaves toward their faces.

Trevor did not move.

Sanda's heart stopped. He was still standing there at the window. He had not turned around. He had not reacted at all.

Then, very slowly, his left arm dropped to his side. The rifle slid out from under his arm and fell to the floor. And then he went down after it.

"Turn your light on!" Sanda shouted at Liz, through the ringing, not knowing how loud she was. "Stand up! Keep your rifle on him!"

They got up. They went up the slope together with their rifles at their shoulders and their weapon lights on, two white beams, and through the empty frame of the sliding door, stepping over broken glass, into the apartment, like two soldiers in a film. Trevor lay face down on the floor below the front window. There were two dark spots on the back of his gray t-shirt, close together, on the words printed across his shoulders: RESTORE: POWER TO THE PEOPLE.

"Get his rifle!" Sanda shouted. "Throw it over here!"

Liz reached down, grabbed the rifle by the barrel, and threw it behind her across the floor, and put her own back on him. Sanda moved her red dot up from his back to the back of his head.

"I'm going to check his pulse," Sanda said. "If he moves at all — at *all* — shoot him again."

Liz nodded.

Sanda knelt down beside him. She held her rifle in her right hand by the grip, with the muzzle on him, and with her left hand she reached out and put two fingers against the side of his neck, under the beard, the way she had seen on television.

She could not feel anything. She did not know whether that was because there was nothing to feel, or because her own hand was shaking too hard to feel it.

*What the fuck am I doing?* Sanda thought, kneeling there with her fingers on the neck of a man she had shaken hands with at a range. *This is stupid.*

She stood up. She took one step back. She raised the rifle to her shoulder and put the red dot on the back of his head, and she pulled the trigger.

* * *

Liz was already across the room. She knelt beside Jim on the floor and got her fingernail under the edge of the duct tape and ripped it off in one pull.

"Ouch! God *dammit*!" Jim shouted. Half his mustache had come away with the tape. His upper lip was bleeding. "Liz! Jesus, Liz!"

"Hold still," Liz said, and kissed him on the forehead, hard, and started on his wrists.

"John!" Sanda was running through the apartment. Kitchen. Hall. Bedroom. Bathroom. The light on her rifle swinging across the walls. "*John!* Where are you! *John!*" She stopped in the middle of the hallway and screamed it. "I can't hear *shit* with this fucking ringing in my fucking ears!"

There was a door at the end of the kitchen. It was closed. She went to it, and put her foot against it beside the knob, and kicked it open.

John, tied to a chair in the middle of a dark garage, with his face broken and his mouth taped shut, looked up into a blinding white light, and beside the light, the black muzzle of an AR-15, pointed straight at his face.

But the silhouette behind the light was much shorter this time.

And John smiled.

* * *

Sanda cut him loose with his own knife. She found the Benchmade in his back pocket and pressed the button and the blade snapped out, and she sawed through the cord at his wrists and his ankles, and then, more carefully than she had ever done anything, she peeled the tape off his mouth.

"Hi," John said, through blood.

"Hi," Sanda said.

She stood up and swept her light around the garage, because John had taught her that, too. Bare concrete. A broom. A red hand truck. On Trevor's workbench, laid out in a row the way he had once laid guns out on glass for a customer, the Sig and Jim's shotgun and John's two Glocks; she handed John the pistols one at a time, and he put them back where they lived. And on the back wall, the narrow louvered closet door where the water heater would be. The door was open a few inches. There was light coming through the gap, faint and bluish. And there was a sound: a low, steady hum, so quiet you had to stop breathing to hear it.

"I thought that was in my ears," John said.

Sanda pulled the closet door open with the barrel of her rifle.

Inside, against the back wall beside the water heater, sat a small generator, red and square, about the size of a suitcase, running. A flexible metal hose ran from its exhaust out through a side door that had been propped open an inch, into the night. Beside the generator, on a steel rack, stood a row of heavy black batteries, a dozen of them, cabled together in a chain of red and black wires. A thick gray cable rose from the rack and disappeared through a hole cut in the ceiling, up toward the patio, toward the panels and the dish.

And on a shelf above the water heater, there was a laptop. It was open. Its screen was lit.

On the screen there was a seal — an eagle, in a circle — and under it, columns and rows of something that looked like a spreadsheet, colored in green and yellow and red. And across the top, in large clean letters, in a typeface John would have known anywhere, in the dark, with his eyes swollen shut, because he had fought for the little spur on the leg of its R for forty-five minutes in a conference room called the Everglades:

*Hello, John.*

He stood in the garage with his wife and looked at it.

His mother came and stood in the doorway behind them with her rifle, holding his father's arm. She looked at the screen for a long moment over her reading glasses.

"Don't touch it," Liz said.

They didn't. They left it there, humming in the dark, and went home.

* * *

They went back the way Sanda and Liz had come, behind the buildings, in the dark. John walked with one hand on Sanda's shoulder, because his right eye had swollen shut and he could not see the ground. Jim walked with Liz holding his arm, touching the bleeding place on his lip.

"My mom's pointing a rifle at the front door," Sanda whispered. "So we go in the back."

They came in through the sliding door. Granny was asleep on the couch under a throw blanket. Dorina sat in the dining chair facing the front door with the old twenty-two across her lap, and her chin was on her chest, and she was breathing slowly and deeply, with a little snore at the end of each breath.

Sanda turned toward the others and made a gesture with her hand, palm down, pressing — *quiet*, *quiet* — over and over.

"What?" Liz said, loudly, because her ears were still ringing.

Dorina came awake all at once with a jerk, and the rifle went off.

The little crack was nothing, after what their ears had been through. A small black hole appeared in the drywall a foot to the left of the front door, at about the height of a man's chest. Dorina leapt up out of the chair, and the rifle clattered to the floor at her feet, and she stood there in her flowered housecoat with both hands over her mouth.

"I'm sorry," Dorina said, turning to them. "I fall asleep."

Granny sat straight up on the couch. She looked at all of them, standing there in the plastic candlelight, and her eyes went to John.

"What happened to your face?" Granny asked.

"Are you okay, Mommy?" Sanda was already beside her mother, turning her by the shoulders, checking her for holes. "Didn't you hear the shots? Three of them, right around the corner?"

"I fall asleep," Dorina said again. "I'm sorry."

John's nose was still bleeding. The right side of his face was red and purple and swollen, and the eye on that side was a slit. Jim had a knot on the back of his head the size of an egg and a raw red patch on his upper lip where half his mustache used to be.

"We're fine," John said, quietly, in a voice so low it was almost his father's. "We had an incident with Trevor. Everything's okay now. We're all very tired. We'll tell you tomorrow."

He found Charlie under the bed in the guest room, shaking. The pug was under the bed with him, curled against his side, sound asleep and snoring.

John went into the master bedroom and laid the Sig on the floor and lay down on the bed on his side and was asleep before he finished lying down. Jim leaned the shotgun against the nightstand and climbed into the other side of the bed and was asleep too.

Sanda came to the doorway to close the door, and stood there looking at them.

"They didn't even take their shoes off," Sanda said.

"I go to bed now," Dorina said, very small. "Good night."

"Me too," Granny said quickly, and they went into the guest room together, and closed the door.

Sanda looked at Liz.

"I'll stay up," Sanda said. "I can't sleep anyway."

"I'll stay up with you," Liz said.

They sat on the floor of the living room with their backs against the wall and their rifles across their knees, facing the front door and the little hole in the drywall beside it.

"I can't believe we did that," Sanda said after a while. "It feels like a dream. I don't think I've — I don't know what I've done yet."

"The important thing," Liz said, "is that they're alive."

Sanda nodded. She did not say anything about the third shot. Liz did not ask. Sanda sat with her rifle across her knees and thought about what it would have been like if she had walked into that garage and John had not been in that chair. She closed her eyes. And for no reason she could ever explain afterward, what came was the kitchen, three weeks ago, the steam and the pot of chestnuts, and John at the counter with his fingers burned brown, squeezing them out into the bowl one after another, saying that hell was a Home Goods where you squeezed chestnut goo for all eternity, and she had laughed until she had to sit down on the kitchen floor.

"You okay?" Liz asked softly. "You want to sleep a little? I'll take it."

Sanda nodded without opening her eyes. She felt herself going.

* * *

She did not know how long she slept. She opened her eyes because something was different.

The living room was dark. The plastic candles had gone out; their batteries had finally died. Liz was beside her, still sitting up, still awake, her rifle across her knees, but she was not looking at the front door. She was looking at the front window, at the narrow gap between two slats of the blinds.

"Sanda," Liz whispered.

Sanda got up on her knees and looked.

Through the gap in the blinds, across the street and down, between two of the buildings on the far side, you could see a slice of the next street over. Of Heron. A slice of the front of an apartment there.

There was a light in the window. It was not a flashlight. It was not a candle. It was the steady, cold, blue-white glow of a screen, filling a whole room, coming out through a front window that had no glass left in it.

It was Trevor's window.

Trevor was dead. Sanda knew that better than anyone alive. She had made sure of it.
