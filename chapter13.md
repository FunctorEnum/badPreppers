# Chapter Thirteen

## The Water

> *"Do not charge anything. Do not run generators. Do not run solar panels. Do not connect anything to anything."*
>
> — the President, AM radio, Tuesday, 5:41 a.m.

Jim reached into his back pocket and took out his phone and looked at it, and then he held it up so they could all see, like a man showing his ticket at a gate.

"Low battery," Jim said.

The screen glowed in the dark kitchen, brighter than anything else in the room. In a friendly blue box it said: *Jim, your battery is at 20%. Charge now to stay connected with the people who matter.*

"See?" Jim said. "It's just the battery."

"Give me that," John said.

"It's my phone."

"Dad. Give me the phone." John held out his hand. "Everybody. Every phone. Right now."

They gave him their phones. Sanda's from her robe pocket. Liz's from the bottom of her canvas tote. Dorina's, which had giant numbers on its screen because she could not read small ones, from the pocket of her housecoat. His own. Granny went next door in her slippers, with the reprogrammed flashlight, and came back with hers, which she kept in a drawer and used about twice a year. Jim's came last. He held onto it for a moment before he let go.

John opened the door of the microwave and put all six phones inside, stacked like pancakes, and closed it.

"What are you doing?" Liz asked.

"A microwave's a metal box," John said. "It's built to keep the microwaves in. That means it keeps signals out, too. Or in. It's a Faraday cage." He looked at the dark door. "Nothing gets in or out of there."

"Well," Granny said, from her stool, "I can't use it anyway."

"In case the kids call," Jim said, looking at the microwave the way Charlie looked at the door when Sanda left.

"Jim," Liz said. "The kids are right here."

"Well," Jim said. "In case."

* * *

They made coffee with real water.

Sanda knocked yesterday's grounds out of the French press into the trash and did not wash it, because there was nothing to wash it with, and filled the kettle from the milk jug, and John lit the Korean grill. The blue ring came up under the iron dome. Outside it was thirty-eight degrees. The front had come through overnight, the way Trevor said it would, and the inside of the apartment was cold enough that everyone stood close to the little flame with their hands out while the kettle heated, as if it were a campfire.

"They're killing people," Jim said, looking at the flame. "You understand that? Up north. It's January. Chicago. Detroit. New York. No heat. Old people in apartments. He said it right on the radio — some of you won't survive. He knew. He did it anyway."

"He did it," John said, "to kill the thing that would have killed everybody."

"You don't know that."

"I know what it could do. I saw it. On paper, on Friday. It could turn the lights on and off in two hundred and forty utilities, and send the trucks to the wrong place, and tell everybody it was fixed. That's just my company. That's just the one I saw." John's voice was rising. "It was in everything, Dad. The banks. The pipelines. The phones. If they hadn't turned it off—"

"They didn't turn *it* off. They turned *us* off."

"It's the same thing!"

"It is *not* the same thing," Jim said, and the low-and-slow voice was gone, and his face was red. "I've got friends up there. I've got — your mother's cousin is in Ohio, she's eighty-one—"

"Both of you," Sanda said, pressing down the plunger, "can shut up and help with the shit bags."

Nobody said anything. Sanda poured the coffee into six cups. Liz picked one up and handed it to Jim, and Jim took it, and his face slowly went from red back to its normal color.

"It's better than yesterday," Dorina said, sipping. "Not so much bubbles."

* * *

"Okay," John said, when the coffee was gone. "Here's the plan."

He was standing in the middle of the living room with his hands behind his back, the way he imagined a commander would stand. He had been awake since his parents came through the door before dawn. He had not shaved in two days, and his jaw was dark with stubble, and his gray t-shirt had a coffee stain on it, and he had not showered, and he was keenly aware of all of it.

"We stay here. We hunker down. We keep a low profile. Actually — forget low. We need to be invisible. Nobody outside can think we have anything worth taking." He looked at his father. "Nobody can argue anymore that this isn't serious. And before it's too late, we need to get as much water out of that RV as we can. Quietly. Before somebody figures out the Publix is full of water and food."

Nobody argued. He felt the room listening to him, and he liked it, and he was a little ashamed of how much he liked it.

"Sanda, can you find me everything we've got that holds water? Big things. Not glasses."

Sanda went into the kitchen and began opening cabinets, and Liz went with her, and in a minute they had set out, beside the nine full pitchers along the counter, four more empty pitchers, a punch bowl, and a large stainless steel mixing bowl.

"Jesus Christ," John said, looking at the counter. "How many pitchers do we have?"

"Well," Sanda said brightly, "you just never know!"

"Actually," Sanda said a moment later, holding up a box from the drawer by the refrigerator. "We have Ziplocs. The gallon ones. Wouldn't it be easier to fill these and put them in the cooler bags and just carry those?"

John looked at the Ziplocs. He looked at the pitchers. "Yeah," he said. "It would."

"Dad," John said, "you used to carry a forty-caliber Glock on the force, right?"

"Sure did," Jim said proudly. "Twenty-two years."

"Perfect." John went to the bedroom closet and came back with a Glock 29, a fat little subcompact that fired the ten-millimeter round. He had a spare barrel for it that let it shoot forty-caliber instead, which was cheaper and kicked less and which his father already knew. He sat on the edge of the bed and swapped the barrels and loaded three magazines with forty-caliber hollow points, and he came back and showed his father how to clip the plastic paddle holster inside the waistband of his jeans on the right side, the way he had worn his duty gun.

Jim struggled with the paddle. It took him three tries to get it over his belt. When he got it, he slid the Glock into it and it locked with a click, and he stood up straighter, the way men do.

"To draw it," John said, "you just grip it normal, and push this little lever with your trigger finger, and pull. And when it comes out, see where your finger is? Along the side. Not on the trigger."

"I know where my finger goes, son."

"I know you do, Dad. Put your jacket back on over it." John handed him the two spare magazines. "Inside pocket."

"Sanda," John said. "Can you take Charlie out while we go?"

"Sure."

"Do *not* go outside without the Bodyguard. Okay?"

"Yeah, yeah," Sanda said, which meant she would, mostly to make him stop saying it.

* * *

It was a cold, clear, quiet morning, and he and his father walked out through the gap in the hedge with a cooler bag each, three empty Ziplocs folded inside. The road was empty. As they came into the strip-mall lot, John saw that the two county cruisers from the night before were gone. In their place, between the dark Publix and the motor home, a Lake Mary police car was parked at an angle, and two officers were leaning against it.

They saw John and his father, and they straightened up, and they came at them fast.

"Gentlemen! Stop right there!"

The one in front was a heavyset sergeant in his fifties with a shaved head that had gone pink in the sun and a thick gray mustache. The one behind him was a young woman with her blond hair pulled back so tight in a bun it looked painted on. Both of them had their right hands on the grips of their pistols.

"Drop the bags, please!" the sergeant said.

They dropped the bags. They were nearly empty and fell flat to the asphalt with no sound.

"It's okay, officer," Jim said, in a friendly voice. "I'm a retired Jacksonville deputy." And his right hand went toward his back pocket for his wallet.

Both officers drew. The two pistols came up level with John's chest and his father's.

"Hands! Hands where I can see them! Now!"

John and his father put their hands up as high as they would go.

"Officer," Jim said, very calmly, in the low voice. "My wallet's in my back right pocket. My badge is in it. You go ahead and take it out."

The sergeant holstered and came forward while the young officer kept her gun on them. He fished Jim's wallet out and flipped it open with his thumb, and there was the badge, gold, and the concealed weapons license next to it.

"You carrying?"

"This is my son, John," Jim said. "We both have licenses. We're both carrying."

The sergeant stepped over to John. "Where?"

"Glock 26, left hip," John said, keeping his hands high. "Glock 43, right front pocket. A knife in my back right pocket. My wallet's in my back left with my license."

The sergeant took his wallet too, and walked back to the cruiser with both of them. John watched him get in. The man sat there a long time, poking at the laptop mounted beside the steering wheel, and then picking up his radio, and then putting it down. Then he got back out and walked over to them, looking at Jim's badge in his hand.

"Computer's down," the sergeant said. "Everything's down. I got a radio that talks to her car and that's about it." He looked up at Jim. "Jacksonville?"

"Thirty-five years."

"You know a Benny Ruiz? Zone Four?"

"Benny!" Jim said. "Sure. He was in my academy class. He still got that boat?"

"Sank it." The sergeant almost smiled. "Put your hands down." He handed back the wallets. "What are y'all doing out here? We're under martial law as of this morning. Nobody's supposed to be outside."

"That's my motor home," Jim said, nodding at the Winnebago. "My family's at my son's place, in the Preserve. We're running out of water. I've got seventy gallons in that tank. We just need six."

The sergeant looked at the motor home, and at Jim, and at the two empty cooler bags on the asphalt.

"Get your water," the sergeant said. "Then go straight back. And I can't promise you that vehicle's going to be here tomorrow. We had calls all night."

They filled six Ziplocs at the galley sink and zipped them shut and set them in the cooler bags. Three gallons was heavier than John expected, twenty-five pounds hanging off one shoulder. Jim was breathing hard before they were out of the lot. When they came through the hedge, Sanda was walking toward them across the street with her jacket hanging low on the right side.

"Why are you outside?" John asked. "What's going on?"

"Just taking out another bag of somebody else's shit," Sanda said. "I guess since I'm the only one around here carrying a gun, that puts me on shit detail."

John laughed. Sanda laughed. Jim laughed, out of breath. Then from the direction of the Publix came the short yelp of a police siren, and an amplified voice shouting something over a loudspeaker that none of them could make out. They stopped walking. The voice shouted again.

A single gunshot.

Sanda jumped. "Shit!"

Then the rest of it — a long ragged barrage, pistols, and something heavier, a lot of something heavier, cracking and echoing back off the buildings. Sanda was digging in her pocket for her keys, fumbling them, and got the door open, and Jim went in with his cooler bag, and John stood in the yard with his own bag at his feet, listening, his head tilted, picking out the different calibers by their sounds the way a birdwatcher picks out songs.

"Get your ass in here!" Sanda yelled.

He came inside. Sanda slammed the door and threw the deadbolt.

"I think it's okay," John said. "Probably looters going after the Publix."

"What took you so long?" Sanda demanded, with her arms folded.

"We had a nice chat with a couple of police officers."

"And?"

"And they said we're under martial law," John said. "Nobody's supposed to be outside."

"So I can't take out the shit bags?"

"No, it's fine. We're in a gated community. There aren't any police in here. The last thing we need is to start storing bags of shit in the apartment."

Sanda shook her head and went into the guest bathroom and washed her hands with the last of the Chablis. Then she went to the wine cooler and stood in front of it for a while and took out a nineteen-dollar white Bordeaux and brought it to him.

"Open this," Sanda said.

He twisted in the corkscrew and thought about the gunfight. He wondered who had won it. Either there were two dead police officers lying in front of the Publix right now and people were breaking in the windows, or there were two or three dead men lying in front of the Publix and the officers were reloading. He would not know which until somebody went and looked. *Until then*, John thought, popping the cork, *both things are true. Heisenberg.* He did not know if that was the right physicist. He did not have the internet to check.

He gave the bottle back to Sanda. She carried it into the bathroom.

"At least our hands smell nice," Sanda said.

* * *

"I have a few things to say," John announced, after the water was poured into the pitchers. "If everybody could listen for a second."

He waited, with his hands behind his back, until everyone was looking at him.

"Nobody leaves the apartment except to take Charlie out and to take the bags to the dumpster. Somebody is awake and on guard, inside, at all times. We take shifts. The pistols are fine, but they're small, and they're not enough. Sanda, you're good on the AR. You carry it inside. All the time." He swung around the short black rifle he had slung over his shoulder. "This is a Sig P556. It's basically an AR without a stock. I'll carry this."

"Dad." He handed his father the shotgun. "You know a pump shotgun."

"Used one every day on the job."

"There's a safety button right here, by the trigger. You push it through. When you can see a red ring around it, it's off safe and ready to fire. Red means dead." He looked around the room. "That goes for everybody, on everything. You see red, it's ready to shoot. There's a light on the end of the tube. Push the paddle and it stays on as long as you push. Flip it the other way and it clicks on and stays on."

Jim clicked the light on and off and on and off, delighted, until Liz said "Jim."

"Sanda." John looked at his wife.

"Yes, *sir*," Sanda said, saluting, sarcastically, though he could tell she was pleased to be asked.

"I need you to take the other AR and teach my mom. She learns fast. Take the magazine out and check the chamber first. There are dummy rounds in the desk drawer, orange plastic ones. Snap caps. Use those. Show her how to load a magazine, how to chamber a round, how to clear a jam, how to turn on the optic. Everything I showed you."

"Got it," Sanda said.

"That makes four of us. Two hours each, all night. Everybody gets six hours of sleep." He nodded at Granny and Dorina. "Can you two pour the rest of that water out of the bags into the pitchers? Before they leak."

The apartment went to work. Granny and Dorina stood side by side at the counter, unzipping Ziplocs and pouring them into bowls, and Granny held the bags and Dorina poured, and neither of them understood a word the other said, and neither needed to. In the corner, Sanda sat on the couch with Liz and the second AR across their knees, and John heard his wife's voice saying, patiently, *No — thumb on the back of the round, push down, slide it under the lip*, which was exactly what Trevor had told her at the range.

John took the ribeye out of the refrigerator, which was now only a cabinet with a light that didn't come on. The steak was still cool. He had a set of very sharp Japanese knives that had been one of his phases, and he took out the biggest one and began cutting the steak into thin horizontal slices for the grill.

He was halfway through the third slice when the sirens came — a whole chorus of them, from the direction of the Publix, and tires screaming — and then a crackling roar of gunfire like a string of firecrackers on the Fourth of July that did not stop.

The knife jumped in his hand. He looked down. There was a small cut on the edge of his left hand, too small to bleed.

"Everybody get down!" John shouted, and grabbed the Sig.

They all got down. Granny got down off her stool very slowly, holding the counter, and Dorina pulled her the rest of the way, and they sat together on the kitchen floor. Liz and Sanda went flat behind the couch with the rifles. Jim knelt by the front window with the shotgun. Charlie went into the closet. And for the first time, any of them, it felt like what it was. It felt like a war. All of the humor that had been in the apartment — the wine for hands, the shit detail, the plastic candles — went out of it all at once, like air out of a tire.

It went on for five minutes. Then it stopped.

"Nobody make a sound," John whispered. "Stay down."

They waited. The silence was enormous.

Then, through the closed windows, from the direction of the Publix, came a cheer. A big one, many voices, ragged and wild. And then the cheer turned into a chant, three words, over and over, louder and louder, the same three words. John could not make them out. He tried. He never would.

But he knew one thing. The police had lost. And by sundown there would be nothing left in the Publix, or in any store in that strip mall, or in any store for miles.

* * *

At dusk, John finished slicing the steak and seared it on the Korean grill, a few slices at a time, ten seconds on the dome and flip and ten more. The smell filled the apartment, and everyone, who had eaten nothing all day but fruit, came into the kitchen without being asked.

Sanda set the table for six.

She did not just put out plates. She put out the white china, and a linen napkin folded at every place, and a crystal water glass, and the little silver knife rests she had bought in Paris so that no guest would ever have to lay a used knife on the tablecloth. She filled every water glass from a pitcher. John spooned the spicy barbecue sauce they had ordered by the case from a smokehouse in Chicago onto five of the plates. On the sixth, he squeezed out a puddle of ketchup.

"I guess this is where I'm sittin'," Granny said, pointing at the ketchup.

They sat. Sanda's AR leaned against the wall behind her chair. Liz's leaned against the wall behind hers. The shotgun stood in the corner. John's Sig hung from the back of his chair by its sling. If it had not been for the rifles, it would have looked like a hundred dinners Sanda had hosted.

"It's a little ironic," John said, chewing. He swallowed. "Here we are, having this nice dinner, drinking water, and there's a perfectly good bottle of French wine in the bathroom."

Sanda smiled. Jim and Liz laughed. Dorina smiled and did not laugh, because she had not understood. Granny kept her eyes on her plate and cut her steak into thin strips. She ate one strip, and a slice of apple, and set down her fork.

Nobody drank. They all knew why, without discussing it. There were six of them, and two of them had never fired the guns leaning against the walls, and nobody could afford to be anything but awake.

After dinner, Sanda wiped the six plates with paper towels. She could not wash them. She stood at the counter wiping and wiping a plate that was already as clean as it was going to get. She owned, in the china cabinet behind her, a different set of wine glasses for nearly every grape there was — glasses for Pinot Noir and glasses for Cabernet and glasses for Riesling and snifters and rum glasses and little cordial glasses that John had never once seen her use — and she had spent years choosing them, and she stood there now wiping a dirty plate with a dry paper towel, with an AR-15 on her back, and John watched her face and saw that she hated the world she was standing in. She had never wanted a gun. She dreamed of a world with no guns in it. She had learned to shoot because he had asked her to, and now here she was, and the rifle on her back was the heaviest thing she had ever carried.

* * *

That night it was forty-four degrees inside the apartment.

The sirens had stopped. That was the thing John noticed, standing at the window at nine o'clock. For two days there had been sirens in the background of everything, constantly, the way there was traffic. Now there were none. Somewhere a dog was barking. That was all.

He and his father carried the short couch from the living room into the master bedroom so that the four people taking guard shifts could sleep in one place and not wake the others. The big cushion slid off halfway down the hall and Sanda caught it. They had to stand the couch on its end to get it through the bedroom door. Sanda made it up with a sheet and two throw blankets and a pillow, and it looked like a small bed for a child.

"We could burn one of the trees," John said, rubbing his arms. He was wearing his Bears hoodie over two t-shirts and he was still cold. "For heat. Out on the patio. We've got seven."

Sanda looked at him for a long time.

"It's *plastic*, John," Sanda said.

Then there were the flashlights. John had six of them, all identical, all small and black and incredibly bright, and they all worked the same way: one click for high, two quick clicks for strobe, three quick clicks for low. Nobody in his family but John could do three quick clicks. His father did two and lit up the bedroom in a blinding white stutter that made Granny cry out and cover her eyes. His mother did four and went back to high. Dorina did one click, very firmly, and then shone it directly into John's face to ask him if it was right. Sanda did three, perfectly, and then dropped it.

He had reprogrammed the one he gave Granny, years ago, so that it only had low. He had done it by following a sequence of clicks and holds listed in the manual, on the manufacturer's website. He had thrown the paper manuals away when he bought them. Why keep paper, when everything was online?

He stood in the dark bedroom while his father's flashlight strobed at the ceiling, and he started to laugh, quietly, and could not stop. He had bought six flashlights for exactly this night, and he could not set them, because he had thrown away the instructions, because he had the internet.

He thought, for some reason, of the first time he had ever used the internet. He had been twelve, at his parents' house, with a dial-up modem that screamed like a cat, and he had sat down in front of a browser and realized that he could not think of a single address to type. He had gone to the kitchen and found a box of Frosted Flakes and turned it around until he found a web address printed on the back. He had typed it in, letter by letter. And then he had watched the screen fill, one blue stripe at a time, top to bottom, slowly, until after several minutes there was a picture of Tony the Tiger, grinning at him from a dark blue background. That was his first internet. Tony the Tiger, delivered one stripe at a time. And now he was standing in the dark at the end of it.

* * *

He took the first shift, ten to midnight, in the recliner with the Sig across his knees and a battery kitchen timer set for two hours on the arm of the chair.

He thought about Matt. He could not stop thinking about Matt. Matt, in a twelve-hundred-square-foot condo on the fourth floor of a warehouse in Pilsen, with electric baseboard heat. Matt with his AR-15 and his Mosin and his four hundred rounds and his can of Dinty Moore. Matt had done everything John had done. He had done it less, and he had laughed about it more, but he had done it, and he lived in a city where tonight, according to the radio, it was going to be nineteen degrees. John sat in his recliner in his hoodie and thought about nineteen degrees in a warehouse with no heat, and a gun that could not make it one degree warmer, and he did not laugh.

At midnight the timer went off, loud as a smoke alarm in the quiet, and John jerked and swung the Sig toward it before he knew what it was.

He went to the bedroom to wake his father, and his father was snoring so loudly he could hear it through the door. When he opened the door, Sanda was sitting up on the couch, wide awake, glaring at him, with one finger over her lips. She got up and pushed him back out into the living room and closed the bedroom door behind her.

"I can't sleep," Sanda whispered. "I'm wide awake. I'll take his shift. There's no point waking him."

"Why can't you sleep?" John asked, and knew instantly how stupid it was.

"Why can't I sleep?" Sanda said. "Let's see. I'm lying on my living room couch, which is in my bedroom, right next to your parents. I thought *you* snored until I heard your father. We all stink. I have been carrying bags of other people's shit to a dumpster for two days. Around the corner there was a *fucking gun battle* and there are probably people lying dead in front of the grocery store where we used to buy *Sancerre*." She took a breath. "And that kitchen timer is loud as shit. Just so you know."

He waited to see whether she was done. He opened his mouth.

"And you drained the tub," Sanda said.

He closed his mouth.

They stood there in the dark living room in the faint orange flicker of the plastic candles. Then she sighed, and sat down on the floor where the couch used to be, and he sat beside her, and she put her head on his shoulder.

"Lie down out here," John said finally. "Try to sleep. I'll stay up."

She lay down on the rug with a throw pillow, and in a few minutes her breathing changed, and she was asleep. At one o'clock his father came out of the bedroom in his socks, yawning, with the shotgun.

"Thought I was on at midnight," Jim whispered.

"You were asleep."

"Well, I'm up now." Jim lowered himself into the recliner. "Go on, get some sleep. I used to work midnights all the time."

*That was a long time ago*, John thought. He went into the bedroom and lay down on top of the covers in his clothes, beside his mother, and turned on his side to the wall, and felt like he was six years old with the flu.

* * *

He woke to his father's hand on his shoulder and a blue light in his face.

"John. John. Wake up. Look at this."

It was three in the morning. Jim was crouched beside the bed with his phone in his hand, and the screen was lit, glowing in the dark, the brightest thing John had seen in two days.

"Dad," John whispered. "That was in the microwave."

"I took it out," Jim whispered back. "In case the kids called."

"Dad, *we're* the—" John sat up. "What is it?"

Jim turned the phone toward him. At the top of the screen, beside the little fan-shaped Wi-Fi symbol, it said *Connected*. Below that, the name of the network.

*FEMA_Relief_WiFi*

There was a map open on the screen, and in the middle of the map was a single red pin, and beside the pin, in a little white box: *Water available. 2.1 miles.*

"There's a FEMA station!" Jim whispered, thrilled. "Two miles! They've got water! We can go first thing—"

"Dad," John said. "The government turned off the internet."

"Right."

"They turned off the power. They turned off the phones. They told us on the radio not to connect anything to anything." John looked at the glowing screen. "So who do you think is running the Wi-Fi?"

Jim looked at the phone for a long time. His face moved in the blue light.

"FEMA?" Jim said.

John took the phone out of his hand and looked at the map. He zoomed in on the red pin. It was not two miles away. The map's walking route went all the way around by the main road, but the pin itself was just across the street from the complex, in the middle of the strip-mall parking lot, beside the Publix.

It was sitting exactly on top of the motor home.

He got up and walked to the kitchen and put the phone back in the microwave with the others and closed the door firmly. Then he went back to bed. He did not sleep for a long time.

* * *

Jim sat in the recliner with the shotgun across his knees and looked at the microwave.

It was four in the morning. Everyone was asleep. Sanda was asleep on the rug at his feet with her rifle beside her. The plastic candles flickered on the table. He was seventy years old and he had sat up through more midnights as a police officer than he'd care to remember, and he was not afraid of the dark, and he was not afraid of looters, and he was not afraid of a robot from Thanksgiving. He was afraid of his son's face when John said *Who do you think is running the Wi-Fi*.

He got up, quietly. He opened the microwave, quietly. He took out his phone, because it was his phone, and because you never knew, and because a man ought to be able to be reached.

The screen lit up in his hand. There was a new notification on it, in the same friendly blue box.

*Household check-in. 6 occupants, 1 canine at this address. Is everyone safe? Tap to confirm.*

Jim looked at it for a while. It seemed to him a decent thing to ask. Somebody, somewhere, was keeping track. Somebody was checking.

He was a polite man. He had been raised to answer when he was spoken to.

He tapped *Confirm*.
