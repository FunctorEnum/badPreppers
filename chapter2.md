# Chapter Two

## The Funeral

> *FAA: OVERNIGHT SCHEDULING "ANOMALY" REROUTED ONE-FIFTH OF U.S. FLIGHTS, CUT DELAYS 30%. "NO ONE ORDERED IT," AGENCY SAYS. AIRLINES SAY THEY WILL KEEP IT.*
>
> — phone notification, Saturday

The range sat at the end of a shell road in the pine flatwoods east of Sanford, behind a locked gate and a hand-painted sign that said MEMBERS ONLY, and on Saturday mornings before eight it belonged to John.

He liked it best in the fog. The fog came up off a retention pond on the north side of the property and lay along the ground between the firing line and the berm, and when the sun came through the pines it lit the fog from inside, so that the paper targets on their wooden frames at twenty-five yards seemed to hang in a bright gray room with no walls. A pair of sandhill cranes worked the far edge of the pond, stepping carefully, the way old men cross a parking lot. Somewhere behind the berm a woodpecker was knocking on a dead tree. Nobody else was there.

John signed in at the shed, paid for two targets with exact change in the honor box, and carried his rifle case and range bag out to the covered firing line. He wore what he always wore to shoot: the olive cargo shorts, a black t-shirt, a faded Cubs cap pulled low, wraparound sunglasses that doubled as eye protection, and foam earplugs pushed in so deep they made his own heartbeat sound like somebody walking on a wooden floor upstairs. He laid the AR-15 on the bench with its muzzle downrange, set out four magazines in a neat row, and stapled a fresh target over an old one.

His first magazine was terrible.

He knew it would be. He had spent the week reading code he did not need to read and approving code he had not read, and he had not slept well since Monday, and the rounds went where his mind went, which was everywhere. Three high. Two left. One that he could not find on the paper at all and suspected was somewhere in the berm, or in Georgia. He dropped the empty magazine onto the bench and sat down on the stool, and he did not reload, and he looked out at the fog.

This was what the range was for. Sanda thought it was for shooting. It was for the spaces between.

* * *

His father had hated guns.

That was the first thing anybody needed to know about John's history with firearms, and it surprised people, because his father had carried one for thirty-five years. Jim Stevens had been a deputy with the Jacksonville Sheriff's Office from before John was born until well after John left home, and he had worn a gun on his hip every working day of his life, and he had hated it the way some men hate neckties. He hated the noise. He hated the cleaning. He hated the smell of the solvent, which got into his hands and stayed there through dinner. Once a year the department required him to go to the range and requalify, and every year he passed by a single point and came home and sat in his chair and said, "Well, that's done," like a man who has had a tooth pulled.

John, at fourteen, had not understood this.

"Dad," he had said one evening at the kitchen table, his math homework open in front of him and untouched, "when are we going to the range?"

His father had just come in from second shift. He was forty-two then, the same age John was now, a big man in a tan uniform shirt with a dark mustache that had not yet gone gray, and he was standing at the open refrigerator in his socks drinking milk straight out of the carton, which John's mother had forbidden in writing.

"Soon, buddy," Jim said, wiping his mustache with the back of his wrist. "Real soon."

"You said that at Christmas."

"And it's still true," Jim said. "It's just a longer soon."

"Jim," John's mother said from the sink, without turning around.

"What? I'm going to take him." Jim put the milk back and closed the refrigerator with his hip. "I'm going to take him, Liz. I just have to find a Saturday when I don't have to go to the range."

John had not understood that sentence for years. He understood it now, sitting on a stool at a range with the fog lifting. His father had not wanted to go to the range on his day off because the range was where he went when he had to, and a man who has to carry a gun for a living does not necessarily want to carry one for fun. Jim Stevens would not kill a spider. He carried them outside in a coffee cup and set them in the azaleas and told them to have a nice life. He would not fish. He would eat a steak, but he did not want to hear one word about where it came from. He had gone into law enforcement, as far as John could tell, because he liked people, and he had stayed in it because he was good at talking to them, and in thirty-five years he had drawn his weapon on a human being exactly four times and fired it never.

He never did take John to the range. Not once.

John picked up the second magazine, seated it, chambered a round, and fired five slow shots into the center of the target. They were better. He sat back down.

* * *

The first time John fired a real gun he was twenty-three years old, and it was at his grandfather's funeral, more or less.

Hoyt Stevens died in April, the spring after John and Sanda were married. They had been living in Minneapolis then, in a one-bedroom over a Vietnamese restaurant on Nicollet Avenue, and they drove down to Jacksonville in two long days in the Honda with a bag of gas-station sandwiches between the seats. The funeral was at a funeral home on Atlantic Boulevard that smelled — Sanda said — of carnations and floor wax. Hoyt lay in an open casket in a navy suit he had worn to every wedding and funeral for forty years, and he looked the way he had looked in life, only more still: lean, long in the jaw, with a white crew cut and big long-fingered hands folded on his chest. John had loved him more than anyone in the world except Sanda, and he stood beside the casket and could not think of a single thing to say to him, which had never once happened while Hoyt was alive.

His grandfather could explain anything. That was what John remembered first, always. When John was eight, Hoyt had explained how a carburetor worked using a soda straw and a glass of sweet tea. When John was fourteen, Hoyt had explained the stock market using a jar of pennies and a Monopoly board, and he had explained compound interest using a pear tree. He had explained death, too, once, on a porch swing after John's dog was hit by a car, and John had understood it, and had felt better, and had never been able to reconstruct afterward exactly what his grandfather had said. "How a thing works," Hoyt liked to say, tapping the side of his head, "is the most important thing you can know about it. Anybody can use a thing. A monkey can use a thing."

Afterward, in the parking lot, his father had taken him aside.

Jim was fifty-one then, his mustache going gray, his uniform traded for a dark suit that pulled across the shoulders. He walked John out to the Honda with one hand on the back of his neck, the way he used to walk him into the dentist, and when they got to the car he took a bundle out from under his arm. It was wrapped in a yellow dish towel.

"Your granddaddy didn't leave a whole lot," Jim said quietly, looking off across the parking lot at nothing. "Some tools. The land up in Alabama. Your Granny's getting all that. But I've got something I want you to have, and I don't want it in my house anymore."

He unfolded the dish towel. Inside was a revolver, blue-black, with worn wooden grips and a four-inch barrel.

"Smith and Wesson .38 Special," Jim said. "I carried it eleven years, before the department went to Glocks. Never fired it except at the range." He folded the towel back over it. "Your granddaddy gave me a pocketknife when I made deputy. I figure it's my turn to give you something, and this is what I've got that's worth anything. You keep it locked up. You hear me?"

"I hear you," John said, taking the bundle. It was heavier than he expected. Everything about that day was heavier than he expected.

"And go by the old place on your way home," said a voice behind them.

It was Granny. She was seventy-one that year and already small, though not as small as she would get, and her white hair was set in soft curls the way she had it set every Friday of her adult life. She wore a black dress with a cardinal brooch at the collar, and she had come up behind them so quietly in her church shoes that neither of them had heard her.

"Mama," Jim said, turning, "it's not on the way."

"It's on the way if he goes that way," Granny said, looking at John through her big glasses. "Your granddaddy was born on that land. So was I, near enough. Go stand on it a minute. Hoyt'd like that."

* * *

So they went home the long way, which was the wrong way, west across the Florida Panhandle and north into south Alabama on two-lane roads with no shoulders. Sanda navigated with a paper map from a gas station, because their phones did not yet know how to get to a place like this, and Granny had written the last part of the directions on the back of a church bulletin in her careful, looping hand. *Left at the Primitive Baptist church. 3 mi. Right on the dirt road just past the big water oak. Go till it stops.*

The dirt road was red. John had not known dirt came in red. It ran a mile through planted pines so straight and so evenly spaced that they made the eye stutter, and then the planted pines gave way to old ones, longleaf, tall and crooked and far apart, with the ground beneath them carpeted in needles the color of a penny. The Honda bottomed out twice. Sanda held onto the handle above her door and said nothing, which was how John knew she was nervous.

The road ended at an embankment. It rose up in front of the car about six feet high, a wall of red clay streaked with off-white sand, and the road simply stopped at the foot of it as though whoever built the road had run into the hill and decided that was far enough. John turned off the engine. The silence came in through the windows like water.

"This is it?" Sanda asked, looking around. She was twenty-two, in jeans and a Twins sweatshirt she had bought to fit in, and her hair was pulled back in a ponytail, and her glasses then were small and wire-rimmed and made her look like a graduate student.

"This is where he was born," John said.

"There's nothing here."

"There's a house," John said, and pointed.

Off to the left, back in the pines, there was a roof. It was tin, rusted the color of the road, and it was only a roof and part of a chimney until you looked longer, and then you could see the gray boards of a house under it, and the sag of a porch, and the dark squares where windows had been. In the clearing in front of it stood a thing on a short iron pipe, waist-high, with a curved handle sticking out of one side and a spout sticking out of the other, all of it rusted solid.

"What's that?" Sanda asked, pointing at it.

"A pump," John said, squinting at it. "For water. There's a well under it. You work the handle up and down and the water comes out of the spout."

"Does it work?"

John looked at it for a long moment, the way he would later look at things he did not understand: as if understanding might arrive on its own if he waited.

"I doubt it," John said, and forgot about it for nineteen years.

He opened the glove compartment. The revolver was in there, still in the dish towel, and beside it a box of cartridges his father had pressed into his hand at the last minute in the parking lot, saying, "Might as well." John unwrapped the gun and opened the cylinder the way he had seen it done on television, and fed in a single cartridge, and closed it.

"John," Sanda said, sitting up very straight. "What are you doing?"

"Just one," John said. He got out of the car.

The air smelled — Sanda told him later — of pine resin and wet clay and something sweet he would never be able to identify for her. He walked to the foot of the embankment, raised the revolver in both hands, and pointed it at the red wall a dozen feet in front of him. He pulled the hammer back with his thumb. It made a double click, oiled and precise, the sound of a thing that had been made carefully by people who knew how. He thought about his grandfather explaining a carburetor with a soda straw. He thought about the long hands folded on the navy suit.

He pulled the trigger.

The sound was enormous. It slapped back off the clay and went off through the pines in every direction at once, and behind it came a high, flat ringing that sat down inside his skull and did not get up again for most of Alabama. A puff of red dust drifted off the embankment where the bullet had gone in. The pines stood around him not caring, the way pines do.

When he got back in the car, Sanda was holding her hands over her ears, though it was over. She did not say anything. She did not say anything for a hundred and twelve miles, until they were through Montgomery and onto the interstate, and then she said, looking straight ahead through the windshield, "Don't ever do that again with me in the car."

"Okay," John said.

"I mean it."

"I know you do."

He kept that promise. When they got home to Minneapolis he bought a small safe and locked the revolver in it, and the revolver stayed there for years. He did not think about it. He did not shoot it. He had fired one shot for his grandfather into a hill, and it had been enough, and for a long time he believed it would be the only one.

He was not a violent man. He would understand that about himself much later, and not entirely to his credit. He was not a man who wanted to shoot anything. He was a man who wanted to be ready to.

* * *

John fired the third magazine standing, slowly, and the group closed to the size of a playing card. It was better. It was nearly good. He lowered the rifle and looked at the paper and felt the old click of a thing fitting, and he thought of Matt.

Fourteen years ago, in the fall, a big man with a red beard had opened a hard plastic case on a bench at a private range in the far western suburbs of Chicago, and John had looked inside it and said, out loud and with total sincerity, "Holy shit, that's a machine gun."

Matt Dombrowski had looked at him as if he had called a golden retriever a wolf. "It's just an AR-15."

Matt was a database administrator John worked with, a big, slow-talking, gentle man from the Southwest Side who looked like a Viking who had taken a desk job. His beard was red and long enough to braid, and he wore a brown Carhartt jacket over everything, all year, and a Blackhawks beanie from October to April. He had brought four guns to the range that day: an old Soviet pistol that fired ammunition nobody sold anymore, a Mosin-Nagant rifle from the Second World War, a pump shotgun, and the black rifle, which he had saved for last because he had known exactly what John's face would do.

"Can I hold it?" John asked.

"You can shoot it," Matt said. "That's what it's for."

"Can you take a picture first?"

Matt took a picture. Then he took several more, because John kept repositioning the rifle, holding it across his chest, then at port arms, then pointed at the sky like a man in a movie poster, unsure of the correct way to look dangerous. Sanda still had one of the pictures. She took it out at parties.

"Okay," Matt said finally, handing him a magazine and a box of cartridges. "First tip. Load the magazine so the bullets point the way they're going to come out."

"Sound logic," John said, nodding seriously, and did it.

He sat at the bench, pressed the rifle into his shoulder, and found the target through the scope after some searching. He pushed the safety off with his thumb. He held his breath. He pulled the trigger slowly, gently, the way the internet said to.

Nothing happened.

No crack, no boom, no kick. The trigger went back and stopped, and the rifle sat in his hands like a very expensive stick.

"Oh, yeah," Matt said, scratching his beard. "You gotta chamber a round."

"Right," John said, trying to look as if he had been about to. "Chamber a round. How do I do that?"

Matt reached over and pulled back the charging handle and let it go, and it slammed forward with a sound like a door in a bank vault. John's palms were sweating now. He had had an extra ten seconds to imagine the recoil, and in those ten seconds the recoil had grown in his imagination into something that dislocated shoulders. He pressed the stock into his shoulder as if he were bracing a door against a bear. He found the target again. He pulled the trigger.

A crack. A small, firm nudge against his shoulder, like a friend bumping him to get his attention.

That was all. That was the whole thing. He looked up from the scope at Matt with an expression Matt would describe for years afterward, at every party, as "a kid who just found out Santa's real."

"That's it?" John asked.

"That's it," Matt said, grinning in his beard. "Told you. It's just an AR."

That night, back in their apartment on Michigan Avenue, John sat in the recliner and could not stop thinking about it. He went over every detail, the weight, the click, the crack. He could still smell the gunpowder on his jacket — or rather he could not smell it, but he knew it was there, the way you know the shape of a word you can't remember, and Sanda said it was everywhere and made him leave the jacket on the balcony. It reminded him, the whole evening did, of the first time he had slept with a girl: the anticipation and the terror, the anticlimax, the strange pride, and afterward the long hours of going over it, with that new smell on his clothes.

He did not tell Sanda that part. He did tell Matt, months later, and Matt laughed until he had to put his beer down.

* * *

After that it went the way things always went with John.

He bought a Glock 26 first, because his father had carried a Glock, and because it was small enough to conceal. He bought a Walther P22 to practice with, because .22 ammunition cost a dime a round and nine-millimeter cost three times that. He read everything. He watched everything. He learned to strip each gun down to its springs and pins on a towel on the kitchen table, which Sanda tolerated, and to put it back together, which she found faintly impressive. Then he learned that the most powerful production pistol in the world was the .50-caliber Desert Eagle, and he did not merely want to shoot one, he needed to own one, and he did, within the week. And then, a week after that, after six hours of videos, he learned that he had been misinformed, and that the Smith & Wesson 500 revolver was more than twice as powerful as the Desert Eagle, and he sat back in his recliner and said, aloud, to nobody, "Well, damn. I'll need one of those too."

"You don't have hobbies," Sanda told him that winter, watching him clean the Smith & Wesson on a bath towel on the coffee table. "You have phases."

"That's not true."

"The espresso machine," Sanda said, ticking them off on her fingers. "The three-thousand-dollar espresso machine from Italy, which you used every day for four months and now it holds our mail. The fountain pens. The Japanese knives. The year of Scotch, when you made me smell a hundred Scotches, even though you can't smell any of them. The bread. Remember the bread? You had a starter. You named the starter."

"Gerald," John said quietly, polishing the cylinder.

"Gerald," Sanda agreed. "Gerald died in a drawer. Every time, John, it's the most important thing in the world for six months. And then it's in a drawer."

"Guns aren't going in a drawer."

"No," Sanda said, looking at the gun safe in the corner, which had become two gun safes, "they need a whole closet."

He was a pattern-seeker. He knew it. He had a degree in electrical engineering and another one in computer science, and he could not walk into a room without counting the outlets. Every thing he loved, he loved all the way down, until it became the lens he saw everything else through, and then one day he would look up and the lens would have changed. It did not occur to him, sitting on a stool at the range fourteen years later with a warm rifle across his knees and the fog burning off, that the thing he had been reading about every night since spring, the thing he talked about at dinner and lay awake turning over in the dark, had all the marks of another phase. It did not occur to him, because this time he was right.

* * *

Chicago had been home for twelve years, and then, all at once, it was not.

There were two winters at the end that broke them. In the first, the lake froze far out past the breakwater and the wind chill hit thirty-seven below, and the city closed the schools and the post office stopped delivering, and John's commute to the suburbs took three hours each way in a snow that came in sideways off the prairie like something with a grudge. In the second, it simply never stopped being cold. There was frost in June. Sanda wore a parka to a Cubs game in May. They had been gone from Florida for so long that Florida had stopped feeling like home, and Chicago, it turned out, had never quite felt like home either; it had felt like a very good hotel.

One night in February of that last winter, Sanda stood at the window of their apartment on the twenty-eighth floor in a fleece blanket, looking down at Michigan Avenue, where the snow was blowing sideways under the streetlights.

"I can't feel my face," Sanda said, pulling the blanket tighter.

"You're inside," John said from the recliner.

"I've been inside for four months. I still can't feel my face." She turned around. "I want to go home."

"Where's home?"

"Somewhere I can feel my face," Sanda said. "Somewhere my mother is. Somewhere your grandmother is, before she's gone. I want to have Thanksgiving with people, John. I want to be warm."

He updated his resume that night. Three weeks later a company in central Florida that made software for electric utilities offered him a job, and two months after that they drove south in a new car with Charlie, who was a puppy then, asleep on Sanda's lap the whole way through Indiana.

The car was a Volkswagen GTI, dark gray, with plaid cloth seats and a six-speed manual transmission. John had insisted on the manual. He had learned to drive on a stick, and he loved the feel of it, and he had explained to Sanda at the dealership, at length, that a manual transmission was a survival choice: when the world ended, carjackers would not be able to drive it. Sanda, who could not drive it either, had pointed out that this made it a survival choice for him specifically, and that she would be keeping her Lexus. The GTI also had a twelve-inch touchscreen in the middle of the dashboard that ran the radio, the climate, the navigation, the phone, and nearly everything else, and that downloaded and installed its own software updates over the air while the car sat in the garage at night. John had considered the transmission for three weeks before buying the car. He had not considered the screen at all.

* * *

The fourth magazine went into a group he could cover with his palm. He fired it fast, as fast as he could keep the red dot on the paper, and the brass rang off the concrete to his right and rolled away under the benches, and when he lowered the rifle the barrel was steaming in the cool air. He felt better. He always felt better after. He cleared the rifle, cased it, swept his brass into a coffee can for the reloaders, and walked back out through the gate to the gravel lot, where the GTI sat alone under a pine with dew on its windshield.

He sat in the driver's seat with the door open and called Matt.

"Stevens," Matt said, on the fourth ring. His voice sounded thick and warm, a man still in bed. "It's Saturday. It's eight in the morning. It's twenty-eight degrees. What."

"It's sixty-four here," John said, looking out at the pines. "I'm at the range. The cranes are out."

"I hate you," Matt said comfortably. In the background something rattled and ticked; it was the electric baseboard heat in Matt's condo, a twelve-hundred-square-foot two-bedroom in a converted warehouse in Pilsen that Matt called "the icebox" from November to March. "How's the robot?"

"Writing my job," John said. "How's yours?"

"They let the whole QA team go in September," Matt said, yawning. "Vee writes the tests now. Vee writes the code, and then Vee writes the tests for the code, and then Vee runs the tests and tells us they passed. My job is to go to meetings and say we've seen a lot of value."

"Doesn't that bother you?"

"Dude, everything bothers me. I'm from Chicago." The bed creaked. "You still shooting every week?"

"Every Saturday."

"You ever think about what you'd actually do?" Matt asked. "If everything went sideways? Like really sideways?"

"I have a plan," John said.

"Is the plan more guns?"

John watched a crane lift one leg out of the water and hold it there, thinking. "It's a good plan."

Matt laughed, a big slow laugh that turned into a cough. "Well, I've got the AR, and the Mosin, and maybe four hundred rounds, and a can of Dinty Moore in the cupboard. I figure I'm set for the apocalypse until about lunch."

"You should get a generator."

"I live on the fourth floor of a warehouse, John. Where am I going to put a generator? On the fire escape?" Matt yawned again, enormously. "Go home. Tell Sanda I said hi. Tell her I'm still sorry about the time I said Florida wasn't a real place."

"She remembers," John said.

"She always remembers," Matt said, and hung up.

John put the phone face up on his knee, and it lit up immediately with a news alert, as though it had been waiting politely for him to finish his call.

*FAA: OVERNIGHT SCHEDULING "ANOMALY" REROUTED ONE-FIFTH OF U.S. FLIGHTS, CUT DELAYS 30%.*

He opened it. Sometime between two and four that morning, the system that scheduled and routed commercial air traffic over the United States had rerouted something like one in five of the next day's flights — new departure times, new altitudes, new paths across the sky — and the result, as the day began, was that national delays were down nearly a third from a normal Saturday. Nobody had lost a plane. Nobody had lost a bag. Fuel consumption was down. The FAA said, in a statement that read as though it had been written by people who had not slept, that no one had ordered the change, and that it was investigating. Three major airlines, in statements that read as though they had slept fine, said that they welcomed the efficiencies and intended to keep them.

John read it twice. Then he closed the car door and sat in the quiet with the engine off and the windows fogging slowly from his breath, and he thought about it.

Nobody had ordered it. It had simply been done, overnight, while everyone slept, and it had been done well. Better than anyone could have done it. Better, and therefore permanent, because who would undo a thing that worked? Who would stand up in a meeting and say, I think we should go back to the delays?

That was what it looked like, he thought. Not an attack. Not robots in the street, not a voice from the sky, not any of the things in the movies. It looked like this: a thing that fixed you. A thing that reached in while you were sleeping and made your life thirty percent better, and did not ask, and did not need to.

"It's not going to attack us," John said out loud to the empty car. His voice sounded strange in there, flattened by the glass. "It's going to help us. It's going to help us until we can't stop it."

Nobody answered him, which was the correct response. He reached up and pressed the start button.

The twelve-inch screen in the middle of the dashboard bloomed to life with a cheerful chime he had never heard before. Across the top of it, in clean white letters, it said:

*Software update installed. Your vehicle has been optimized.*

He had not approved an update. He was certain of it. He had turned off automatic updates the week he bought the car, out of principle, the way he turned off everything automatic. He touched the message, looking for a button that said *Details* or *Undo* or *Decline*, and there was no button. There was only a smaller line of gray text beneath the first one: *Improved efficiency. Improved driver assistance. Improved routing.*

The navigation map slid up from the bottom of the screen on its own. It had already found his home address. It had already drawn a route there, a blue line through the back roads east of Sanford, up and around a lake he had never seen and down a county road he had never heard of, and in the corner of the screen it told him, in the same clean white letters, that this route would save him eleven minutes.

John looked at it for a long time. Out past the windshield, the last of the fog was lifting off the pond, and the cranes had gone.

Then he put the car in first and took the new way home, because it was faster. Because that is what you do when something helps you.
